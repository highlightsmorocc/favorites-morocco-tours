    What is a solar eclipse?

    A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon passes directly between the Earth and the Sun, casting its shadow on our planet. It can only happen at new Moon. There are four recognised types:

    • Total solar eclipse – the Moon completely covers the Sun’s bright disk along a narrow path of totality. Daylight fades rapidly, the corona becomes visible, temperature drops and stars and planets can appear.
    • Annular solar eclipse – the Moon is slightly farther from Earth and appears smaller, leaving a bright “ring of fire”.
    • Partial solar eclipse – only a portion of the Sun is covered; visible over a much wider area.
    • Hybrid solar eclipse – rare events that appear total in some places and annular in others.

    The geometry is a remarkable coincidence: the Sun is roughly 400 times larger in diameter than the Moon, yet it is also about 400 times farther away, so the two disks appear almost the same size from Earth. On average a total solar eclipse is visible from any given location only once every 300–400 years. The longest theoretical duration of totality is just over seven minutes. The 6-minute solar eclipse 2027 comes remarkably close to that maximum and will be the longest total solar eclipse visible from land for many decades.

    When is the next solar eclipse? 2026 and 2027 overview

    As of late 2026 the next major solar eclipses of interest are:

    • 17 February 2026 – Annular solar eclipse visible primarily from Antarctica.
    • 12 August 2026 – Total solar eclipse crossing Greenland, Iceland and northern Spain (maximum totality about 2 minutes 18 seconds near Iceland). Partial phases are widely visible across Europe, North Africa and much of North America. In Morocco this appears as a significant partial eclipse — a useful preview of the following year’s total event.
    • 6 February 2027 – Annular solar eclipse visible from parts of South America and the South Atlantic.
    • 2 August 2027 – Total solar eclipse of exceptional length (up to 6 minutes 23 seconds) crossing the eastern Atlantic, southern Spain, Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Yemen and Somalia.

    For travellers focused on Morocco the two dates that matter most are 12 August 2026 (deep partial) and especially 2 August 2027 (total near Tangier and the northern coast).

    Solar eclipse 2026 in more detail

    The 12 August 2026 total solar eclipse path runs across the Arctic, Greenland, Iceland and then northern Spain (including areas around Bilbao, Zaragoza and Valencia). Maximum totality lasts roughly 2 minutes 18 seconds. In Morocco the eclipse is partial but still impressive. Observers across the country will see a large fraction of the Sun covered. It is an excellent opportunity to practise safe viewing techniques and to test equipment before the far more dramatic 2027 event. Travellers already planning a Morocco itinerary in August 2026 can enjoy the partial phases from a coastal riad, a desert camp or a rooftop in Fes or Marrakech.

    The 6-minute solar eclipse 2027: exact details for Morocco

    The 6-minute solar eclipse 2027 takes place on Monday, 2 August 2027. At greatest eclipse the Moon completely covers the Sun for 6 minutes and 23 seconds — the longest totality visible from land until the year 2114. The path of totality begins in the eastern Atlantic, crosses the Strait of Gibraltar and then travels across Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Egypt (near Luxor for the absolute maximum), Saudi Arabia, Yemen and the Horn of Africa.

    In Morocco the first African landfall occurs near Tangier. Totality there lasts approximately 4 minutes 51 seconds. Other locations along the northern coastal strip enjoy roughly 4 to 5 minutes of darkness. Southern Spain (Cádiz, Tarifa, Ceuta) also lies under the umbra. The Sun is high enough in the sky (around 38° at maximum in Tangier) for comfortable viewing.

    Because Morocco offers favourable early-August weather statistics, international airports, good road connections and a rich cultural backdrop of medinas, Rif Mountains, imperial cities and Sahara dunes, it has become one of the most practical and appealing destinations for the 2027 event.     How to safely view a solar eclipse

    Eye safety is non-negotiable. Looking directly at the Sun — even when it is partially eclipsed — can cause permanent retinal damage in seconds. The only time it is safe to look with the naked eye is during the brief period of totality. At all other times you must use certified protection:

    • ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses or handheld solar viewers are the simplest and most widely available option.
    • Solar filters designed specifically for telescopes, binoculars or cameras must be placed in front of the optics. Never use ordinary sunglasses, smoked glass, exposed film or homemade filters.
    • Indirect methods such as a pinhole projector allow groups to watch the progress of the eclipse safely.

    Never look through an unfiltered camera viewfinder, telescope or pair of binoculars at the Sun. During totality you may remove the filters and enjoy the corona with the naked eye. The moment the first bead of sunlight reappears (the “diamond ring” effect), put the filters back on immediately.

    A deeper look at solar eclipse science

    The Moon’s shadow consists of two main parts: the dark central umbra (or antumbra in the case of an annular eclipse) and the lighter surrounding penumbra. Only observers inside the umbra experience totality. The width of the path of totality is typically 100–250 km, and the shadow races across Earth’s surface at high speed.

    Saros cycles — sequences of eclipses repeating every 18 years and 11 days — allow astronomers to predict future events with great accuracy. The 2 August 2027 total solar eclipse belongs to Saros 136, a series known for producing long-duration totalities. The same series produced the famous 2009 total eclipse over China and the Pacific that reached 6 minutes 39 seconds over open ocean.

    Scientifically, total solar eclipses remain valuable. They allow observations of the solar corona that are difficult even with space-based instruments, and historically they provided critical tests of Einstein’s general theory of relativity (most famously in 1919).

    Historical and cultural perspectives on the solar eclipse

    Long before the geometry of eclipses was understood, cultures around the world invented rich mythologies to explain the sudden disappearance of the Sun. In ancient China a celestial dragon was said to devour the Sun. Norse mythology spoke of wolves chasing the Sun and Moon across the sky. In Hindu tradition the demon Rahu’s severed head periodically swallows the Sun.

    These myths reflect a universal human reaction to an event that, without scientific context, feels deeply unsettling. Today we understand the mechanics, yet the emotional impact remains powerful. First-time viewers of totality frequently describe feelings of awe, humility and even mild disorientation.

    In Morocco and across the Maghreb, Islamic astronomical traditions have long tracked lunar and solar cycles for the religious calendar. Contemporary Moroccan sky-watchers combine that heritage with modern scientific interest, making the country an especially resonant place to experience the 2027 event.

    What to expect during totality

    The progression of a total solar eclipse follows a predictable sequence that still manages to feel magical every time:

    1. First contact – The Moon takes the first tiny “bite” out of the Sun. Partial phases begin.
    2. Deepening partial phase – Over roughly an hour the crescent Sun shrinks. Light takes on a metallic quality; shadows sharpen.
    3. Baily’s beads and diamond ring – Just before totality, sunlight streams through lunar valleys, creating bright beads. The final bead forms the dazzling diamond-ring effect.
    4. Totality – The corona springs into view. Temperature drops several degrees. Birds may go quiet. The horizon glows with a 360-degree sunset. Planets and bright stars appear.
    5. Second diamond ring and third contact – The first ray of sunlight reappears; filters must return immediately.
    6. Final partial phases – The Moon slowly uncovers the Sun until fourth contact ends the eclipse.

    Experienced eclipse chasers often say that no photograph or video fully captures the sensation of standing under the Moon’s shadow. The sudden darkness, the drop in temperature and the ethereal corona create a multi-sensory experience that is difficult to describe yet impossible to forget.

    Looking beyond 2027

    After the 6-minute solar eclipse 2027, the next major total solar eclipses include 22 July 2028 (Australia and New Zealand) and several others later in the 2030s. Europe and North Africa will not see another total solar eclipse of comparable duration for many decades. That rarity is precisely why the 2027 event is already generating significant interest among both dedicated eclipse chasers and ordinary travellers who simply want to witness something extraordinary.

    Partial solar eclipses will continue to occur more frequently. Checking reliable sources such as NASA’s eclipse pages or timeanddate.com closer to each date will give precise local circumstances for any given city.

    Final thoughts: why the solar eclipse still matters

    In an age of constant digital stimulation, a total solar eclipse remains one of the few events that can silence a crowd and make people look up together in shared wonder. It is a reminder that we live on a planet moving through a dynamic solar system, and that the geometry of that system occasionally produces moments of pure visual poetry.
