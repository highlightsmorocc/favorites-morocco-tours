    Dar el Bacha Marrakech: Museum, Bacha Coffee, Hours & Tickets

    Dar el Bacha guide: El Glaoui's palace the Museum of Confluences Bacha Coffee Marrakech opening hours ticket price and tips to beat

    Favorites Morocco Tours · Updated: 23 September 2026

    Quick answer: Dar el Bacha is the former palace of Pasha Thami El Glaoui, built in 1910 in the Marrakech medina. Since 2017 it has been the Museum of Confluences and home to the famous Bacha Coffee. It opens Tuesday to Sunday, 10 am to 6 pm, and entry for foreign visitors costs 60 dirhams.

    Summary in 30 seconds

    • Dar el Bacha means “the house of the pasha” in Moroccan Arabic.
    • The palace was built in 1910 for Thami El Glaoui, Pasha of Marrakech from 1912.
    • King Mohammed VI inaugurated the Museum of Confluences on 9 July 2017.
    • It is run by Morocco’s National Museums Foundation.
    • Opening hours: Tuesday to Sunday, 10 am to 6 pm. Closed on Mondays.
    • Tickets: 60 MAD for foreign visitors, 25 MAD for Moroccan citizens and 15 MAD for residents under 18.
    • Inside is Bacha Coffee, a coffee room serving more than 200 Arabica coffees.
    • The wait for the café can exceed an hour, so arrive at opening time.

    What is Dar el Bacha and why is everyone talking about it?

    In recent years Dar el Bacha has become one of the most searched-for sights in Marrakech. Many travellers arrive after seeing the café on social media. However, the place is far more than a pretty coffee shop.

    It is a historic palace with a century of political, social and artistic life. For decades it was the centre of power in southern Morocco. Moreover, statesmen, writers and film stars passed through its salons.

    Today the palace combines three experiences in one visit. First, an art museum with temporary exhibitions. Second, almost intact Moroccan luxury architecture. And third, a coffee room that has become famous worldwide.

    On top of that, the location is very convenient. It sits inside the medina, a few minutes’ walk from the Mouassine Mosque and Le Jardin Secret. So it fits easily into any walk through the old town of Marrakech.

    The history of Dar el Bacha: the palace of the last great pasha

    Thami El Glaoui, “Lord of the Atlas”

    To understand Dar el Bacha, you need to know its owner. Thami El Glaoui belonged to the powerful Glaoua family, an Amazigh (Berber) dynasty from the High Atlas. His clan controlled the Tizi n’Tichka pass, the caravan route between Marrakech and the south.

    In 1912 Sultan Moulay Youssef appointed him Pasha of Marrakech. From then on, for more than forty years, he was the most powerful political figure in southern Morocco under the French protectorate. In addition, he was famous for his wealth, his hospitality and his taste for European society.

    His story, however, ended bitterly. El Glaoui supported the exile of Sultan Mohammed V in 1953. When the sultan returned in 1955, the pasha knelt before him to ask forgiveness. He died in January 1956, a few weeks before Moroccan independence. After his death, the new state confiscated much of his property.

    A palace built to impress

    El Glaoui built Dar el Bacha in 1910 with a clear aim: to dazzle his guests. That is why he spared no expense on materials or craftsmen. He brought master artisans from Fes and Marrakech who specialised in zellige, carved stucco and cedar wood.

    Interestingly, it was one of the first palaces in Marrakech with lavish decoration on the outside too. Traditionally, medina houses hid their luxury behind plain walls. Dar el Bacha, by contrast, showed its wealth from the street.

    The famous guests

    The pasha’s banquets were legendary. According to accounts of the time, guests at Dar el Bacha included Winston Churchill, Franklin D. Roosevelt, Charlie Chaplin, Josephine Baker and the writer Jean Cocteau. Churchill in particular adored Marrakech and painted many of his watercolours in the city.

    In fact, El Glaoui was invited to the coronation of Queen Elizabeth II in London in 1953. His network of international contacts explains why his palace became a diplomatic salon.

    Abandonment and rebirth

    After independence the palace passed to the state and remained closed to the public for decades. Many locals walked past it every day without knowing what lay inside.

    Everything changed with the restoration led by the National Museums Foundation. On 9 July 2017 King Mohammed VI officially inaugurated the Museum of Confluences Dar el Bacha. Shortly afterwards, the palace’s old coffee room reopened as Bacha Coffee.

    Finally, the Al Haouz earthquake of September 2023 damaged the building. The museum closed temporarily for repairs and later reopened. Today it operates normally.

    Life in the palace in El Glaoui’s time

    Picture the courtyard of Dar el Bacha in the 1930s. At night, musicians played Andalusian music and servants brought out the “diffa”, the great Moroccan welcome feast. Dishes kept coming for hours: pigeon pastilla, whole roast lamb, tagines, couscous and fruit. Finally came mint tea and almond pastries.

    The pasha was also a keen golfer. Indeed, he promoted the creation of the Royal Golf de Marrakech in the 1930s, one of the oldest courses in the country. Many of his European guests played there.

    In addition, the palace worked as an administrative centre. Tribal chiefs, merchants and French officials came through its doors. So Dar el Bacha was at once a home, a court and a government office. This mix of private life and public power explains the size and luxury of its salons.

    If you are curious about the food served at those feasts, our guide to traditional Moroccan dishes explains how they are cooked today.

    Churchill, Roosevelt and Marrakech

    Winston Churchill first came to Marrakech in the 1930s and returned many times. He stayed at La Mamounia hotel and spent his days painting the medina, the Atlas and the palm groves. He reportedly called Marrakech “the Paris of the Sahara”.

    In January 1943, after the Casablanca Conference, Churchill brought President Roosevelt to Marrakech to watch the sunset over the Atlas. It was during that trip that he painted the “Tower of the Koutoubia Mosque”, his only painting of the war years. He gave it to Roosevelt as a gift. In 2021 the painting sold at Christie’s in London for about 8.3 million pounds, a record for a Churchill work.

    These visits explain why the pasha’s palace was part of the social circuit of the time. So when you walk through the courtyard of Dar el Bacha, you are stepping into the same world that Churchill described in his letters.

    The crafts you will see at Dar el Bacha

    The palace is a showcase of traditional crafts still practised in Marrakech. Recognising them will also help you shop better in the souks.

    The zellige maalem

    The “maalem” is the master craftsman. For zellige, he draws the pattern, cuts every piece and lays them face down before fixing them with mortar. A single large panel can take weeks.

    Woodcarvers

    Carpenters in the Mouassine district still work cedar with techniques very close to those of 1910. You can watch them in the nearby fondouks.

    Pierced metalwork

    Brass and copper lamps with perforated patterns give the salons their typical light. On the street around Dar el Bacha you will find many of these pieces, from small lamps to large chandeliers.

    Textiles and rugs

    Amazigh rugs from the Atlas and cactus silk fabrics often appear in the exhibitions. They are also among the most popular souvenirs. Learn to recognise them in our guide to traditional Moroccan clothing.

    The Museum of Confluences: what to see inside

    The name “Confluences” is no accident. The museum sets out to show how Morocco has always been a crossroads of cultures: Amazigh, Arab, Andalusian, Jewish, African and Mediterranean.

    Temporary exhibitions

    Unlike other medina museums, Dar el Bacha works mainly with temporary exhibitions. So each visit can be different. Shows usually cover decorative arts, ceramics, textiles, jewellery or manuscripts. They also often compare Moroccan pieces with objects from other world cultures.

    Before you go, check which exhibition is on. That way you can decide how much time to spend in the museum.

    The central courtyard and garden

    The heart of the palace is a large rectangular courtyard with a garden divided into four symmetrical parts. This layout, called a riad, represents paradise in Islamic tradition. It has fountains, orange trees and shaded seating areas.

    In spring the scent of orange blossom fills the courtyard. Therefore March and April are especially lovely months to visit.

    The salons and the hammam

    Around the courtyard open salons with carved and painted cedar ceilings. Many doors keep their original colours. In addition, the palace has an old private hammam, which shows how the elite lived at the time. If this tradition interests you, read our guide to the Moroccan hammam.

    Dar el Bacha architecture, detail by detail

    Even if history is not your thing, the architecture of Dar el Bacha justifies the visit on its own. It is a living catalogue of early 20th-century Moroccan craftsmanship.

    Zellige

    Zellige is a mosaic of small hand-cut glazed ceramic pieces. At Dar el Bacha it covers floors, dados and fountains with highly complex geometric stars. Each piece is chipped out one by one with a sharp hammer.

    Carved stucco (gebs)

    Above the zellige run panels of carved plaster with floral, geometric and calligraphic motifs. Craftsmen work the fresh plaster with chisels, almost like lace.

    Cedar wood

    Ceilings and doors are made from Atlas cedar. The wood is carved and then painted with natural pigments. Moreover, cedar gives off a soft scent you can still notice in some rooms.

    European influences

    On the other hand, the palace shows European touches such as stained glass, some furniture and wrought-iron details. This blend reflects the protectorate era and El Glaoui’s cosmopolitan taste. In short, the house is itself a “confluence”, just like the museum.

    Bacha Coffee Marrakech: the city’s most famous coffee room

    For many visitors, Bacha Coffee is the main reason to go to Dar el Bacha. And no wonder. The room recreates the atmosphere of the palace’s old coffee salons, with high ceilings, round tables and waiters dressed in white.

    What makes it special

    The menu lists more than 200 100% Arabica coffees from over 35 countries. There are single origins, flavoured blends and house specials. Each coffee arrives in a silver-coloured pot on a tray, with whipped cream, sugar sticks and a small sweet.

    The menu also includes French pastries, savoury toasts and breakfasts. That is why many people treat it as a late breakfast or afternoon tea stop.

    A brand that went global

    The success of the Marrakech room gave rise to a luxury brand. In 2019 Bacha Coffee opened its first boutique outside Morocco, in Singapore. It later reached other cities in Asia, the Middle East and Europe. However, the Marrakech palace remains its spiritual home and the most authentic place to try it.

    The boutique

    Next to the room is a shop selling whole-bean or ground coffee, collector tins and accessories. It is a popular gift to take home. Prices, though, are premium-brand prices, well above an ordinary medina café.

    How much does a coffee cost at Bacha Coffee?

    Prices change often. As a guide, a coffee from the menu costs considerably more than in a traditional medina café, and a full breakfast approaches the price of a mid-range restaurant. Even so, you are paying for the service, the setting and the place. Check prices on the day of your visit.

    What to order at Bacha Coffee

    With more than 200 coffees, the menu can feel overwhelming. These simple tips help you choose:

    • Ask the waiter: staff can suggest a coffee based on your taste, from mild to intense.
    • Try a house blend: the flavoured Moroccan-inspired blends are what most visitors come for.
    • Pick a single origin: if you prefer pure coffee, choose a bean from a country you have never tried.
    • Add a pastry: croissants and French pastries pair well with the richer blends.
    • Share with friends: order different coffees and taste each other’s to explore the menu.

    Finally, remember that the whipped cream and sugar come on the side. You can enjoy the coffee black first and then add them to compare.

    Coffee and tea in Morocco: why Bacha Coffee is unusual

    Morocco is, above all, a tea country. Sweet green tea with mint, poured from a height, is the great symbol of hospitality. It is offered in homes, shops and offices at any time of day.

    Coffee has its own story, though. It reached Morocco along Ottoman and Mediterranean trade routes. During the French protectorate, European-style cafés also spread through the new towns, such as Gueliz in Marrakech or central Casablanca.

    In ordinary neighbourhoods, Moroccan coffee is often drunk “cassé”, cut with milk, or flavoured with spices. Some families add cinnamon, cardamom, ginger or nutmeg to the ground coffee. This spiced coffee, known as “café épicé”, is very popular in winter.

    So Bacha Coffee does not represent everyday habits but the life of the early 20th-century elite. Its silver service, salons and menu of world coffees recreate the luxury of the old palaces. In short, it is a historical experience rather than a daily one.

    Where to try traditional mint tea

    After Bacha Coffee, compare it with a tea on a medina rooftop or in a Berber family home on a trip to the Atlas. The difference in atmosphere and price is huge, and both experiences belong to the journey. Discover more flavours in our guide to cuisine in Morocco.

    Do you need a ticket for Bacha Coffee?

    In practice, you reach the café through the palace. So you normally need a museum ticket to get to the coffee room. Coffee is paid separately. Check at the ticket desk, as arrangements can change.

    Can you book a table?

    The coffee room does not usually take reservations. It works on a first-come basis, with a waiting list at the entrance. Therefore the best strategy is to arrive at opening time or late in the afternoon.

    How to beat the queues at Dar el Bacha

    The wait for a table at Bacha Coffee often exceeds an hour, especially mid-morning and at weekends. These tips will save you time:

    • Arrive at 10 am: it is the quietest moment of the day.
    • Avoid Saturday and Sunday: many Moroccan visitors come at weekends too.
    • See the museum first: put your name on the café list and explore the rooms while you wait.
    • Travel in low season: July, August and January tend to bring fewer foreign tourists.
    • If you only want the shop: the boutique has a much shorter wait than the coffee room.

    A new detail for 2026: Morocco returned to permanent GMT on 20 September. As a result, it gets dark earlier on the clock in autumn and winter. For good light in the courtyard, go in the morning.

    Dar el Bacha street: what is there and what to buy

    Many travellers search for “Dar el Bacha street” on the map and get confused. In fact, the name is used for the route around the palace, linking the Bab Doukkala area with the Mouassine district.

    The street is known for antique shops, rugs, pierced metal lamps, ceramics and furniture. Unlike the central souks, the atmosphere is calmer and the quality is usually higher. That is why interior designers love the area.

    Shopping tips

    • Compare prices in several shops before deciding.
    • Bargaining is normal, but keep it friendly.
    • Ask shops to ship large pieces: many handle international delivery.
    • Carry cash in dirhams for small items.

    What to see near Dar el Bacha

    One advantage of Dar el Bacha is that interesting places surround it. In a single morning you can make a short walking loop.

    Mouassine Mosque and fountain

    A few minutes’ walk away stands the 16th-century Mouassine Mosque with its decorated public fountain. Non-Muslims cannot enter the mosque, but the square and the fountain are worth a stop.

    Le Jardin Secret

    This restored garden brings back an old riad with an Islamic garden and an exotic one. Its tower also offers a good view over the medina rooftops.

    The Mouassine fondouks

    Fondouks were old caravan inns. Today many house craft workshops. It is an ideal place to watch wood, leather or metal being worked.

    Jemaa el-Fna and the souks

    From Dar el Bacha you reach Jemaa el-Fna square in about 15 minutes on foot through the souks. In the evening the square fills with food stalls and musicians. Find more in our complete Marrakech travel guide.

    Other National Museums Foundation museums

    The National Museums Foundation runs a network of museums across the country. If you like Dar el Bacha, these follow the same approach:

    • Mohammed VI Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art (Rabat): the country’s leading art museum.
    • Dar Batha Museum (Fes): a palace with an Andalusian garden and collections of Fes blue pottery. More ideas in our guide on what to do in Fez.
    • Kasbah Museum (Tangier): in the old sultan’s palace, overlooking the Strait of Gibraltar.

    All of them fit into a private tour of the imperial cities. See our tours from Fes and tours from Tangier.

    From the Marrakech palace to the Telouet kasbah: the El Glaoui trail

    The story of Dar el Bacha continues in the mountains. The Glaoui family’s base was the Telouet kasbah in the High Atlas, on the old caravan route. Today it is partly in ruins, yet its decorated salons still amaze visitors.

    The Glaouis also controlled the Taourirt kasbah in Ouarzazate and held sway over the ksar of Ait Ben Haddou. So if you enjoy the pasha’s story, follow his trail along the Tizi n’Tichka road.

    Tips for visiting Dar el Bacha

    What to wear

    There is no strict dress code. Even so, clothes that cover shoulders and knees are recommended in the medina. See our guide on what to wear in Morocco.

    Photos

    Photos without flash are usually allowed in the courtyard and many rooms. Some temporary exhibitions may ban them. Always follow the signs.

    Accessibility

    The courtyard and ground floor are largely accessible. However, the medina lanes leading to the palace are narrow and uneven. If you have limited mobility, take a taxi to the nearest drop-off point.

    Getting there

    From Jemaa el-Fna it is about 15 minutes on foot. From the Bab Doukkala area, about 5 minutes. Taxis can drop you near Bab Doukkala. Use offline maps or buy a SIM card in Morocco.

    Guided or independent?

    You can visit the palace on your own. However, a local guide will tell you El Glaoui’s story and lead you through the medina without getting lost. Our private trips include an official guide in Marrakech.

    Dar el Bacha through the seasons

    Spring (March to May)

    This is the best time. The courtyard orange trees blossom, temperatures are mild and the light is perfect for photos. On the other hand, it is also high season, with longer waits at the café.

    Summer (June to August)

    Marrakech often passes 40 °C. So go first thing in the morning. Thanks to its thick walls, the palace interior stays fairly cool.

    Autumn (September to November)

    The heat eases and the city recovers its rhythm. Since 20 September 2026 it gets dark earlier on the clock, so plan visits for the morning.

    Winter (December to February)

    Days are sunny and cool. It is an ideal time for a hot coffee in the palace salon. However, Christmas and New Year bring crowds of European visitors. See our guide to the best time to visit Morocco.

    Visiting Dar el Bacha with children

    Children usually enjoy the courtyard, fountains and colourful zellige. Even so, the museum has no permanent children’s activities. These tips help:

    • Turn the visit into a game: hunt for eight-pointed stars in the mosaics.
    • Go early to avoid the long café wait.
    • The menu includes hot chocolate and pastries as well as coffee.
    • Combine the visit with Le Jardin Secret, which is easier for little ones.

    Dar el Bacha for UK and North American travellers

    For English-speaking visitors the palace has a special appeal. Churchill’s love of Marrakech, the 1953 coronation invitation and Roosevelt’s visit tie the building to British and American history. So it is a natural stop for anyone interested in 20th-century politics.

    From London, Marrakech is about three and a half hours by direct flight. From the USA and Canada, Royal Air Maroc flies direct to Casablanca, with a short connection or train to Marrakech. British, US, Canadian and Australian passport holders do not need a visa for tourist stays of up to 90 days. See our guide to flights to Marrakech.

    Common mistakes when visiting Dar el Bacha

    • Going on a Monday: the museum is closed.
    • Arriving at midday: it is the peak waiting time for the café.
    • Thinking it is just a café: the museum and palace deserve at least 45 minutes.
    • Confusing it with Dar Si Said or the Bahia Palace: they are different places.
    • Following unofficial street “guides”: they will steer you to shops that pay commission.
    • Carrying no cash: some shops on the street only take dirhams.

    Frequently asked questions about Dar el Bacha

    What is Dar el Bacha?

    It is the former palace of Pasha Thami El Glaoui in the Marrakech medina, built in 1910. Today it is the Museum of Confluences and houses the Bacha Coffee room.

    How much is the Dar el Bacha entrance fee?

    Entry costs 60 dirhams for foreign visitors, 25 for Moroccan citizens and 15 for residents under 18, according to the National Museums Foundation.

    What are the Dar el Bacha opening hours?

    It opens Tuesday to Sunday, from 10 am to 6 pm. It is closed on Mondays.

    Do you have to pay to enter Bacha Coffee?

    Usually yes, because the café is inside the palace. You pay the museum entry and then the coffee separately.

    How long is the wait at Bacha Coffee Marrakech?

    Mid-morning and at weekends the wait often exceeds an hour. At 10 am it is usually much shorter.

    Who was El Glaoui?

    Thami El Glaoui was Pasha of Marrakech from 1912 until his death in 1956. He was the most powerful man in southern Morocco during the French protectorate.

    Which famous people visited Dar el Bacha?

    Guests included Winston Churchill, Franklin D. Roosevelt, Charlie Chaplin, Josephine Baker and Jean Cocteau.

    Where is Dar el Bacha street?

    It is the route around the palace, between Bab Doukkala and the Mouassine district in the Marrakech medina. It is known for its antique shops.

    How long do you need to visit Dar el Bacha?

    About 45 minutes for the museum and around one hour for the café, not counting the wait.

    Is Dar el Bacha worth visiting?

    Yes, especially if you enjoy Moroccan architecture and want an elegant break in the medina. It is one of the best-preserved palaces in Marrakech.

    Conclusion: Dar el Bacha, history, art and coffee in one place

    Dar el Bacha sums up 20th-century Marrakech beautifully: power, luxury, craftsmanship and meetings between cultures. Indeed, in a single morning you can explore a historic palace, see an art exhibition and drink one of the world’s most famous coffees. Moreover, its medina location lets you combine it with Mouassine, the souks and Jemaa el-Fna.
