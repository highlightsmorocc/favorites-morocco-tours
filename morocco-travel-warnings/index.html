    Canada and US Issued Morocco Travel Warnings Over Security Risks: What They Really Mean (2026)

    Canada and US Issued Morocco Travel Warnings Over Security Risks: What They Actually Mean for Your Trip

    Updated for 2026  |  Written by the team at Favorites Morocco Tours, a licensed local operator based in Morocco

    If you have been planning a trip and then saw a headline saying that Canada and US issued Morocco travel warnings over security risks, your first reaction was probably a knot in the stomach. Should you cancel? Move the trip? Send a nervous message to your travel partner asking whether this is still a good idea?

    Take a breath. The headlines are real, but they are also badly misunderstood. Government travel advisories are legal risk documents, not verdicts on whether a country is worth visiting. They are written to protect a government from liability, not to help you decide where to spend a week in October. Read out of context, a routine advisory update can sound like a war zone. Read properly, it is often a list of things any sensible traveller already does.

    This guide breaks down exactly what the American and Canadian advisories say, where the warnings genuinely apply, where they do not, and what all of it means for the kind of trip most visitors actually take — Marrakech, Fes, the Atlas Mountains, the Sahara, the coast. We live and work here. We drive these roads every week. What follows is the honest version, without the panic and without the sales pitch.

    What actually happened, and why the headlines appeared

    In spring 2026, the Government of Canada updated its travel advice for Morocco, and a wave of news coverage followed. Several outlets ran the story with dramatic framing about escalating danger in a popular getaway. The substance was more modest: Canada urged travellers to exercise a high degree of caution, citing petty theft, armed robbery, credit and debit card fraud, and the threat of terrorism, alongside a specific warning to avoid the area near the Algerian border because of military presence and an unstable security situation there.

    At roughly the same time, coverage noted that Canada’s guidance was broadly in line with the United States, which also lists Morocco at Level 2, the tier that asks visitors to exercise increased caution.

    Two things are worth noticing immediately. First, neither country told its citizens not to go. Second, the US rating did not change at all — the American advisory has sat at the same level for a long time. What produced the headlines was a routine refresh of Canada’s page, amplified by travel news sites that earn clicks from alarming titles.

    The short version

    Morocco is not on any “do not travel” list. Both the US and Canada place it in the second of four tiers — the same broad category that covers many popular destinations in Europe. The warnings focus on petty crime, terrorism as a general regional threat, and two remote border zones that are nowhere near any normal tourist route.

    What the US advisory actually says

    The State Department’s assessment is public, dated, and specific, which makes it easy to check rather than guess at. Morocco is rated Level 2 — Exercise increased caution, due to terrorism, with the advisory issued on 21 April 2025. The country guidance attached to that page was refreshed on 13 August 2026, so the information is current rather than a leftover from a previous decade.

    The summary itself is brief. It states that terrorist groups continue plotting possible attacks, that attacks may come with little or no warning, and that potential targets include tourist locations, transportation hubs, markets, shopping malls and local government facilities. That paragraph is close to boilerplate: near-identical wording appears in advisories for a long list of countries across Europe, Asia and Africa.

    The advice that follows is the practical part, and it is genuinely worth taking: stay alert in tourist spots, avoid demonstrations and crowds, enrol in the Smart Traveler Enrollment Program so the embassy can reach you, and buy travel insurance that covers medical evacuation and trip cancellation.

    The same page also notes something that rarely makes the headlines. It records that Morocco has strong security services which continue to arrest people allegedly linked to groups seeking to attack foreign or Moroccan government targets. That is not a small detail. Counter-terrorism capacity is exactly what separates a country where a threat exists on paper from one where it materialises regularly.

    How the four-level system works, and why Level 2 is not a red flag

    Most of the confusion around this story comes from people not knowing what the numbers mean. The American system has four tiers, and the gap between them is enormous.

    LevelWordingWhat it signals
    1Exercise normal precautionsThe baseline. Applies to a minority of countries.
    2Exercise increased cautionBe more alert than at home. The most common rating worldwide.
    3Reconsider travelSerious risks. Think hard before booking.
    4Do not travelActive conflict, collapse of services, or extreme danger.

    Morocco sits at Level 2. So do many of the countries that Americans and Canadians visit in enormous numbers without a second thought, including several in Western Europe. Level 2 is the middle of the road. It is a nudge to pay attention, not a signal to stay home.

    Canada uses different language for the same idea. Its “exercise a high degree of caution” sits between “take normal security precautions” and “avoid non-essential travel”. Again, the middle rung.

    The one genuinely useful habit is to read past the headline number and look at the regional breakdown, because that is where the real information lives. Which brings us to the part of both advisories that actually matters.

    Where the warnings genuinely apply

    Both governments single out specific zones, and both are frank about why. These are not vague cautions — they are precise geographic exclusions.

    The Algerian border

    The land border between Morocco and Algeria has been closed since 1994, and diplomatic relations between the two countries are poor. Canada’s advisory tells travellers to stay away from this area, pointing to the increased military presence, the unstable security situation, and the practical difficulty of even knowing exactly where the border runs. That last point is not trivial. In remote desert and mountain terrain the line is not marked, and wandering across it is a serious legal problem, not a photo opportunity.

    The far south and the Mauritanian frontier

    Reporting on the Canadian update also noted a warning about the threat of kidnapping against Westerners in Morocco, particularly in remote regions and the areas bordering Algeria and Mauritania. Coverage of Canada’s wider advisory round also referenced advice against travel within 30 kilometres of a boundary in the far south because of landmine risk.

    This is real, and it deserves to be taken seriously. It is also hundreds of kilometres from anywhere a normal itinerary goes. The southern desert routes used by tour operators run through Merzouga, Zagora, the Draa Valley and the Dades and Todra gorges — all far to the north of these zones and well inside the standard tourist corridor.

    Remote mountain hiking alone

    Canada’s guidance advises maintaining a high level of vigilance in southern and border areas, and specifically warns against hiking alone in remote mountain regions. This is the single piece of advice we would underline hardest, and not only for security reasons. The High Atlas is genuinely wild country. Weather changes fast, phone coverage disappears, trails are unmarked and rescue is slow. Solo unguided trekking in the Atlas is a bad idea for reasons that have nothing to do with crime.

    Put the geography in perspective

    Morocco is slightly larger than California. The zones both advisories flag are remote border regions in the far east and far south. The classic route — Marrakech, Fes, Chefchaouen, Casablanca, Rabat, Essaouira, the Dades and Todra gorges, the dunes at Merzouga — does not enter any of them. Not part of it. None of it.

    What this means for a normal trip

    Here is the practical translation. If your plan looks like a week or ten days built around the imperial cities, a couple of nights in the desert and some time on the coast, the border warnings do not touch your itinerary at any point. The parts of the advisories that are relevant to you are the ordinary ones: pickpocketing in crowded medinas, scams aimed at visitors, traffic, and the general advice to avoid demonstrations.

    Tourism here is not limping along under a cloud. The country continues to draw millions of international visitors a year, and it is preparing to co-host the 2030 World Cup, with infrastructure investment on a scale that reflects long-term confidence rather than crisis. Flights are running normally. Hotels are open. Guides are working. The airport at Marrakech in October is not a place that feels like a country under warning.

    The State Department’s own country guidance is also more reassuring on tourism than the headline suggests. It notes that the tourism industry is generally regulated in major destinations such as Marrakech, Tangier, Fez, Casablanca, Rabat, Essaouira and Agadir, with safety rules regularly enforced and trained staff available for organised activities. It contrasts this with rural and remote areas, where safety inspections are rare, hazards are not always signposted, staff may not be certified and medical help takes longer to arrive.

    Read that carefully, because it contains the single most useful insight in the whole document: risk in Morocco is not evenly distributed, and it drops sharply when you are in regulated tourist areas or with a professional operator, and rises when you improvise in remote places without local support.

    The risks that actually affect visitors

    Terrorism dominates the headlines because it is the stated reason for the Level 2 rating. Statistically, it is not what will affect your trip. These are.

    Pickpocketing and bag snatching

    This is the number one issue, and the advisories are direct about it. The State Department notes that pickpocketing occurs frequently in crowded tourist areas, markets and on public transport, and that bag snatching and theft from vehicles also occur. The Marrakech souks on a busy evening are exactly the environment thieves prefer: dense, distracting, full of people looking at everything except their own bag.

    Practical defence is boring and effective. Carry a crossbody bag with the strap across your chest and the bag in front of you. Keep your phone in a zipped inside pocket, never a back pocket. Leave your passport in the hotel safe and carry a photocopy or a photo on your phone. Do not put a bag on the back of a chair in a café. Do not leave anything visible in a parked car.

    Scams and overfriendly strangers

    The classic version: someone tells you the square is closed today, or the tannery has a special ceremony, or your riad is in the other direction, and offers to guide you. At the end there is a demand for money, or you find yourself in a shop where leaving without buying becomes uncomfortable. It is rarely dangerous. It is frequently annoying and occasionally expensive.

    The advisory also flags financial and online romance scams as common, along with people impersonating Americans in trouble abroad who need money. If someone you met online asks for a transfer, the answer is no.

    The fix for street scams is simple: use official guides, book activities through your riad or a licensed operator, and be politely unmovable. A firm “no thank you” while continuing to walk resolves almost everything.

    Traffic

    This is the risk visitors most consistently underestimate. The State Department’s language is unusually direct, noting that driving practices are very poor and result in serious injuries and fatalities, with accidents especially common at dusk during Ramadan and between July and September. Secondary roads in rural areas are narrow, and mountain roads through the Rif and Atlas are steep, winding and unlit.

    If you are considering a self-drive holiday, weigh this honestly. Mountain driving here demands real concentration, and doing it after a long flight in an unfamiliar car is how holidays go wrong. It is the main reason a large share of our clients choose a driver instead — not for luxury, but because someone who drives the Tizi n’Tichka pass weekly handles it differently from someone seeing it for the first time.

    Weather and flash flooding

    Between November and March, flash flooding is frequent and can be severe enough to wash away roads in rural areas. Mountain passes take snow in winter. This affects desert and Atlas itineraries far more often than any security issue does, and a good operator will reroute around it. Morocco also sits in a seismically active zone, which is worth knowing when you choose travel insurance.

    Harassment

    Verbal harassment and unwanted attention, particularly towards women, is a genuine and common complaint. It is usually persistent rather than threatening, but it is tiring. More on how to handle it below.

    Demonstrations and regional tension

    Both advisories mention demonstrations, and this is the risk most likely to intersect with an ordinary itinerary, simply because protests happen in city centres where visitors walk. The State Department notes that demonstrations are common, can be unpredictable, and that during periods of heightened regional tension large gatherings may take place in major cities. It also points out that all demonstrations legally require a permit, but spontaneous unauthorised ones occur and can turn violent.

    In practice this is easy to manage. Moroccan protests are usually announced, concentrated in specific squares and avenues, and heavily policed. They are not roaming or unpredictable in the way the wording implies. Check local news in the morning, ask your riad or your guide whether anything is planned, and if you come across a crowd, walk away from it rather than through it or towards it with a camera. Photographing protests is a bad idea for reasons that go beyond safety.

    Regional events in the wider Middle East and North Africa occasionally produce larger gatherings in Rabat and Casablanca. These are political expressions rather than anti-tourist sentiment, and visitors are not targets. The correct response is distance, not anxiety.

    One more thing the advisories flag: football matches

    An unusual but specific piece of American guidance advises avoiding stadiums and their surrounding areas on match days, because supporter groups can become unruly and harass bystanders on the way. Crowds often march or drive in convoy towards the ground. With the country co-hosting the 2030 World Cup and hosting continental fixtures regularly, this is worth knowing if your trip coincides with a big game. Enjoy the atmosphere from a café, not from the middle of a supporters’ march.

    City by city: where to be alert

    Marrakech. Heavy tourist policing, especially around Jemaa el-Fna and the main medina arteries. The crowd density is the issue, not violence. Keep bags secured in the souks and around the square after dark, and agree taxi fares or insist on the meter before setting off. Our tours from Marrakech start and end at your accommodation, which removes the most common friction point.

    Fes. The medina is a genuine labyrinth with thousands of lanes, and getting lost is close to inevitable. That is charming at two in the afternoon and less charming at eleven at night. Use a guide for your first full day so you learn the main axes. Our tours from Fes are built around that logic.

    Casablanca. A working city rather than a tourist stage. It behaves like any large port city: fine in the central and business districts, less so in unfamiliar outer neighbourhoods late at night. Standard big-city habits apply. See our tours from Casablanca.

    Tangier. Transformed over the past decade, with a rebuilt marina and a much calmer medina than its old reputation suggests. Still a port with a hustle culture around the ferry terminal. Our tours from Tangier cover the north and the Rif.

    Chefchaouen, Essaouira, the coastal towns. Small, relaxed and among the easiest places in the country to walk around. The main risk is sunburn.

    The Sahara and the southern valleys. Merzouga, Zagora, the Draa, Dades and Todra are well-established tourist routes with organised camps and experienced crews. The advisories’ remote-area warnings concern the far border zones, not these. What matters here is the operator: a licensed camp with proper vehicles, satellite communication and drivers who know the pistes is a completely different proposition from an improvised arrangement made in a car park. If you are planning a day trip from Marrakech or a longer desert route, this is the question to ask.

    Specific travellers, specific advice

    Women travelling alone or together

    The State Department advises that women exercise caution in public spaces, notes that women walking alone in some urban and rural areas are more vulnerable, and suggests travelling with a companion or in a group where possible. Realistically, most solo women who visit have a good trip, but almost all report catcalling and persistent attention.

    What helps: dressing on the modest side of what you would wear at home, particularly in the medinas and smaller towns; walking with obvious purpose even when lost; declining conversation without engaging; using registered taxis at night rather than walking; and choosing accommodation with a staffed reception. Ignoring harassment rather than responding to it is the approach most experienced travellers settle on.

    LGBTQ+ travellers

    Honesty matters more than comfort here. Same-sex sexual relations are criminalised under Moroccan law, and the State Department states plainly that penalties include fines and up to three years’ imprisonment. In practice, prosecutions of foreign visitors are rare, and gay and lesbian travellers visit every year without incident, but the law exists and public displays of affection can attract attention. Many couples book a twin room. We cover this subject in more depth in our dedicated guide on the blog.

    Families

    Morocco is a strongly family-oriented society and children are welcomed everywhere, often enthusiastically. The practical concerns are heat, long transfers and food and water hygiene rather than security. Tap water is not safe to drink in many areas, so stick to sealed bottled water, skip ice where you are unsure, and avoid raw salads outside established restaurants.

    Travellers of sub-Saharan African descent

    The State Department’s guidance notes that some travellers report harassment or discrimination based on perceived race, ethnicity, religion or sexual orientation, and specifically that travellers who appear to be of sub-Saharan African descent have reported discrimination. It is worth knowing in advance rather than being surprised by it.

    Local laws that catch visitors out

    Several of these have nothing to do with security and everything to do with avoiding an entirely preventable problem at the airport or a police checkpoint.

    • Drones are prohibited. Do not bring one without prior government authorisation. Customs will confiscate it, and you will not get it back. This is the single most common expensive mistake visitors make.
    • Satellite phones are strictly regulated and require authorisation to import or use.
    • Do not photograph palaces, government buildings, diplomatic missions or anything of military or security interest. When in doubt, ask.
    • The dirham is a closed currency. You exchange on arrival and change leftover dirhams back before departure — keep your exchange receipts, as you will need them. Exporting dirhams is prohibited.
    • Do not mail passports or medication into or through Morocco. Couriered passports and medicines are confiscated by the authorities.
    • Proselytising is illegal. The constitution protects private religious practice, but attempting to convert Moroccans has resulted in arrest, detention or expulsion.
    • Carry prescription medication in original packaging with the prescription. Check anything unusual against Moroccan rules before you fly.
    • Unpaid bills are treated as fraud. Non-payment of a hotel bill or a bounced cheque is a criminal matter here, and bail is generally unavailable to non-residents.

    Numbers and contacts to save before you fly

    Emergency numbers in Morocco

    • Police: 190 from a mobile, 19 from a landline
    • Ambulance and fire: 150 from a mobile, 15 from a landline
    • Gendarmerie Royale (rural areas and highways): 177

    Note that ambulance services are not widely available and response times vary. In many situations a taxi to the nearest major hospital is faster than waiting.

    US citizens should note that the embassy in Rabat does not provide consular services to the public — all consular assistance goes through the Consulate General in Casablanca. Canadian travellers should register with the Registration of Canadians Abroad service, the equivalent of the American STEP programme.

    On insurance: most domestic health plans do not cover you abroad, medical care here is not free, and hospitals frequently require payment upfront before treatment or admission. Medical evacuation cover is the piece people skip and then regret. If your itinerary includes the desert or the mountains, evacuation cover is not optional in any meaningful sense.

    How to reduce your risk to close to nothing

    Strip away the noise and there is a short list that handles nearly everything in both advisories.

    1. Book through licensed operators. Regulated tourism is where inspections happen, staff are trained and someone is accountable if something goes wrong. This is the State Department’s own distinction, not ours.
    2. Do not drive the mountain routes yourself unless you are confident. A local driver is the cheapest safety upgrade available.
    3. Stay out of demonstrations. They are common, usually peaceful, and occasionally not. Walk the other way and check local news.
    4. Secure your valuables the way you would in Barcelona or Naples. Crossbody bag, nothing in back pockets, passport in the safe.
    5. Skip the flagged border zones. No legitimate itinerary goes there anyway.
    6. Buy proper insurance with medical evacuation and cancellation cover.
    7. Register with your embassy so you receive alerts and can be located in an emergency.
    8. Drink sealed bottled water, and be careful with raw produce outside established restaurants.
    9. Keep a photocopy of your passport separate from the original.
    10. Trust your instincts. If a situation feels off, leave. You owe no one politeness at the cost of your comfort.

    How to read a travel advisory without scaring yourself

    Once you understand how these documents are written, they stop being frightening and start being useful. Four habits make the difference.

    Check the date, and check whether the level changed. Most advisory news stories are triggered by a page update, not by an event. If the level is the same as it was last year, nothing has escalated. The American rating for Morocco was issued in April 2025 and has carried the same level and the same risk indicator since. A refreshed page is not a new emergency.

    Read the regional detail, not the headline number. The country-level rating is an average across territory that may be the size of a large American state. The useful information is always in the specific geography: which provinces, which borders, which distances. A country can carry a cautious overall rating because of one remote district while the areas you plan to visit are unremarkable.

    Notice what the advisory does not say. Ratings that describe genuine danger use unmistakable language: reconsider travel, avoid non-essential travel, leave immediately, we cannot provide consular assistance. When none of that appears, and the document instead offers advice about pickpockets, taxi meters and travel insurance, you are reading a caution rather than an alarm.

    Compare it to somewhere you would go without thinking. This is the fastest sanity check available. Look up the advisory for a country you have already visited comfortably. If the wording is broadly similar, your instinct about the destination in front of you is probably being distorted by a headline rather than by the underlying assessment.

    Finally, remember who writes these. Advisories are produced by governments with a duty to warn and no obligation to reassure. There is no penalty for being too cautious and a real one for being too relaxed. That asymmetry is built into every sentence, and it is why the documents consistently read as gloomier than the experience of actually being here.

    Frequently asked questions

    Did the US and Canada tell people not to travel to Morocco?

    No. Neither issued a “do not travel” or “avoid all travel” warning. The US keeps Morocco at Level 2 of four, and Canada asks for a high degree of caution. Both are middle-tier ratings that mean stay alert, not stay home.

    Is Morocco safe to visit in 2026?

    For the overwhelming majority of visitors, yes, with normal precautions. The realistic risks are petty theft, scams, road safety and seasonal weather. Millions of international visitors come each year and the vast majority have entirely uneventful trips.

    Why is Morocco rated Level 2 rather than Level 1?

    Because of terrorism as a stated risk factor. The State Department’s position is that groups continue plotting attacks in the region. Level 2 reflects a threat that exists rather than one that materialises frequently, and many popular European destinations carry the same rating.

    Which parts of Morocco should I actually avoid?

    The area near the Algerian border, the far southern zone near the Mauritanian frontier where landmine risk is flagged, and remote mountain areas if you would be hiking alone and unguided. Standard tourist routes do not go near any of these.

    Is the Sahara desert safe?

    The established desert destinations — Merzouga, Zagora, the Draa Valley — are well within the regulated tourist zone and are visited daily. The remote-area warnings concern border regions far to the south and east. What matters is going with an operator that uses proper vehicles, experienced drivers and camps with real communications.

    Has there been a recent terrorist attack in Morocco?

    Morocco has not experienced frequent attacks. The advisories describe an ongoing threat and note that the security services regularly disrupt plots, which is a different thing from a pattern of successful attacks. The country invests heavily in counter-terrorism.

    Is it safe for a woman travelling alone?

    Many women travel solo here and enjoy it. Expect catcalling and persistent attention, which is wearing but rarely threatening. Dress moderately, avoid walking alone late at night, use registered taxis, and choose accommodation with staffed reception. Joining a small group tour removes most of the friction.

    Do I need a visa, and how long can I stay?

    US and Canadian citizens do not need a visa for stays of 90 days or less. Your passport should have at least six months’ validity and a blank page for the stamp. Make sure the officer stamps you in on arrival, as a missing entry stamp causes problems on departure. Overstaying 90 days without an extension means appearing before a judge before you can leave.

    Are flights to Morocco still operating normally?

    Yes. European routes run on full schedules and transatlantic services continue as usual. We cover routes, airlines and booking timing in detail in our guide to flights to Morocco on the blog.

    Should I cancel a trip I have already booked?

    Nothing in either advisory suggests you should. If you have specific concerns, the productive move is to check your operator’s credentials, confirm your itinerary avoids the flagged border zones, and make sure your insurance covers medical evacuation. If you would like us to look over an existing plan, get in touch — we will tell you honestly what we think.

    What is the single most useful thing I can do before flying?

    Buy travel insurance with medical evacuation cover, and register with your embassy’s traveller programme. Those two take fifteen minutes and cover the scenarios that actually matter.

    The bottom line

    Headlines about warnings sell. Context does not. The reality behind the story that Canada and US issued Morocco travel warnings over security risks is a routine advisory refresh, an unchanged American rating, and two remote border zones that no ordinary itinerary touches.

    What the advisories genuinely ask of you is to watch your bag in a crowded souk, avoid protests, take road safety seriously, stay out of two remote frontier regions, and use regulated operators rather than improvising in remote places. That is not a warning against visiting Morocco. It is a description of travelling anywhere with your eyes open.

    The country on the other side of that paperwork is the one we live in: a place where you can stand in a thousand-year-old market in the morning, cross a mountain pass at lunch, and watch the light go out over the dunes the same evening. The advisories are worth reading. They are not a reason to miss it.
