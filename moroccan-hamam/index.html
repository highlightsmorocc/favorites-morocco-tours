    Moroccan Hammam

    Discover the authentic Moroccan hammam: traditional steam bath, black soap scrub, and relaxing massage. Experience pure luxury and deep

    Favorites Morocco Tours · Updated: 25 September 2026

    Steam curls up from warm marble. A bucket of hot water lands on your shoulders. Then a woman in a black glove starts to scrub, and grey rolls of old skin slide off your arms. Ten minutes later, you feel lighter than you have in years. That, in one breath, is the hammam Morocco experience.

    Yet most visitors arrive with the same worries. Do I have to be naked? How much should I pay? Is a public hammam in Morocco safe for a foreigner? And which is better, a neighbourhood bath or a riad spa? This guide answers all of it, with real 2026 prices, the unwritten rules that locals rarely explain, and the best hammams in Marrakech, Fes and beyond.

    We are a Marrakech-based team at Favorites Morocco Tours. We build hammam stops into our private routes every week, so the advice below comes from the road, not from a brochure.

    Quick Answer: What Is a Hammam in Morocco?

    A hammam in Morocco is a traditional steam bath where you sweat in hot rooms, cover yourself in black olive soap and get scrubbed with a kessa glove before a cool rinse. Locals go every week. A public hammam costs about 10–20 dirhams to enter, or 50–150 dirhams with a scrub. Spa hammams cost 150–700 dirhams. Men and women always bathe separately.

    1. What Is a Hammam in Morocco? Meaning, Rooms and Ritual

    The Word and the Idea

    The word hammam comes from the Arabic root ḥ-m-m, which means “to heat”. So a hammam is simply a heated place. For Moroccans, going to the hammam is as normal as going to the barber or the market. Many families still go once a week, often on the same day and at the same hour.

    However, the Moroccan bath is not a pool, a sauna or a jacuzzi. There is no plunge pool at all. Instead, water comes from taps and buckets, and heat rises from the floor. In practice, you sit or lie on warm tiles, pour water over yourself and let the steam soften your skin.

    Hammam vs Turkish Bath: The Real Difference

    Many travellers call every hammam a “Turkish bath”. The two share Roman and Byzantine roots, but they grew apart over the centuries. An Ottoman bath usually centres on a raised marble platform under a dome. A Moroccan hammam, by contrast, is a row of vaulted rooms with buckets, low taps and a strong focus on scrubbing. Moreover, the Moroccan ritual relies on local products: black olive soap, ghassoul clay and argan oil.

    The Three Rooms: Barrani, Wasti and Dakhli

    Almost every traditional hammam follows the same layout. First comes the changing room, often called the goulsa, where you undress and leave your bag. Then you walk through three bathing rooms:

    • Barrani — the outer, cooler room, used to rinse and cool down.
    • Wasti — the middle, warm room, where many people scrub and chat.
    • Dakhli — the inner, hottest room, right beside the furnace wall.

    Behind that wall sits the furnace. There, a stoker feeds wood, olive pits or sawdust into the fire all day. Hot water flows to the taps, and hot air runs under the floor, just like in a Roman hypocaust.

    2. A Short History of the Hammam in Morocco

    From Roman Baths to Islamic Purification

    Rome built baths across North Africa, including at Volubilis near Meknes. After the arrival of Islam, the idea took on a new meaning. Muslims perform a full-body wash, called ghusl, in certain situations, and the hammam offered hot water in cities where homes had none. As a result, builders placed baths next to mosques, bakeries and fountains. Together, they formed the basic kit of every medina neighbourhood. Our guide to religion in Morocco explains this daily link between faith and cleanliness.

    Marinid Fes and Saadian Marrakech

    Some of those early bathhouses still stand. In Fes, the Hammam Saffarin on the coppersmiths’ square dates to the 14th-century Marinid period. It still keeps its original furnace and underfloor heating. Nearby, Hammam al-Mokhfiya was built around the reign of Sultan Abu Inan (1348–1358). It once belonged to the religious endowment of the Qarawiyyin Mosque.

    In Marrakech, the Saadian sultan Abdallah al-Ghalib ordered the Mouassine Mosque complex between 1562 and 1573. The complex included a mosque, a fountain, a library and a hammam. Remarkably, that hammam still welcomes bathers today, more than 460 years later.

    The Furnace, the Stoker and the Tanjia

    The furnace also shaped Marrakech food. The stoker, known as the farnatchi, keeps the fire alive from dawn to night. Local men drop off clay urns filled with meat, spices and preserved lemon, and he buries them in the hot ashes. Hours later, the urns come back as tanjia, the city’s famous slow-cooked dish. You can read more about it in our guide to cuisine in Morocco.

    3. Hammam Morocco in 2026: What Has Changed

    The Drought Closures of 2024 and 2025

    If you read older forums, you may see warnings that hammams close three days a week. That was true for a while. On 22 January 2024, after six years of drought, several cities began closing public baths every Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday. The order followed an Interior Ministry circular asking governors to save drinking water.

    Casablanca reimposed the rule on 12 August 2024 and lifted it on 23 October 2024. Rabat was the last big city to reopen its baths every day, in March 2025, after heavy rains refilled the dams. Today, in 2026, hammams in Marrakech, Fes, Casablanca and Rabat open daily. Still, local governors can bring limits back if reservoirs drop again, so ask your riad before you go.

    Why Water and Wood Matter

    The numbers explain the debate. Morocco has about 12,000 hammams, and the sector supports around 200,000 jobs. A busy bath can use 60 to 120 cubic metres of water and up to one and a half tonnes of wood per day, according to the Moroccan business weekly La Vie Éco. Overall, hammams represent roughly 2% of the country’s water use.

    For this reason, the Moroccan Agency for Energy Efficiency (AMEE) promotes improved boilers that burn olive pits and argan shells instead of forest wood. The agency says these systems can raise efficiency by about 40%. As a visitor, you can help too. Fill one or two buckets, not five, and close the tap between rinses.

    Earlier Evenings Since September 2026

    One more change affects your timing. On 20 September 2026, Morocco returned to permanent GMT. As a result, the sun now sets an hour earlier by the clock than it did last summer. Many neighbourhood hammams switch to men-only hours in the evening. Women travellers should therefore plan a late-morning or early-afternoon visit.

    4. Public Hammam, Riad Spa or Palace Spa?

    Your first big choice for a hammam in Morocco is the type of bath. Each one gives a very different afternoon, so match it to your comfort level rather than to your budget alone.

    The Neighbourhood (Public) Hammam

    This is the real thing. You pay at a small window, undress in a tiled room and bathe beside local families. Nobody speaks much English, there is no music and there are no candles. On the other hand, it costs almost nothing and shows a side of daily life that few tourists ever see. Our tip: go with your guide or a local friend the first time.

    The Tourist or Riad Hammam

    Riad spas and mid-range day spas sit in the middle. Staff speak French and English, towels and slippers are provided, and a therapist guides every step. Most visitors choose this option for a first visit. It still feels traditional, yet you never have to guess what comes next.

    The Palace Spa

    Five-star hotels such as La Mamounia and the Royal Mansour in Marrakech turn the ritual into a long luxury treatment. Expect carved cedar ceilings, private suites and aromatic oils. The scrub is gentler and the setting is stunning. However, it feels far less like Moroccan everyday life.

    5. Hammam Treatment in Morocco, Step by Step

    Whether you choose a public bath or a spa, the core hammam treatment in Morocco follows the same order. Knowing it in advance removes most of the nerves.

    1. Change. Leave your clothes in the goulsa and keep on underwear or swim bottoms.
    2. Warm up. Sit in the warm room for five to ten minutes and pour warm water over your body.
    3. Steam. Move to the hot room and relax for 10 to 15 minutes until you sweat freely.
    4. Black soap. Spread savon beldi over your skin and leave it for about five minutes.
    5. Rinse. Wash the soap off with warm water.
    6. Scrub. The attendant scrubs you firmly with the kessa glove, from neck to toes.
    7. Ghassoul. A clay mask goes on the body and hair, and sometimes on the face.
    8. Final rinse. Warm water first, then cooler water to close the pores.
    9. Rest. Wrap up in the cool room, drink water or mint tea and let your body settle.

    What the Scrub Really Feels Like

    Be ready for firm pressure. The attendant, often called a tayyaba on the women’s side and a kessal on the men’s side, works fast and with strength. Grey rolls of skin will appear. That is not dirt; it is dead skin softened by steam and soap. If it hurts, say “shwiya”, which means “gently” or “a little” in Darija. Every attendant understands it.

    The Treatment Menu, Decoded

    Spa menus often look confusing. Here is what the most common names mean:

    • Beldi or traditional ritual: steam, black soap, kessa scrub and rinse. About 45 minutes.
    • Ghassoul ritual: the traditional ritual plus a clay wrap for body and hair.
    • Royal or “Sultan” ritual: scrub, clay, a body mask and a 30- to 60-minute argan massage. Around two hours.
    • Rose or orange blossom ritual: the same steps with floral water and scented oils.
    • Couples’ hammam: a private room for two. Spas offer it; public baths never do.

    7. What Do You Wear in a Hammam in Morocco?

    This is the question we hear most. The honest answer: less than you think, but not nothing.

    Women

    In the women’s section, most Moroccan women wear only underwear or bikini bottoms. Being topless is completely normal there, and nobody stares. If you prefer, keep a two-piece swimsuit on, because attendants are used to it. In spas, staff hand you disposable underwear. Either way, bring a dry pair for afterwards.

    Men

    Men never go fully naked. Keep boxer shorts or swim shorts on at all times, including during the scrub. Among Moroccan men, full nudity in the bath is considered deeply improper. Spas usually provide disposable shorts.

    Children and Families

    Mothers often bring young children of both sexes to the women’s side. Older boys, however, go with their fathers. For modest clothing outside the bath, see our guide on what to wear in Morocco. Our article on traditional Moroccan clothing also explains the djellaba many locals wear to the bath.

    8. What to Bring: Your Hammam Kit

    Spas supply everything. For a public hammam in Morocco, however, pack a small bag with:

    • A kessa glove (15–30 MAD in any souk)
    • Savon beldi black soap, sold by the scoop in a small plastic bag
    • Ghassoul clay, shampoo and a comb
    • Flip-flops, because the floors are wet and hot
    • A towel and a dry change of underwear
    • A plastic mat, and a small bucket if the bath does not rent one
    • Small coins for the entry, the attendant and the cloakroom keeper
    • A bottle of water

    Leave jewellery, watches and phones at your riad. Lockers are rare, and nobody takes photos inside.

    9. Hammam Etiquette: 12 Unwritten Rules

    Moroccans learn these rules as children. Visitors, on the other hand, have to pick them up fast. Follow them, and every hammam in Morocco will welcome you warmly.

    1. Never take photos or videos inside a bath.
    2. Keep your underwear on, especially on the men’s side.
    3. Rinse your floor space before you sit down.
    4. Do not splash water onto your neighbours.
    5. Fill only the buckets you need, because water is precious.
    6. Ask before you use a tap, since people often “reserve” one with a bucket.
    7. Scrub a neighbour’s back if she or he asks; it is a friendly gesture.
    8. Keep your voice low in the hot room.
    9. Walk slowly, as the tiles are slippery.
    10. Pay the attendant directly, and tip if you are happy.
    11. Avoid Friday before noon prayer and the day before Eid, when baths are packed.
    12. Leave the space clean for the next person.

    Travelling alone? Public baths are generally safe and friendly. Still, read our full guide on whether Morocco is safe for tips on scams and late-night walks back to your riad.

    10. A Hammam in Morocco for Every Kind of Traveller

    First-Timers

    Start with a riad or day spa. A therapist leads each step, so you can relax instead of copying the people around you. Then, once you know the ritual, try a neighbourhood bath later in the trip.

    Solo Women

    The women’s side of a hammam in Morocco is one of the most welcoming spaces in the country. Local women often help foreigners, show them where to sit and even lend them soap. Go during the day, and ask your riad host to walk you to the door the first time.

    Couples and Honeymooners

    Because public baths separate the sexes, couples need a spa or a riad with a private hammam room. Many riads in Marrakech and Fes offer a couples’ ritual followed by tea on the roof terrace. Book it for the last evening of your trip, when you most need to unwind.

    Families with Children

    Children usually love the warm water and the buckets. Nevertheless, keep their sessions short, about 20 to 30 minutes, and stay in the warm room rather than the hottest one. Some spas also set a minimum age for massages, so check before you book.

    Muslim Travellers

    For Muslim visitors, a hammam in Morocco feels instantly familiar. Separate sections, female therapists for women and modest dress are the norm, not a special request. In addition, many neighbourhood baths open very early, so men can wash before dawn prayer.

    11. The Best Hammams in Morocco by City

    Hammam in Marrakech: The Widest Choice

    Marrakech has the richest mix of baths in the country. For history, start with Hammam Mouassine, still running inside its Saadian complex. The medina also hides many small neighbourhood baths near mosques, so ask your riad for the closest one. For comfort, dozens of riads in the Kasbah and Mouassine districts run small hammam suites. For a splurge, the palace spas of La Mamounia and the Royal Mansour are world famous.

    Combine your bath with a visit to Dar el Bacha and the souks nearby. Interestingly, the smooth, waterproof walls of many hammams use tadelakt, the polished lime plaster of Marrakech. The whole old city belongs to the Medina of Marrakesh UNESCO site, and our complete Marrakech travel guide maps it all.

    Hammam Fes Morocco: The Oldest Tradition

    Fes may be the best city for a truly local bath. Its medina counts more than 100 hammams, many of them centuries old. The restored Hammam Saffarin on Place Seffarine is a must-see for architecture lovers, even if you bathe elsewhere. Meanwhile, several riads near Bab Boujloud and in the Andalusian quarter offer calm, guided rituals at lower prices than Marrakech.

    The city sits inside the Medina of Fez World Heritage site, inscribed by UNESCO in 1981. Plan your stay with our Fes Morocco guide and our list of things to do in Fez.

    Chefchaouen and the North

    In the blue town, small public baths serve the medina, and a few guesthouses have private hammams. In Tangier and Tetouan, you may even meet attendants who speak some Spanish, a legacy of the old protectorate. Read more in our Chefchaouen Morocco guide.

    Essaouira and the Atlantic Coast

    After a windy day on the beach, a hammam in Essaouira feels perfect. Riads there often pair the scrub with argan oil from nearby cooperatives. The town is an easy day trip from Marrakech, although one night gives you time for the spa.

    The Atlas Mountains

    In the Berber villages of the High Atlas, such as Imlil, small wood-heated hammams serve whole communities. Some guesthouses also let trekkers use their family bath after a long day on the trails toward Toubkal. Read our guide to the Atlas Mountains Morocco or join our Imlil valley day trip.

    The Sahara: Sand Baths in Merzouga

    The desert has its own version of the bath. Each summer, mainly in July and August, Moroccans travel to the dunes of Erg Chebbi near Merzouga for sand baths. Helpers dig a shallow trench in the hot sand, and the bather lies inside, covered up to the neck for 10 to 15 minutes. Locals believe it eases rheumatism and joint pain. Afterwards, bathers rest in a tent and drink hot tea. Only try it with experienced helpers, and skip it if you have heart problems. Learn more in our Sahara desert Morocco guide.

    13. Hammam Products to Bring Home

    Savon Beldi (Black Soap)

    Savon beldi is a soft, dark paste made from olive oil and crushed black olives, sometimes with eucalyptus. Real beldi feels like thick jelly and smells earthy. Buy it in herbalist shops, called attarine, rather than at tourist stalls.

    Ghassoul Clay

    Ghassoul, also spelled rhassoul, is a mineral clay mined in the Moulouya valley of eastern Morocco, near Missour. Mixed with water or rose water, it cleans hair and skin without harsh detergents. Moroccan brides still receive it as part of their wedding hammam.

    Argan Oil

    Argan oil comes from a tree that grows mainly in south-west Morocco. In 2014, UNESCO added argan practices and know-how to its list of intangible cultural heritage. Cosmetic argan is pale and almost odourless, while culinary argan smells toasted. Buy from women’s cooperatives when you can; our note on artisan factories and cooperatives shows how to spot the real ones.

    The Kessa Glove and Floral Waters

    Finally, pick up a kessa glove, rose water from Kelaat M’Gouna in the Valley of Roses, and orange blossom water. Together they weigh little and let you repeat the ritual at home every week.

    14. How to Recreate a Hammam Morocco Ritual at Home

    You do not need marble to keep the habit alive. A hot shower and a few products from the souk are enough.

    1. Close the bathroom door and run a hot shower for five to ten minutes to build steam.
    2. Step in, wet your skin and spread black soap over your body. Wait three to five minutes.
    3. Rinse, then scrub with the kessa glove in long, firm strokes.
    4. Mix two spoons of ghassoul with warm water or rose water, and leave it on for five minutes.
    5. Rinse with warm water, then finish with a cooler splash.
    6. Pat dry and massage a few drops of argan oil into damp skin.

    Once a week is plenty. More often, the kessa can irritate your skin, especially in winter.

    15. Health Benefits and Who Should Skip the Heat

    Why People Love It

    A hammam in Morocco relaxes tired muscles, and the scrub removes dead skin, so your skin feels smooth for days. Many travellers also sleep better after a session. That said, scientific studies on specific products such as ghassoul remain limited. Treat bold health claims with care.

    When to Check with a Doctor First

    • Pregnancy, especially in the first trimester
    • Heart disease, or very high or low blood pressure
    • Recent tattoos, sunburn, open wounds or eczema flare-ups
    • Waxing or shaving within the last 24 hours
    • A heavy meal or alcohol just before the session

    In the heat, stand up slowly and drink water. If you feel dizzy, move to the cool room at once.

    16. Five Myths About the Moroccan Hammam

    • “It’s the same as a Turkish bath.” Not quite. Moroccan baths have no pool and focus on buckets, black soap and the kessa scrub.
    • “You must be naked.” Wrong. Men always keep shorts on, and women choose what to keep.
    • “Public hammams are dirty.” In reality, floors are rinsed constantly; still, bring flip-flops and your own mat for peace of mind.
    • “Foreigners are not welcome.” Most local baths welcome respectful visitors, although a few serve regulars only.
    • “The grey rolls are dirt.” They are actually dead skin cells loosened by steam and soap.

    18. When to Go: Days, Hours and Seasons

    Timing makes a big difference for a hammam in Morocco. Most neighbourhood baths open from early morning until late evening. Women usually have the middle of the day, while men take the early morning and the evening. Some larger baths have two separate sections and serve both at once.

    Thursday evening and Friday morning are the busiest times, because many people wash before Friday prayer. The days before Eid are even busier. During Ramadan, hammams often open later and stay open past midnight. Ramadan 2027 is expected to begin around 8 February 2027, depending on the moon.

    As for seasons, winter is the best time of year for a hammam. Riads can feel cold from December to February, and the warm rooms are a joy. Summer baths, by contrast, feel intense, so go in the morning. For month-by-month advice, read our guide to the best time to visit Morocco.

    Hammam Morocco FAQ

    What is a hammam in Morocco?

    A hammam in Morocco is a traditional public steam bath. You sweat in hot rooms, wash with black olive soap and get scrubbed with a rough kessa glove. Moroccans use it weekly for hygiene, relaxation and social life.

    How much is a hammam in Morocco?

    A public hammam costs 10–20 MAD to enter and 50–150 MAD with a scrub in 2026. A riad or day spa charges 150–700 MAD. Palace spas often start around 1,500 MAD.

    What do you wear in a hammam in Morocco?

    Women usually wear underwear or bikini bottoms, and going topless is normal on the women’s side. Men must keep shorts or underwear on at all times. Spas provide disposable underwear.

    Can tourists go to a local hammam?

    Yes. Most neighbourhood baths welcome respectful visitors. Go with a local guide the first time, ask the price before you undress and bring your own kit.

    How long does a hammam last?

    A basic scrub takes about 45 minutes. A full ritual with a clay wrap and massage takes 90 minutes to two hours. Locals often stay longer and chat.

    Are hammams in Morocco mixed?

    No. Men and women always bathe separately, either in different sections or at different hours. Only private spa rooms accept couples.

    Is the hammam in Fes better than in Marrakech?

    Fes offers older, more local baths and lower prices. Marrakech offers more choice, from Hammam Mouassine to five-star palace spas. Both are excellent.

    Can couples do a hammam together?

    Yes, but only in private spa or riad rooms. Public hammams never mix men and women, even for married couples.

    Are hammams still closed because of the drought?

    No. The three-day weekly closures of 2024 ended in Casablanca in October 2024 and in Rabat in March 2025. Hammams now open daily, although local authorities can reintroduce limits in dry years.

    Is a hammam in Morocco good for your skin?

    Yes, for most people. The steam opens the pores and the kessa removes dead skin, so your skin stays soft for days. People with eczema, rosacea or very sensitive skin should ask for a gentle scrub or skip it.

    Do I need to book a hammam in Morocco?

    Only for spas and riads, ideally a day ahead. Neighbourhood baths take walk-ins during men’s or women’s hours.

    What should I do after a hammam?

    Rest, drink water and apply argan oil while your skin is still warm. Avoid strong sun, swimming pools and heavy exercise for a few hours.
