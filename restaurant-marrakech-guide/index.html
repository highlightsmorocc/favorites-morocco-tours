    Restaurant Marrakech Guide: Where to Eat in the Red City

    The complete restaurant Marrakech guide: street food rooftops riad dining and Gueliz plus prices alcohol rules Ramadan hours and dishes

    Favorites Morocco Tours · Updated: 11 September 2026

    Choosing a restaurant Marrakech visitors will actually remember is harder than it should be. The medina hides hundreds of places behind unmarked doors, the square fills with a hundred identical-looking stalls each night, and the best cooking in the city is often served in a converted house with no sign at all.

    This guide sorts the options. You will find the types of restaurant Marrakech offers, the neighbourhoods worth eating in, the dishes to order, realistic prices, and clear answers on alcohol, Ramadan hours and dietary needs. Use it to build a week of good meals rather than gambling on whichever terrace you happen to walk past.

    The Restaurant Marrakech Scene in 2026

    Marrakech now supports one of the most varied food scenes in North Africa. Three forces shaped it. First, tourism brought demand for quality and for menus in several languages. Second, a generation of Moroccan chefs trained in France and returned to reinterpret traditional recipes. Third, the riad conversion boom created hundreds of atmospheric dining rooms in the medina.

    The result is a city where you can eat grilled sardines from a cart for 30 dirhams at lunch and a tasting menu for 900 dirhams at dinner. Both are legitimate. Furthermore, the gap in quality between them is smaller than the gap in price.

    What has changed recently

    • Card payment is now common in mid-range and upmarket venues, though cash still rules at stalls.
    • Reservations matter far more than they did five years ago, especially at rooftops.
    • Vegetarian menus have expanded significantly beyond vegetable couscous.
    • Specialty coffee arrived in Gueliz and quickly spread into the medina.
    • Several long-standing tourist traps around the main square have finally closed.

    Why this guide names no venues

    Any restaurant Marrakech list goes stale quickly, because kitchens open and close fast and chefs move on. This guide teaches you to read a place instead. Learn the signals, the fair price and the dishes worth ordering, and you will choose well on any street, including one that opened last week.

    Types of Restaurant Marrakech Offers

    Understanding the restaurant Marrakech categories saves time. Each one delivers a different evening, and mixing them across a trip gives the fullest picture of Moroccan cooking.

    Street food stalls

    Found on Jemaa el-Fnaa and in local markets, these serve grilled meat, snails, sardines, harira soup and sheep’s head. Prices are the lowest in the city, turnover is fast, and the atmosphere is unmatched. Point at what you want, because menus rarely exist.

    Neighbourhood eateries

    Small, plain rooms where residents eat lunch. Expect a single daily dish, plastic chairs and a bill under 60 dirhams. These are the best value in Marrakech and the least visited by tourists.

    Riad restaurants

    Converted courtyard houses serving traditional menus by candlelight, often with a set three-course format. Ambience is the main product, and quality ranges from outstanding to merely pleasant. Booking is essential, since most seat fewer than forty guests.

    Rooftop terraces

    Every second building in the medina seems to have one. The best combine a Koutoubia view with genuinely good grilling. The worst rely entirely on the view. Check recent reviews rather than trusting the terrace itself.

    Palace restaurants

    Large, richly decorated venues offering multi-course Moroccan feasts with music and occasionally belly dancing. They suit groups and celebrations. Purists find them theatrical, and portions are enormous.

    Modern Moroccan and fusion

    Chef-led kitchens reworking tanjia, pastilla and pigeon with lighter technique. This category has grown fastest, and it produces the most interesting cooking in the city. Most sit in Gueliz, Hivernage or the quieter medina lanes.

    International restaurants

    Italian, Japanese, Lebanese, French and Spanish kitchens serve residents and long-stay visitors. Standards are solid, and they are useful when a family has eaten tagine four nights running.

    Hotel dining

    Five-star hotels in Hivernage and the Palmeraie run polished restaurants with reliable service, licensed bars and poolside lunches. Prices sit at the top of the market.

    Restaurant Marrakech Districts: Where to Eat

    Location shapes the restaurant Marrakech experience as much as the kitchen does. Here is how each district eats. For the wider geography of the city, our guide to Marrakech old town and new town maps every quarter.

    Jemaa el-Fnaa and the surrounding lanes

    The square is a restaurant in itself after dark. Numbered stalls set up from around six, serving skewers, merguez, aubergine salad, chips and harira on communal benches. Choose a stall with locals eating at it, agree what you are ordering before food arrives, and check the bill carefully.

    Around the square, rooftop cafés serve mediocre food with excellent views. Treat them as a drinks stop rather than a dinner plan.

    Mouassine and Dar el Bacha

    This quarter holds the most interesting restaurant Marrakech has inside its walls. Restored riads, design-led cafés, small wine-free bistros and specialty coffee sit within a few hundred metres of each other. If you only have one medina dinner, book it here.

    The Kasbah

    Quieter and more residential, the Kasbah suits lunch after visiting the Saadian Tombs and El Badi. Expect straightforward tagines, good value and few crowds.

    The Mellah

    The old Jewish quarter holds the city’s best spice market and several excellent grill houses. Prices drop noticeably here compared with the main square.

    Gueliz

    The new town is where residents eat. Fixed prices, licensed venues, international cuisines, proper coffee and later hours define it. Any restaurant Marrakech locals recommend for a birthday is probably in Gueliz.

    Hivernage

    Upmarket, hotel-adjacent and dressier. Come for polished service, cocktails and modern Moroccan cooking. Reservations are expected at weekends.

    Palmeraie

    Resort restaurants and garden terraces, ideal for a long lunch by a pool. Getting there requires a taxi, so build it into an unhurried afternoon.

    Sidi Ghanem

    The design district north of town has developed a small cluster of daytime cafés serving contemporary food to studio workers. It pairs well with a shopping morning.

    Restaurant Marrakech Menus: Dishes You Should Order

    Moroccan cooking rewards curiosity, and no restaurant Marrakech menu is fully explored in one meal. Order beyond the tagine, and the trip changes. Our guide to traditional Moroccan dishes covers each plate in far more depth.

    Tanjia marrakchia

    This is the signature dish of the city, and no restaurant Marrakech visitors choose should be judged before they try it. Beef or lamb, preserved lemon, cumin, garlic and smen are sealed in a clay urn and buried in the embers of a hammam furnace for five to seven hours. The meat collapses at the touch of a spoon. Order it a day ahead where possible. Our guide to the Moroccan hammam explains where those embers come from.

    Tagine

    The conical clay pot gives its name to the stew cooked inside. Classic versions include chicken with preserved lemon and green olives, lamb with prunes and almonds, and kefta with eggs baked on top. Vegetable tagines are widely available and genuinely good.

    Couscous

    Traditionally a Friday dish served after midday prayers. Steamed three times, topped with seven vegetables and a broth poured at the table. Many restaurants serve it daily now, but Friday remains the day to seek it out.

    Pastilla

    A layered pie of shredded pigeon or chicken, almonds and eggs in warqa pastry, dusted with icing sugar and cinnamon. The sweet-savoury combination surprises first-timers and converts most of them.

    Harira

    Tomato, lentil and chickpea soup thickened with flour and finished with coriander. It costs a few dirhams on the square and appears everywhere during Ramadan.

    Mechoui

    Whole lamb slow-roasted in an underground oven and sold by weight. The alley of mechoui stalls just off Jemaa el-Fnaa serves it with cumin and salt from mid-morning until it runs out.

    Moroccan salads

    Meals begin with small cooked salads: zaalouk of smoked aubergine, taktouka of peppers and tomatoes, carrots with cumin, and beetroot with orange. Order a selection and plenty of bread.

    Breakfast and street snacks

    • Msemen — flaky square pancakes with honey or cheese.
    • Beghrir — spongy semolina pancakes with butter and honey.
    • Sfenj — doughnuts fried fresh and sold by the ring.
    • Bissara — fava bean soup with olive oil and cumin, eaten early.
    • Briouat — filled pastry triangles, savoury or sweet.

    Drinks

    Mint tea arrives constantly and is poured from height to aerate it. Fresh orange juice costs almost nothing on the square. Avocado and almond milkshakes are a local obsession. Coffee comes as nous-nous, half espresso and half milk. Our overview of cuisine in Morocco sets the whole larder in context.

    Does a Restaurant Marrakech Visitors Pick Serve Alcohol?

    Some do, many do not, and the distinction follows licensing rather than location. Morocco permits alcohol sales to non-Muslims and, in practice, to anyone in licensed venues.

    Where you will find it

    • Hotel restaurants and bars, almost universally licensed.
    • Upmarket riads, many of which hold licences.
    • Most restaurants in Gueliz and Hivernage.
    • Supermarkets such as Carrefour, in a separate section.

    Where you will not

    Street stalls, neighbourhood eateries and a large share of medina restaurants serve no alcohol at all. Nobody minds if you do not drink, and mint tea is the default accompaniment to dinner.

    Practical advice

    If wine matters for a particular evening, confirm when you book rather than on arrival. Some unlicensed venues allow you to bring your own bottle for a small corkage fee, though you should always ask first. Public drinking outside licensed premises is not acceptable anywhere in the city.

    Moroccan wine is worth trying

    Vineyards around Meknes and Benslimane produce respectable reds, rosés and a few crisp whites. A bottle in a restaurant typically costs 250 to 500 dirhams, which is far better value than imported labels.

    Is Every Restaurant Marrakech Has Open During Ramadan?

    Most are, though the pattern shifts. During Ramadan, most Moroccans fast from dawn until sunset, and daytime life slows accordingly.

    What stays open by day

    Hotel restaurants serve normally, and a good number of tourist-facing venues in the medina and Gueliz continue lunch service, sometimes behind screens. Riads always feed their own guests.

    What closes

    Neighbourhood eateries, street stalls and local cafés generally shut until sunset. Consequently, casual daytime snacking becomes harder, so plan lunch in advance.

    What Ramadan gives you instead

    The evening is extraordinary. At sunset the whole city breaks its fast together with harira, dates, chebakia pastries and hard-boiled eggs. Restaurants fill immediately, then a second wave of eating continues past midnight. Booking iftar at a riad is one of the warmest experiences the city offers.

    Etiquette while others fast

    Avoid eating, drinking or smoking in the street during daylight hours. Nobody will confront you, yet the courtesy is noticed and appreciated.

    Vegetarian, Vegan and Special Diets

    Vegetarian

    Eating vegetarian in any restaurant Marrakech offers is straightforward. Vegetable tagine, vegetable couscous, zaalouk, taktouka, lentil dishes, bissara and salads all appear on standard menus. Ask whether the couscous broth contains meat, because it often does.

    Vegan

    Harder but perfectly manageable. Bread, olive oil, salads, fava bean soup and vegetable tagines form the base. Watch for butter, smen and honey. Several dedicated vegan restaurants now operate in the medina and Gueliz.

    Gluten-free

    Challenging, since bread accompanies everything and couscous is semolina. Tagines, grilled meat, salads and rice dishes work well. Carry a written explanation in French, which every kitchen reads.

    Nut allergies

    Take this seriously. Almonds appear in pastilla, tagines, pastries and sauces, and cross-contamination is common. Explain clearly in French and choose simpler grilled dishes when in doubt.

    Halal and pork

    Effectively all food in the city is halal. Pork appears only in a handful of international venues and imported sections of large supermarkets.

    Kosher food

    Marrakech has a small Jewish community centred on the Mellah, and kosher catering still operates through the synagogue and a handful of certified kitchens. Arrangements are usually made in advance rather than by walking in, so contact the community office before you travel. Several hotels can also organise sealed kosher meals with notice.

    Halal certification

    Formal certification is rare because it is unnecessary: meat sold in Morocco is halal by default. Travellers who require documented certification should ask their hotel to confirm supplier details, since restaurants themselves rarely hold paperwork.

    Ordering with confidence

    Whatever your dietary requirement, write it in French on your phone and show it to the kitchen rather than the waiter. French is the language of professional cooking here, and a written note removes the ambiguity that spoken explanations often create.

    Tipping

    Leave around 10 per cent in a sit-down restaurant, and round up at cafés and stalls. Some upmarket venues add a service charge, so check the bill before adding more.

    Paying

    Cards work in most mid-range and upmarket venues. Stalls, small cafés and neighbourhood eateries take cash only. Keep 20 and 50 dirham notes, because change is frequently short.

    How a bill quietly grows

    Extras are the usual mechanism. Bread, olives and bottled water are charged separately in many places, normally 10 to 20 dirhams. That is not a scam, yet it is worth knowing before you accept a third basket of bread.

    Arriving at seven for dinner means an empty dining room. Aim for half past eight if you want atmosphere. Check the country’s clock too, since our guide to what time it is in Morocco now explains the recent change.

    Booking

    Reserve any restaurant Marrakech keeps inside the medina with fewer than forty covers, and reserve every rooftop with a Koutoubia view. Many riads take bookings only by phone or WhatsApp, so ask your accommodation to call. Weekend evenings from March to May and September to November fill fastest.

    Dress code

    Smart casual covers almost every restaurant Marrakech puts in front of you. Palace restaurants, hotel dining rooms and Hivernage venues lean dressier, while the medina stays relaxed. Cover shoulders and knees when walking there and back, since you pass through residential lanes. Our guide on what to wear in Morocco goes further.

    Children

    Children are welcome in nearly every restaurant Marrakech families walk into, and staff usually make a fuss of them. High chairs are rarer than in Europe, so ask when booking. Portions are generous enough to share.

    How to Spot a Good Restaurant and Avoid the Traps

    Restaurant Marrakech tourist traps cluster where footfall is highest, particularly on the streets immediately around Jemaa el-Fnaa. A few signals separate the good from the forgettable.

    Green flags

    • A short menu, which suggests fresh cooking rather than a freezer.
    • Moroccan customers, especially at lunchtime.
    • Tanjia offered with advance notice, a sign of a real kitchen.
    • Prices displayed clearly outside.
    • Staff who describe dishes rather than pushing you inside.

    Red flags

    • A host physically blocking the pavement to bring you in.
    • Laminated photo menus in eight languages.
    • Identical menus at six neighbouring venues.
    • No prices anywhere, followed by a bill negotiated at the end.
    • Empty dining rooms at nine in the evening during high season.

    The bill

    Check it. Errors happen, occasionally deliberately. Confirm the price of bread, olives and bottled water, which are sometimes charged separately. Politeness works better than confrontation, and mistakes are usually corrected without argument.

    Food Safety and Eating Well

    Stomach upsets are common but avoidable, and they rarely come from the busiest restaurant Marrakech kitchens. The main risks are water, unwashed salad and food that has been sitting.

    • Drink bottled or filtered water, and skip ice in very cheap venues.
    • Choose busy stalls, because high turnover means fresh food.
    • Eat cooked food hot rather than lukewarm.
    • Peel fruit yourself where possible.
    • Carry hand sanitiser, since stalls rarely have sinks.
    • Introduce street food gradually rather than on your first evening.

    Pharmacies stock effective remedies and pharmacists give sensible advice, so a mild upset need not derail a trip.

    One further habit helps more than any other: pace yourself. Portions are generous, meals are long, and mint tea is sweet. Travellers who order three courses at every sitting usually feel heavy by day three, whereas those who alternate a big lunch with a light dinner keep eating happily all week.

    The Best Rooftop Restaurants Experience

    Rooftops define the restaurant Marrakech experience, and choosing well transforms an ordinary meal.

    What makes a rooftop worth it

    Look for a clear line of sight to the Koutoubia minaret, shade or heaters depending on season, and a kitchen that grills properly. Sunset seating books out first, so reserve two hours before dusk.

    Rooftops near Jemaa el-Fnaa

    Several cafés on the southern edge of the square open their terraces from late afternoon. Food is simple, yet watching the stalls assemble below is worth the price of a tea alone.

    Rooftops in Mouassine and the Kasbah

    Quieter, more design-conscious and generally better for food. Many belong to riads and welcome non-residents with a booking.

    Rooftops in Gueliz

    Modern terraces with cocktails, wider menus and views over the new town toward the Atlas. These stay open later than medina venues.

    Cooking Classes and Food Tours

    Cooking classes

    A half-day class usually starts with a market visit, continues with salad preparation, and finishes with a tagine or pastilla you cook yourself. Expect to pay 400 to 800 dirhams per person including lunch. It is the single best way to understand why every restaurant Marrakech opens tastes the way it does.

    Street food tours

    Guided evening walks take you to stalls you would never choose alone: snail broth, grilled hearts, mechoui, sfenj and fresh juice. Around 400 to 600 dirhams covers three hours and plenty of food.

    Market visits

    The Mellah spice market and Bab Doukkala market show how residents actually shop. Go in the morning, ask before photographing, and buy saffron only from reputable sellers, because adulteration is common.

    Fitting them into a trip

    A cooking class works best on your first full day, because it teaches you the names you will see all week. A street food tour pays off later, once you know what you like. We can add either to your day trips from Marrakech.

    Coffee, Pastries and Sweet Things

    Moroccan pastries

    Almond-heavy and often honey-soaked, they arrive with tea rather than after dinner. Look for kaab el ghzal (gazelle horns), chebakia, ghriba and briouat with almond paste.

    Specialty coffee

    Every restaurant Marrakech now shares the city with a real coffee culture, with roasters in Gueliz and a few excellent medina cafés. If you want a flat white rather than nous-nous, the new town is your best bet.

    Ice cream and juice

    Orange juice carts on the square charge a handful of dirhams. Avocado and date shakes are richer and more filling. Several good gelaterias operate in Gueliz for hot afternoons.

    How Service Works in a Moroccan Restaurant

    Dining rhythms in any restaurant Marrakech runs differ from European habits, and knowing the sequence prevents confusion on your first evening.

    Bread arrives immediately

    Round khobz appears before you order, and it functions as cutlery. Tear a piece, pinch the food with it, and eat with your right hand.

    Salads come first

    A traditional meal opens with several small cooked salads shared across the table. They are not starters in the European sense; they are part of the same course, and refills are common.

    The main dish is shared

    Tagines and couscous arrive in one vessel for the table. Everyone eats from the section directly in front of them rather than reaching across. Hosts will push the best pieces of meat toward guests, which is a compliment.

    Service is unhurried

    Expect a leisurely pace. Waiters rarely bring the bill until you ask, because rushing guests would be impolite. Say l’hsab, afak when you are ready.

    Mint tea closes the meal

    Tea is poured from height into small glasses, and refusing the first glass is unusual. Three glasses is the traditional sequence, each slightly stronger than the last.

    Restaurant Marrakech Options by Budget

    Every budget eats well here, and the restaurant Marrakech market is unusually wide. What changes is the setting rather than the quality of the cooking.

    Under 100 dirhams per person

    Street stalls, neighbourhood counters and market grills. Order harira, a skewer plate, bissara or a simple tagine. You will eat as well as anyone in the city, sitting on a plastic stool.

    100 to 300 dirhams per person

    Rooftop terraces and mid-range medina venues. This band covers the majority of restaurants in Marrakech that visitors use, and it includes most riad dinners with a set menu.

    300 to 700 dirhams per person

    Chef-led modern Moroccan kitchens, licensed dining rooms in Gueliz and Hivernage, and the better palace restaurants. Service is polished and reservations are expected.

    Above 700 dirhams per person

    Tasting menus, hotel fine dining and destination restaurants with gardens and live music. Worth it for one special evening rather than every night.

    Spreading a week’s budget

    One formula works better than the rest. Give two evenings to the middle band, one to the top, and eat cheaply the rest of the week. You sample every register without the weekly total climbing, and the cheap meals are usually the ones you describe afterwards.

    Eating Alone in Marrakech

    Solo travellers eat comfortably in almost any restaurant Marrakech offers, though a few habits help. Lunch is the easiest solo meal, since neighbourhood eateries are busy and nobody pays attention to a single diner.

    For dinner, communal benches on Jemaa el-Fnaa work well, because you sit alongside other people by design. Riad restaurants also suit solo guests, as staff are attentive and the rooms are small. Book a table rather than arriving late, when the last seats go to couples.

    Women dining alone report very few problems, particularly in Gueliz and in riads. Sitting inside rather than on a street-facing terrace reduces unwanted conversation, and taking a taxi back after ten in the evening is a sensible default.

    A Four-Day Restaurant Marrakech Eating Plan

    Use this as a template rather than a rule. It balances price, atmosphere and variety, and it keeps each day’s heaviest meal at a sensible hour.

    Day one

    Breakfast: msemen and mint tea on your riad terrace.
    Lunch: a simple tagine near the souks.
    Dinner: stalls on Jemaa el-Fnaa, followed by tea on a rooftop overlooking the square.

    Day two

    Breakfast: bissara and bread at a local counter.
    Lunch: mechoui lamb by weight in the alley off the square.
    Dinner: a candlelit riad restaurant in Mouassine, booked in advance.

    Day three

    Morning: a cooking class starting with the market.
    Lunch: whatever you cooked.
    Dinner: modern Moroccan in Gueliz, with a bottle of Moroccan red.

    Day four

    Breakfast: specialty coffee and pastries in the new town.
    Lunch: a long, slow poolside meal in the Palmeraie.
    Dinner: tanjia, ordered a day ahead at a traditional restaurant.

    If it is Friday

    Move couscous to lunch. Families eat it after midday prayers, and many kitchens prepare their best version only on that day.

    Eating Outside the Restaurant Scene

    Markets and self-catering

    If your riad or apartment has a kitchen, the markets compete directly with any restaurant Marrakech can offer. Bab Doukkala market sells vegetables, olives, herbs and fish. Bakeries sell bread twice daily, and many households still send dough to a communal oven.

    Picnics and day trips

    Buy bread, olives, cheese, tomatoes and fruit before heading to the Ourika Valley or the Agafay desert. Roadside cafés exist, but a picnic beside the river beats most of them. Our Ourika Valley day trip and the Agafay desert excursion both suit this.

    Desert and mountain food

    On a Sahara trip, meals are simple and repetitive: soup, tagine, bread, fruit. Bring snacks you actually like, particularly if you are fussy or travelling with children. Our comparison of Erg Chebbi and Erg Chigaga explains what camp catering involves at each, and our 3-day desert tour from Marrakech lists what is included.

    Eating with Berber families

    On Atlas and southern routes, many stops include a meal in a family home. That is often the best tagine of the whole trip, because it cooks slowly for a small table. Our guide to Berber Morocco explains the etiquette.

    Seasonal Restaurant Marrakech Menus Through the Year

    Menus shift with the harvest, and the restaurant Marrakech serves you in January differs from the one you visit in June.

    Winter

    Soups dominate. Harira, bissara and lentil dishes appear everywhere, and slow-cooked tagines suit the cold evenings. Oranges and mandarins are at their best, so juice is cheap and excellent.

    Spring

    Artichokes, broad beans and young peas enter the tagines, and the first apricots arrive in May. Rooftops become usable again, and terrace bookings tighten.

    Summer

    Salads, grilled fish and melon replace heavy stews at lunch. Many restaurants shift dinner service later, because nobody wants to eat before nine. Air-conditioned dining rooms suddenly become desirable.

    Autumn

    Dates arrive from the southern oases, figs and grapes appear, and pomegranate turns up in salads and desserts. Most food writers consider October the best month to eat here, and our guide to the best time to visit Morocco covers the wider climate.

    Food Souvenirs Worth Taking Home

    • Ras el hanout — the house spice blend, best bought where you can smell it first.
    • Argan oil — buy culinary grade from a cooperative, and check it smells nutty rather than rancid.
    • Preserved lemons — sealed jars travel well and transform a home tagine.
    • Dates — Medjool from the Draa Valley, sold loose by weight.
    • Amlou — almond, argan and honey spread, often called Moroccan Nutella.
    • Green tea and dried mint — light, cheap and genuinely useful at home.
    • A tagine pot — buy a cooking version in unglazed clay, not a painted display piece.

    A word on saffron

    Real Moroccan saffron comes from Taliouine and costs a lot. Anything sold cheaply in the souks is almost certainly safflower. Buy from a specialist spice shop and expect to pay accordingly.

    Customs and packing

    Check your home country’s rules on food imports before buying meat, dairy or fresh produce. Spices, tea, oil and sealed preserves are usually fine, though liquids over 100 millilitres must travel in checked luggage. Our guide to flights to Marrakech covers baggage rules.

    Mistakes to Avoid When Choosing a Restaurant

    • Eating every meal on a rooftop. Views are wonderful; the kitchens are often average.
    • Judging the city by one bad tagine. Quality varies enormously between venues.
    • Following touts. The best places do not need someone stopping you in the street.
    • Skipping lunch. Lunch is the main Moroccan meal and often the best value.
    • Never ordering tanjia. It is the city’s signature dish and rarely appears outside Marrakech.
    • Assuming every restaurant Marrakech offers serves wine. Many do not, so check first.
    • Arriving at 19:00 for dinner. You will be alone in the room.

    Choosing Your Restaurant in Marrakech Tonight

    If you are reading this on the day and simply need a decision, work through these five questions.

    1. Do you want a view or good food? Rooftops win on views; courtyards win on cooking.
    2. Do you want wine? If so, book in Gueliz or Hivernage, since many restaurants in Marrakech are unlicensed.
    3. How much walking do you want after dinner? Medina lanes are dark late; the new town is well lit.
    4. Is it Friday? Then eat couscous at lunch and something lighter in the evening.
    5. Have you tried tanjia yet? If not, call a traditional restaurant now and order it for tomorrow.

    Answering those five settles most evenings. When you are still undecided, ask the person who runs your riad. Their recommendation for a restaurant Marrakech residents actually use will beat any ranking online, because they eat there themselves and they will hear about it if the meal disappoints.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Do restaurants in Marrakech serve alcohol?

    Licensed venues do, including most hotels, upmarket riads and restaurants in Gueliz and Hivernage. Street stalls and many medina restaurants serve none. Check when booking if wine matters to your evening.

    Are restaurants open in Marrakech during Ramadan?

    Hotel restaurants and many tourist-facing venues stay open for lunch, while local eateries and stalls close until sunset. After sunset the city eats enthusiastically, and iftar is worth booking.

    What are the must-try restaurants dishes in Marrakech?

    Order tanjia marrakchia first, then pastilla, a lamb tagine with prunes, Friday couscous, mechoui lamb and a spread of small cooked salads with bread.

    How much does a restaurant Marrakech dinner cost?

    Street food runs 50 to 90 dirhams, a mid-range riad dinner sits between 250 and 400, and fine dining reaches 700 to 1,500 per person.

    Is street food safe to eat?

    Generally yes, if you choose busy stalls with fast turnover and eat food hot from the grill. Avoid lukewarm dishes and untreated water.

    Do you need to book a restaurant Marrakech visitors recommend?

    For riads, rooftops and anywhere popular, yes, especially at weekends in spring and autumn. Stalls and neighbourhood eateries never need a reservation.

    Is vegetarian food easy to find?

    Very. Vegetable tagines, couscous, lentils, fava bean soup and cooked salads appear on almost every menu. Confirm that the broth is meat-free.

    What time do restaurants close?

    Medina venues typically stop serving around 23:00, while Gueliz and Hivernage run later, particularly at weekends.

    Can you drink the tap water?

    Better not to. It is treated, yet it differs enough from European supplies to upset some visitors. Bottled water costs very little.

    Should you tip?

    Yes, modestly. Ten per cent in a sit-down venue and rounding up at cafés and stalls is considered correct.

    Final Thoughts

    The best meal you eat here probably will not be the most expensive. It might be a plate of grilled sardines on a plastic stool, a bowl of bissara at eight in the morning, or a tanjia ordered the day before and eaten with your fingers and a piece of bread.

    Move between the categories. Spend one night on a rooftop, one at a stall, one in a courtyard and one in the new town. Order something you cannot pronounce at least twice. Whichever restaurant Marrakech finally serves you your favourite dish, it will almost certainly be one you found by asking a local rather than by walking past.
