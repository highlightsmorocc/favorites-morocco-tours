    What Is Tadelakt? How It’s Made, Bathrooms, Care & Marrakech Workshops

    What is tadelakt? The polished lime finish of Marrakech: origins, how it's made step by step, tadelakt bathrooms and showers, care tips and workshops.

    Favorites Morocco Tours · Updated: 23 September 2026

    Quick answer: tadelakt is a traditional Marrakech wall finish made from local lime, polished by hand with a stone and sealed with black soap. The result is a smooth, glowing and water-resistant surface. That is why it has been used for centuries in hammams, riads, fountains and cisterns, and today in modern bathrooms and showers too.

    Summary in 30 seconds

    • Tadelakt comes from Marrakech and is one of the symbols of the “Red City”.
    • Its name is linked to a verb meaning “to rub” or “to massage”, because it is polished by hand for hours.
    • The base material is Marrakech lime, a regional lime with semi-hydraulic behaviour.
    • It is burnished with a smooth stone and sealed with black soap, which reacts with the lime and makes it water-repellent.
    • It contains no plastics or solvents: it is a mineral, breathable, seamless finish.
    • It is used in bathrooms, showers, basins, bathtubs, walls, fountains and hammams.
    • It takes natural pigments: ochre, red, sand, white, green, blue or black.
    • In Marrakech you can see it in riads, hammams and palaces, and even join a workshop with a maalem.

    What is tadelakt?

    Tadelakt is a lime plaster applied in layers, compacted with a trowel and polished with a stone until it shines. Finally, the craftsman rubs it with black soap. This step closes the surface pores and makes it resistant to water.

    At first glance it looks like polished stone or ceramic. However, when you touch it, you feel a soft, almost silky texture with gentle undulations. Those irregularities are precisely the signature of handwork.

    In addition, tadelakt has no grout lines. A whole shower can be one continuous surface, from floor to ceiling. That is why architects and interior designers around the world love it.

    In Morocco it is not a trend but a living tradition. You will spot it all over the old town of Marrakech, from public baths to luxury riads.

    The name: what does “tadelakt” mean?

    The word tadelakt is associated with the Arabic verb “dalaka”, meaning to rub, massage or knead. The “ta-…-t” form is also typical of Amazigh (Berber), the language of the Atlas peoples. So the name itself reflects Morocco’s blend of cultures.

    The meaning makes perfect sense. Making tadelakt literally means rubbing a wall for hours with a stone. It is slow, physical and almost meditative work. If this mix of languages interests you, read our guide to the languages spoken in Morocco.

    The origins of tadelakt: from cisterns to palaces

    A Marrakech know-how

    Tadelakt developed around Marrakech, where a lime with very special properties is found. Craftsmen used it to line cisterns, tanks and water channels. Water flowing down from the Atlas needed watertight containers, and tadelakt offered a local, durable solution.

    Over time, the technique moved into hammams. There, steam and humidity never stop. Therefore a surface was needed that could handle hot water without breaking down. Tadelakt did the job better than any other material available.

    From hammam to palace

    Later, wealthy families brought it into their homes. Palaces and riads used tadelakt in private baths, courtyard fountains and decorative walls. It was often combined with zellige and carved stucco, the other great arts of Moroccan architecture.

    If you visit a traditional hammam, look at the walls. They are very likely covered in tadelakt. We explain the experience in our guide to the Moroccan hammam.

    The late 20th-century revival

    For much of the 20th century, cement and industrial tiles replaced many traditional techniques. As a result, tadelakt almost disappeared as a craft.

    From the 1980s and 1990s, however, a new wave of interest arrived. Architects restoring riads in the medina revived the material and brought it into contemporary design. Soon tadelakt was appearing in interior magazines across Europe and North America. Today it is applied in hotels, spas and homes worldwide.

    Lime plaster traditions around the world

    Lime is one of humanity’s oldest building materials. The Romans used lime mortars in aqueducts and baths. In Britain, lime plaster and limewash protected cottages and churches for centuries, and conservation builders still use them on listed buildings today. Italy developed Venetian plaster, and Greece and southern Spain whitewash entire villages every year.

    What makes Marrakech unique is the finish. Stone burnishing and black soap turn the lime into a glossy, water-repellent skin. By contrast, a British limewash is matt and porous, and Venetian plaster is usually finished with wax. In short, they are cousins, not twins.

    For a wider view of the country’s history and influences, read our pillar guide to Morocco.

    Marrakech lime: the secret of the material

    Authentic tadelakt is made with lime from the Marrakech region. It is produced by burning local limestone in traditional kilns. During firing, not all the stone transforms evenly. So the lime keeps small unburnt particles and other minerals.

    Thanks to this composition, Marrakech lime behaves in an intermediate way. On the one hand, it hardens in air, like an air lime. On the other, it has some hydraulic capacity, meaning it can set in the presence of moisture. This combination makes it ideal for wet areas.

    The “Red City”: lime, earth and colour in Marrakech

    Marrakech is known as the Red City. Its rammed-earth ramparts, mosques and many of its houses glow in shades from pink to ochre. The colour comes from the earth of the Haouz plain, used to build the Almoravid and Almohad walls.

    Local authorities have also protected this visual identity for decades. That is why even new buildings in the city are usually painted in pink or ochre tones. The result is a city of great colour harmony that shifts with the daylight.

    Lime finishes are part of this look. On façades, courtyards and fountains, lime protects the earth from rain and reflects the heat. Consequently, the Marrakech cityscape makes more sense once you know its materials. To explore its districts, gates and monuments, see our guide to Marrakech.

    How tadelakt is made, step by step

    The traditional process takes experience. A maalem (master craftsman) may need years to master it. These are the main steps:

    1. Prepare the substrate

    The wall must be sound, clean and slightly rough. Traditional work goes on brick, stone or a lime mortar. The substrate is also dampened so it does not suck water out of the lime too quickly.

    2. Mix the lime

    Marrakech lime is sieved and mixed with water. If colour is wanted, mineral pigments are added at this stage. The mix is then left to rest so it hydrates properly.

    3. Apply the coats

    Usually two thin coats go on with a trowel. The first grips the substrate and the second builds the final thickness. Between them, the craftsman waits for the lime to start firming, without letting it dry completely.

    4. Compact

    When the surface is firm but still damp, it is compacted with a plastic or metal trowel. This step closes the pores and starts to bring up a sheen.

    5. Burnish with a stone

    This is the signature step. The craftsman rubs the surface with a smooth, hard stone in small circles for hours. The pressure aligns the lime particles and creates tadelakt’s typical satin glow.

    6. Seal with black soap

    Finally, diluted black soap is applied and the surface is polished again with the stone. The soap, made with olive oil, reacts with the lime to form a water-insoluble compound. Thanks to this reaction, the surface becomes water-repellent.

    7. Curing

    Tadelakt keeps hardening for weeks. So it is wise to wait before using a new shower heavily. The craftsman will advise on the exact time depending on climate and thickness.

    ToolWhat it does
    Steel trowelApplies and smooths the lime coats
    Flexible plastic trowelCompacts the surface without marking it
    Polishing stoneRubs in circles to create shine and close pores
    Black soap (savon beldi)Seals the surface and makes it water-repellent

    Many craftsmen keep their polishing stones for years. With use, the stone adapts to each maalem’s hand and movement. That is why some treat them almost as a personal possession.

    The maalem’s craft: how the know-how is passed on

    In Morocco, traditional crafts are learned mainly in the workshop. A young apprentice starts by helping the master: preparing lime, cleaning tools and watching. Only after a long time is he allowed to polish a whole wall.

    This practice-based apprenticeship repeats across other crafts: zellige, woodwork, leather and metal. Therefore the maalem is not just a technician but also a carrier of culture.

    In recent decades, schools and training centres for traditional arts have also appeared. Their goal is to protect these skills from industrial construction. Even so, the family workshop remains the main school.

    Finally, many Moroccan craftsmen now also work in Europe and the Gulf. Their experience is highly valued in hotel, spa and luxury home projects.

    Black soap: the ingredient that changes everything

    Moroccan black soap, called “savon beldi”, is made from olive oil and macerated olives. It has a paste-like texture and a dark brown colour. In Morocco it is mainly used in the hammam, to prepare the skin before scrubbing with the kessa glove.

    In tadelakt it plays another role. When applied to fresh lime, the fatty acids in the oil react with the calcium. As a result, a thin layer of calcium soap forms, which does not dissolve in water. This layer is what makes water droplets bead and run off the surface.

    Interestingly, it is the same soap travellers buy in the souks as a souvenir. So when you see it on a spice stall, remember that it is also a building material.

    Where tadelakt is used

    Tadelakt bathrooms and tadelakt showers

    This is the most popular use today. A tadelakt shower has no grout lines where dirt can build up. It also adapts to curves, niches and built-in benches. That is why it is a classic in riads and boutique hotels.

    Basins and bathtubs

    Craftsmen can shape basins, worktops and entire bathtubs in tadelakt. The result is a one-off piece with no visible joins.

    Hammams

    The hammam is tadelakt’s natural habitat. It withstands steam, hot water and temperature changes.

    Walls and ceilings

    In living rooms and bedrooms it is used as a decorative finish. Its soft sheen reflects light warmly.

    Fountains, pools and exteriors

    In courtyards and gardens it lines fountains and small ponds. It is also used on exterior walls, although there it needs more maintenance.

    Decorative objects

    Today you can also find tadelakt vases, lamps and bowls. They make an original, lightweight souvenir.

    Authentic Moroccan tadelakt versus imitations

    International success has also brought plenty of imitations. Today you can buy paints, resins and microcements with a “tadelakt effect”. They can be useful in some cases, but they are not the same thing.

    How to spot the real thing

    • Composition: authentic Moroccan tadelakt is lime, water, mineral pigment and black soap. Nothing else.
    • Feel: cool, mineral and silky. Imitations often feel plasticky.
    • Sheen: satin and deep, with small variations. A resin shines more evenly.
    • Smell: lime does not smell of solvent.
    • Repair: the real thing can be touched up with lime and soap; a resin cannot always be.

    When an imitation makes sense

    If a wall takes constant knocks, or there are no trained craftsmen nearby, an industrial product may be more practical. Even so, know what you are buying and do not pay craft prices for a factory finish.

    In Morocco, the best approach is to see the work in person. That is why many interior designers travel to Marrakech to choose colours and craftsmen before a project. A private driver and local guide make workshop and supplier visits much easier. You can pair them with an Ait Ben Haddou day trip to see the earth architecture of the south.

    Tadelakt colours

    The natural colour of Marrakech lime is off-white or pale grey. However, it can be tinted with mineral pigments. The most common tones are:

    • Marrakech red and pink: the colour of the Red City, widely used on façades.
    • Ochre and sand: recalling the kasbahs of the south and the desert.
    • White and cream: the brightest choice for small bathrooms.
    • Green and blue: common in hammams and fountains.
    • Black and charcoal: very fashionable in contemporary design.

    Very bright colours are hard to achieve with natural pigments. The final tone also shifts slightly with polishing and soap. So every wall is unique.

    How to care for a tadelakt surface

    • Clean with water and a neutral soap or diluted black soap.
    • Avoid bleach, vinegar, descalers and acidic products.
    • Do not use abrasive scourers.
    • Wipe away standing water, especially at first.
    • Once or twice a year, apply black soap or a dedicated wax to boost protection.
    • If a small crack appears, ask a craftsman: many can be repaired.

    Where to see tadelakt in Morocco

    In Marrakech

    Marrakech is the capital of tadelakt. You will see it in many restored medina riads, traditional hammams and hotel spas. Some historic palaces also combine it with zellige and stucco. A great way to discover it is to stay in a riad with a tadelakt bathroom.

    To plan your stay, see our guides to Marrakech holidays and the restaurant Marrakech guide.

    In Fes

    Fes is dominated by zellige and ceramics, but you will also find tadelakt in riad bathrooms and hammams. Discover the city in our guide to Fes, Morocco.

    In the south and the desert

    Kasbahs in the south and some luxury Sahara camps use tadelakt in bathrooms and basins. It suits earth architecture perfectly. You will see it, for example, in many stays on our 3-day desert tour from Marrakech.

    Tadelakt course in Marrakech: workshops for travellers

    More and more travellers want to learn the technique. Marrakech has introductory workshops lasting a few hours or a full day, usually with a local maalem. Some riads also organise demonstrations for their guests.

    What you learn

    • How to recognise Marrakech lime and prepare the mix.
    • How to apply the coats with a trowel.
    • How to burnish with a stone and seal with black soap.
    • How to make a small object, such as a bowl or plaque, to take home.

    Tips

    • Book ahead in high season.
    • Wear clothes you do not mind getting dirty.
    • Ask whether your piece will be dry in time for your flight.

    Other Moroccan crafts you will see alongside tadelakt

    Tadelakt belongs to a family of crafts that shape traditional architecture. Knowing them makes your visit much richer.

    • Zellige: mosaic of hand-cut ceramic pieces, typical of Fes.
    • Gebs: carved plaster with geometric and floral motifs.
    • Cedar wood: carved and painted ceilings and doors.
    • Pierced metalwork: brass and copper lamps with perforated patterns.
    • Amazigh rugs: woven in the Atlas with wool and traditional symbols.

    In Marrakech and the Atlas villages there are cooperatives where you can watch artisans at work. We explain how to visit them in our guide to SIM cards and artisan factories in Morocco. To learn about textiles, read our guide to traditional Moroccan clothing.

    Buying tadelakt pieces in the souks

    In the Marrakech souks you will find tadelakt bowls, soap dishes, vases and lamps. They are light and easy to pack. However, it helps to know how to judge quality.

    • Feel: real tadelakt is smooth and slightly wavy, not plastic.
    • Sheen: satin, not glossy like varnish.
    • Weight: pieces have some heft because of the lime.
    • Price: bargain politely and compare several stalls. See our guide to the Moroccan dirham.

    Also buy a little black soap. It will help you maintain your pieces and enjoy your own hammam at home.

    Tadelakt and sustainability

    In recent years tadelakt has attracted interest for ecological reasons. It is a mineral material with no plastics or synthetic resins. It also lets walls breathe, which helps manage moisture in older buildings.

    Lime, moreover, reabsorbs CO2 from the air as it hardens, a process called carbonation. Even so, firing limestone uses energy. So tadelakt is not “zero emissions”, but it is a natural, durable alternative to many industrial finishes.

    Finally, choosing tadelakt supports a traditional craft. Every commission keeps the know-how of Marrakech’s maalems alive.

    What affects the price of a tadelakt finish

    There is no single price. The final cost varies widely with country, craftsman and project. These are the main factors:

    • Area: total square metres and wall height.
    • Shape: curves, niches, benches and basins take more hours.
    • Substrate: whether the wall needs preparing or reinforcing first.
    • Colour: some pigments cost more or are harder to match.
    • Location: labour is cheaper in Morocco than in the UK or the USA.
    • Experience: a renowned maalem charges more, but the result is more reliable.

    So the sensible approach is to get several quotes and see the craftsman’s finished work. Also ask about guarantees and included maintenance.

    Tadelakt in hotels, spas and luxury design

    Many high-end hotels in Morocco and abroad now choose polished lime. They want a warm, natural, seamless look that feels calm. It also fits the “wabi-sabi” design trend, which values imperfection and honest materials.

    In Marrakech, the spas of the grand hotels combine lime walls, zellige and cedar wood. The result is a very Moroccan sensory experience. If you travel on a higher budget, asking for a hotel or riad with these finishes is a simple way to live local craftsmanship.

    Interior designers also use it in homes worldwide. So a technique born to waterproof cisterns is now a symbol of quiet luxury.

    Tadelakt bathroom ideas inspired by Marrakech riads

    Riad bathrooms are a great source of inspiration for a home project. These ideas translate well to European and American homes:

    • The walk-in shower with a bench: a built-in seat moulded in the same material as the walls, so the whole space reads as one surface.
    • The arched niche: a horseshoe or pointed arch recessed into the wall for soaps and candles.
    • The sculpted basin: a basin shaped directly on the worktop, with no visible join.
    • The two-tone room: a darker lower band and a lighter upper wall, a nod to traditional dados.
    • Brass and cedar accents: pierced brass lamps and a cedar mirror frame warm up the mineral walls.
    • Natural light: the satin finish glows best in soft daylight, so keep windows uncovered where possible.

    Before copying a design, however, remember that riads were built with thick masonry walls. In lighter modern buildings, the substrate needs careful preparation.

    Questions to ask a tadelakt installer

    Whether you hire a craftsman in Marrakech or at home, these questions help you choose well:

    • Which lime do you use, and is it genuine Marrakech lime?
    • Do you finish with black soap or with a synthetic sealer?
    • Can I see finished bathrooms or showers you have done, ideally a few years old?
    • How will you prepare my walls and floor?
    • How long must the shower cure before daily use?
    • What maintenance do you recommend, and do you offer touch-ups?
    • What happens if a crack appears in the first year?

    A confident craftsman will answer clearly and show you real projects. In contrast, vague answers about materials are a warning sign. Taking the time to ask these questions protects both your budget and the final result.

    Can you have tadelakt outside Morocco?

    Yes, and it is increasingly common in the UK, the USA and across Europe. However, keep several points in mind:

    • Material: Marrakech lime is exported and sold by specialist suppliers. Ready-mixed products that mimic it also exist.
    • Climate: in cold or very damp places, drying is slower and needs more care.
    • Substrate: plasterboard walls need special preparation.
    • Craftsman: look for someone with visible work and references. Burnishing cannot be improvised.

    It is also wise to see a sample before choosing a colour. The tone changes considerably once polished and sealed.

    The hammam: the best place to feel polished lime

    To truly understand this material, nothing beats a hammam. Warm, smooth walls, steam and soft light create a unique atmosphere.

    Public hammam or spa

    The neighbourhood hammam is simple, cheap and very local. Men and women use separate rooms or hours. The hotel or riad spa, by contrast, is more comfortable, private and expensive. Both are worth trying.

    What it includes

    • A steam bath to open the pores.
    • Black soap on the skin.
    • A scrub with the kessa glove.
    • Sometimes a ghassoul clay mask and an argan oil massage.

    Bring swimwear or spare underwear, flip-flops and a towel for a public hammam.

    Staying in a riad with a tadelakt bathroom

    A riad is a traditional house built around a courtyard with a garden or fountain. Many have been restored as small guesthouses. So they are the best way to experience Moroccan architecture from the inside.

    In restored riads, bathrooms often have polished-lime walls and showers. Many also have a rooftop terrace, a private hammam and a Moroccan breakfast.

    How to choose a good riad

    • Check it is close to a medina gate reachable by car.
    • Ask whether they offer airport transfers.
    • Ask for photos of the bathrooms: they reveal a lot about how the house is cared for.
    • In summer, confirm it has air conditioning.

    On our private trips we select riads with character, many of them with these traditional finishes.

    When to visit Marrakech for workshops

    Workshops run all year. However, spring and autumn are the best seasons for weather. In summer the intense heat makes the work harder and some craftsmen shorten their hours. During Ramadan, many workshops also close earlier.

    Since 20 September 2026, Morocco has used permanent GMT. As a result, it gets dark earlier on the clock in autumn and winter. Plan workshop visits for the morning. See our guide to the best time to visit Morocco.

    Common mistakes with tadelakt

    • Confusing it with microcement: microcement contains resins; tadelakt is pure lime.
    • Using acidic cleaners: they damage the soap layer and the lime.
    • Using a new shower too soon: the lime needs weeks to harden.
    • Applying it on an unstable substrate: cracks will appear.
    • Choosing a craftsman on price alone: experience makes the difference.
    • Buying imitation “tadelakt”: some products are just effect paint.

    Frequently asked questions about tadelakt

    What is tadelakt?

    It is a traditional Marrakech finish made from local lime, polished with a stone and sealed with black soap. It creates a smooth, glowing, water-resistant surface.

    Is tadelakt waterproof?

    It is highly water-resistant thanks to the reaction between lime and black soap. That is why it is used in showers and hammams. It still needs simple maintenance.

    Where does tadelakt come from?

    It comes from Marrakech and its region, where the traditional lime used to make it is produced.

    What does the word tadelakt mean?

    It is linked to the Arabic verb “dalaka”, meaning to rub or massage, with a form typical of Amazigh.

    What is the difference between tadelakt and microcement?

    Microcement is an industrial product with resins. Tadelakt is a breathable mineral lime finish applied by hand.

    How do you clean tadelakt?

    With water and neutral soap or diluted black soap. Avoid bleach, vinegar and descalers.

    Can you make a whole bathroom in tadelakt?

    Yes. Walls, floor, shower, basin and bathtub can all be made in tadelakt, without joints.

    Where can you see tadelakt in Marrakech?

    In restored medina riads, traditional hammams, hotel spas and some historic palaces.

    Can you learn tadelakt in Marrakech?

    Yes. There are introductory workshops of a few hours or a full day with local craftsmen.

    How long does tadelakt last?

    Well applied and cared for, it can last for decades. Old hammams are the best proof.

    Conclusion: tadelakt, the skin of Marrakech

    Tadelakt is much more than a wall finish. Indeed, it sums up the ingenuity of Moroccan craftsmen: a local material, a patient technique and a result that is both beautiful and practical. Moreover, every wall tells the story of the hands that polished it.
