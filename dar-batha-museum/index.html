    Dar Batha Museum: 2026 Guide (Hours, Tickets, Highlights)

    2026 guide to the Dar Batha Museum in Fes: opening hours, 60 MAD tickets, must-see pieces, how to get there and the new Jewish Culture Museum.

    Favorites Morocco Tours · Updated: 29 September 2026

    The Dar Batha Museum is one of the best-kept secrets of Fes. For almost a century it was a modest crafts museum that most travellers walked past. Then, after six years of restoration, the old palace reopened in February 2025 with a new name, a new story and a collection that had never been shown this way. This 2026 guide covers what to see inside, ticket prices, opening hours, how to get there from Bab Boujloud and how to fit the visit into a wider Morocco trip.

    Quick answer: the Dar Batha Museum, now officially the Al Batha Museum of Islamic Arts, sits in a 19th-century palace five minutes from Bab Boujloud in the Fes medina. It opens from 10:00 to 18:00 and closes on Tuesdays. Adult tickets cost 60 dirhams (about US$6.30 or £4.70). Allow 90 minutes to two hours, including the Andalusian garden.

    What the Dar Batha Museum is today

    Many guidebooks still call it a “museum of Moroccan arts and traditions”. That label is now out of date. From 1915 until 2019, Dar Batha mostly displayed pottery, embroidery and everyday objects from Fes. It was charming but basic. In addition, labels were sparse and the rooms were dim.

    By contrast, the museum that reopened in 2025 tells a complete story. The new route explains how the first Islamic state in Morocco was born, how Arabic calligraphy evolved and what each dynasty left in the city. That is why its new name is the Museum of Islamic Arts. Still, the crafts have not gone anywhere; they now sit inside a clear historical narrative.

    A museum with a message

    At the opening ceremony, Mehdi Qotbi, president of the National Museums Foundation, presented the museum as a place of tolerance. He spoke about the long coexistence of Muslims, Jews and Christians in Morocco. Meanwhile, curator Alaa Fechtali explained that the restoration respected the original architecture. You can feel this balance in every room: modern display cases inside a palace that keeps its soul.

    Why it matters for travellers

    In practice, the Dar Batha Museum is the perfect introduction to the medina. If you go on your first morning, you will understand far more of what you see later at the Bou Inania Madrasa, the Qarawiyyin or the souks. In fact, many objects come from those very mosques and madrasas. Think of it as reading the preface before you open the book.

    History of the Dar Batha palace

    The building is as interesting as the collection. So, to make sense of it, it helps to look at its history in four stages. You will see how a royal summer palace became Morocco’s oldest museum.

    A 19th-century summer palace

    Sultan Moulay Hassan I of the Alaouite dynasty ordered the palace in the late 19th century. His successor, Moulay Abdelaziz, finished and decorated it. The complex served as a summer residence and as a place to receive important guests. Next door stands Dar el Beida, another palace from the same complex. Dar el Beida is not open to visitors.

    The Protectorate and the first museum

    The French Protectorate began in 1912. Soon after, the palaces housed administrative offices. However, in 1915 Dar Batha became a museum. That is why Moroccan media often describe it as the oldest museum in the country. For decades it showed the arts and traditions of Fes and its region.

    Restoration from 2019 to 2025

    On 15 April 2019, King Mohammed VI launched the restoration. On the same day, work also began on the new Museum of Jewish Culture in the Mellah. The National Museums Foundation supervised the project together with ADER-Fès, the agency in charge of rehabilitating the medina. The work ran longer than planned, so the museum stayed closed for almost six years.

    The reopening on 26 February 2025

    Finally, the museum reopened on Wednesday 26 February 2025. Regional officials and cultural figures attended, including two Nobel physics laureates, Alain Aspect and Serge Haroche. As Médias24 reported, the reopening ended six years of work. By the end of April 2025, the museum had already welcomed more than 10,000 visitors, according to the Moroccan press. For a place that used to be overlooked, that was a big change.

    What changed with the reopening

    If you have read old reviews, forget them. The Dar Batha Museum of 2026 looks nothing like the one of 2018. Here are the main changes:

    • New concept: it moved from an ethnographic museum to a museum of Islamic arts.
    • Sharper selection: about 700 pieces are on display from a collection of over 6,500.
    • Ten themed sections: each one tackles a subject, from calligraphy to science.
    • Labels in three languages: Arabic, French and English, clearly written.
    • Museum-grade lighting: darker rooms with well-lit objects, like major European museums.
    • Craft videos: screens show today’s masters carving wood, plaster and zellige.
    • Updated price: the ticket rose from 10 to 60 dirhams, which makes sense after such an investment.

    In other words, the museum has gone from a quick stop to a highlight of Fes. Many travellers who planned 30 minutes end up staying for two hours, mostly because the garden invites you to slow down. For a first visit to Morocco, that pause is precious: it gives you time to absorb the medina before diving back into its lanes.

    Therefore, if you find online information quoting “10 DH” or split morning and afternoon hours, it dates from before 2019.

    Architecture of the Dar Batha Museum

    Even if you come for the collection, save time for the building. The palace follows the Hispano-Moorish style, with a large rectangular courtyard in the centre and two main wings to the east and west.

    The courtyard and galleries

    The side galleries feature brick horseshoe arches. On the north and south sides, by contrast, you will see tall painted wooden doors. The courtyard floor is covered in zellige, the mosaic of small glazed tiles that Fes is famous for. Also, several marble and tile fountains keep the air cool.

    Painted ceilings and carved plaster

    Look up in every room. In fact, the painted cedar ceilings are among the palace’s greatest treasures. Likewise, the walls mix carved stucco with tiled dados. If Moroccan decoration fascinates you, compare these surfaces with tadelakt, the polished lime plaster you will see in riads and hammams.

    The Andalusian garden

    The garden is the real surprise. It covers more than half the site and follows the classic riad plan: two paths divide it into four parts, with a fountain in the middle. Its 1915 layout is credited to the French landscape architect Jean-Claude Nicolas Forestier. Besides palms, jacarandas and hibiscus, there is a huge holm oak that shades an entire corner.

    In spring the garden blooms and fills with birdsong. On the other hand, it has long hosted concerts during the Fes Festival of World Sacred Music, held every May. If you travel then, check the programme.

    Must-see pieces at the Dar Batha Museum

    With 700 objects, it is easy to feel overwhelmed. So here is a short list of highlights. Head straight to them if you are short on time.

    1. The Idrisid wooden beams

    Among the oldest pieces in the Dar Batha Museum are carved timbers from the Idrisid period. The Idrisids founded Fes at the end of the 8th century. Seeing woodwork from that era, more than a thousand years later, is moving. These pieces also explain the roots of the Fassi carpentry that artisans still practise in the medina today.

    2. The minbar of the Andalusian Mosque

    This is the star of the museum. A minbar is the stepped pulpit from which the imam preaches on Fridays. This one comes from the Andalusian Mosque, founded in the 9th century on the east bank of the Fes river. Its panels date from the late 10th century. Very few wooden objects of that age survive anywhere in the Islamic world, so give it a few minutes.

    3. The minbar of the Bou Inania Madrasa

    The museum also holds a 14th-century minbar from the Bou Inania Madrasa. This Marinid madrasa is one of the finest in Morocco and lies only a few minutes away on foot. That is why we suggest seeing both on the same day: first the minbar in the museum, then the original building.

    4. Fes blue pottery

    The ceramics collection is one of the most complete in the Arab world. Cobalt blue on a white glaze is the signature of Fes potters. In the display cases you can follow how shapes and patterns changed over the centuries. Later, if you visit the potteries at Ain Nokbi, you will recognise the same designs.

    5. Qurans, calligraphy and manuscripts

    A whole section traces the history of Arabic script. There are old Qurans, illuminated pages and examples of the Maghrebi hand, which stands out from eastern scripts with its wide, rounded curves. To understand the role of faith in the city, read our guide to religion in Morocco.

    6. Astrolabes and scientific instruments

    For centuries, Fes was a great centre of learning. Astrolabes measured the position of the stars, set prayer times and found the direction of Mecca. Seeing them up close helps you understand why the Qarawiyyin drew students from across the Mediterranean.

    7. Amazigh rugs, jewellery and costumes

    The ethnographic side did not disappear. Instead, it became part of the new story. You will find rugs from Middle Atlas tribes, silver jewellery and ceremonial dress. For more on this, pair your visit with our guide to traditional Moroccan clothing and our article on Berber Morocco.

    8. Musical instruments

    Lutes, frame drums and other instruments remind you that Fes is the home of Andalusian music in Morocco. They also link nicely to the concerts held in the garden itself.

    A 90-minute route through the museum

    You explore the Dar Batha Museum clockwise around the courtyard. Here is a simple plan to make the most of your time without tiring out:

    1. Minutes 0 to 10: buy your ticket, pause in the courtyard and find the toilets.
    2. Minutes 10 to 25: the timeline room, covering the Idrisid, Almoravid, Almohad, Marinid, Saadian and Alaouite dynasties.
    3. Minutes 25 to 45: woodwork, minbars and ancient beams, the heart of the museum.
    4. Minutes 45 to 60: calligraphy, Qurans and science.
    5. Minutes 60 to 75: ceramics, zellige and the arts of fire.
    6. Minutes 75 to 90: textiles, jewellery, music and, finally, a break under the garden’s oak.

    If you like to read every label, allow two and a half hours. With young children, however, one hour is usually enough.

    Morocco’s dynasties in five minutes

    The first room of the museum is a timeline. For that reason, it pays to arrive with a basic idea of the dynasties. You will get far more out of the visit.

    Idrisids (788-974)

    Idris I arrived from the East and founded Morocco’s first Islamic state. His son, Idris II, made Fes the capital early in the 9th century. The carved beams in the museum belong to this founding era.

    Almoravids and Almohads (11th-13th centuries)

    These two Amazigh dynasties built empires that reached as far as al-Andalus in Spain. Under their rule, Marrakech grew in political weight. Even so, Fes stayed the great religious and craft centre.

    Marinids (13th-15th centuries)

    This was the golden age of Fes. The Marinids built most of the madrasas you admire today, including Bou Inania. Much of the woodwork and zellige in the museum comes from this period.

    Saadians and Alaouites (16th century to today)

    The Saadians ruled from Marrakech. Then came the Alaouites, the dynasty that still reigns. In fact, the Dar Batha palace itself is an Alaouite building, so the house closes the circle of the story it tells.

    Watch out for the time change

    Since 20 September 2026, Morocco has returned to GMT permanently. In practice, it gets dark an hour earlier than many travellers expect. Because the museum closes at 18:00, in autumn and winter it is best to go in the morning or early afternoon. See our article on what time it is in Morocco now for details.

    How to get to the Dar Batha Museum

    The museum stands at the western edge of the medina, on flat ground that is easy to find. It is also one of the few major sights in Fes you can reach without getting lost.

    From Bab Boujloud

    Bab Boujloud, the famous Blue Gate, is your landmark. From there, walk south across the square and follow the signs to Place du Batha. It takes about five minutes. If you are staying in a riad, ask the staff to mark the route on your map.

    From the new town

    Take a red petit taxi and ask for “Batha”. From Avenue Hassan II the ride usually costs 15 to 25 dirhams on the meter. If the driver will not use the meter, agree on the fare before you get in.

    From Fes railway station

    The station is in the new town. From there, a taxi takes 10 to 15 minutes depending on traffic. If you are arriving by train from Tangier, Rabat or Casablanca, check times and tickets on the official ONCF website.

    By car or with a private driver

    Cars cannot enter the medina, but they can reach the Batha area. If you travel on one of our tours from Fes, your driver can drop you close to the entrance and pick you up afterwards.

    Best time to visit

    Overall, the Dar Batha Museum is quiet. Still, some moments are better than others.

    • At opening (10:00): soft light in the garden and nearly empty rooms.
    • Midday: a cool refuge when the medina is hot and noisy.
    • Spring (March to May): the garden is in bloom and temperatures are pleasant.
    • Autumn (September to November): mild weather and fewer groups than in spring.
    • Summer: Fes often passes 35 °C, so the museum becomes a shady oasis.

    To choose your season, read our guide to the best time to visit Morocco.

    Visiting with children, older travellers or limited mobility

    The Dar Batha Museum is one of the easiest sights in the medina. It sits on flat ground near a street where taxis stop. In addition, most of the route is on the ground floor, around the courtyard.

    Even so, there are a few steps between rooms and the garden paths can be uneven. So if you use a wheelchair, ask at the desk about the easiest access. For older travellers, the garden offers benches and shade, which are hard to find elsewhere in Fes.

    With kids, keep it concrete. Try a small treasure hunt: find an astrolabe, a minbar and a blue pot. Then reward them with time in the garden. That way the visit feels like an adventure, not a history lesson.

    Photography tips

    The palace is very photogenic, but please respect the rules. These tips will help you get good shots without disturbing anyone:

    • Use your phone and turn off the flash, which damages old objects.
    • Shoot the courtyard arches from the galleries to frame the garden.
    • Capture the painted ceilings in portrait mode.
    • In the morning, soft light makes the zellige colours pop.
    • Do not photograph staff or other visitors without asking.

    Looking for more photogenic places in the north? The Blue City is about four hours away by road. Read our guide to Chefchaouen.

    Getting to Fes from the UK, US and Canada

    Most visitors reach the Dar Batha Museum as part of a wider trip. So here is a quick overview of how to get to Fes in the first place.

    From the UK and Europe

    Fes-Saïss Airport has direct flights from several European cities, often with low-cost airlines. Schedules change by season, so check current routes before you book. Alternatively, fly into Marrakech, Casablanca or Tangier and continue overland. If you are comparing airports, our guide to flights to Morocco will help.

    From the US and Canada

    Royal Air Maroc flies non-stop from North America to Casablanca. From there, the train to Fes takes a few hours, or a private driver can collect you at the airport. Many travellers also connect through a European hub. In addition, remember that US, UK and Canadian passport holders can usually visit Morocco visa-free for up to 90 days, although you should always confirm the current rules before travelling.

    From elsewhere in Morocco

    Trains link Fes with Tangier, Rabat, Casablanca and Marrakech. Buses and private transfers cover the rest. For the desert, however, a private tour is by far the most comfortable option, because it combines the drive, the camel trek and the camp in one plan.

    What to see nearby: a Fes museum walk

    The museum is an ideal starting point for a full morning. All the places below are within a 20-minute walk.

    Bab Boujloud and Talaa Kebira

    The Blue Gate was built in 1913 and opens onto the medina’s main shopping street. From the outside it is blue, the colour of Fes; on the inside it is green, the colour of Islam. From here the street runs downhill into the heart of Fes el Bali.

    Bou Inania Madrasa

    It is about five minutes from Bab Boujloud. This 14th-century madrasa shows zellige, plaster and cedar of astonishing quality. If you saw its minbar in the museum, you will now understand where it came from.

    Nejjarine Museum of Wooden Arts

    It occupies a former funduq, or merchants’ inn. It pairs well with the Dar Batha Museum because it focuses on carpentry. Its rooftop also offers good views over the medina.

    The Qarawiyyin and the tanneries

    Fatima al-Fihri founded the Qarawiyyin in 859. Non-Muslims cannot enter the prayer hall, although you can glimpse the courtyard from some doors. Afterwards, continue to the Chouara tannery. For more ideas, see our guide on what to do in Fez.

    New in 2026: the Museum of Moroccan Jewish Culture

    This is the big cultural news of the autumn. The Museum of Moroccan Jewish Culture opens on Monday 12 October 2026 in the Fes Mellah, according to Le360. Work on it started on the same day as the Dar Batha restoration, in April 2019. As a result, the two museums make a natural pair.

    It is presented as the first publicly funded museum entirely devoted to Jewish culture in an Arab-Muslim country. It covers more than 1,700 m², with about 1,200 m² of galleries. The route spans over 2,500 years of Jewish life in Morocco, the creation of the Mellah in the 15th century and the arrival of Jews expelled from Spain and Portugal from 1492 onwards.

    So, if you visit Fes from mid-October 2026, combine the two. Spend the morning at the Dar Batha Museum. Then, in the afternoon, walk through the Mellah, the Jewish cemetery and the new museum.

    Practical tips for your visit

    These small details make the difference between a good visit and a great one. Most of them also apply to other sights in the medina, so they will serve you throughout your stay in Fes.

    • Clothing: there is no strict dress code, but modest clothes are the most comfortable choice in the medina. See what to wear in Morocco.
    • Photos: recent visitors could use phones, but staff asked them to put away DSLR cameras. Never use flash.
    • Toilets: the museum has clean restrooms, a real bonus in the medina.
    • Cash: bring dirhams; do not count on paying by card.
    • Fake guides: unofficial “guides” may approach you near Bab Boujloud. Decline politely.
    • Language: staff usually speak French and English. For more, read about the languages spoken in Morocco.
    • Safety: the Batha area is calm and busy. Still, watch your bag in narrow lanes. More advice in is Morocco safe?

    Common mistakes to avoid

    We have guided many travellers around Fes. These are the mistakes we see most often:

    1. Going on a Tuesday. The museum is closed and half a morning is lost.
    2. Trusting old prices. It no longer costs 10 dirhams, but 60.
    3. Leaving it for late afternoon. With GMT, there is little time left by 17:30.
    4. Skipping the garden. It is half of the experience.
    5. Not linking the museum to the medina. Seeing Bou Inania straight after doubles the interest.
    6. Confusing it with Dar el Beida. That neighbouring palace is not open to visitors.

    Two days in Fes around the Dar Batha Museum

    Fes deserves at least two full days. Here is a short plan to organise your time:

    • Day 1, morning: Dar Batha Museum, Bab Boujloud and Bou Inania Madrasa.
    • Day 1, afternoon: Talaa Kebira, Nejjarine, the Qarawiyyin and the Chouara tannery.
    • Day 2, morning: Fes el Jdid, the Mellah and the Royal Palace gates (exterior only).
    • Day 2, afternoon: Marinid Tombs at sunset and dinner in the medina.

    The museum and the UNESCO medina

    The Medina of Fez has been on the UNESCO World Heritage List since 1981. The Dar Batha Museum sits inside this historic ensemble. Its restoration is part of a wider programme to rehabilitate the medina, with fountains, madrasas and funduqs brought back to life in recent years. You can read more background on the palace on Wikipedia.

    In short, visiting the museum also supports that effort. Every ticket helps keep alive a heritage that belongs to everyone.

    Dar Batha Museum FAQ

    Is the Dar Batha Museum open in 2026?

    Yes. It reopened on 26 February 2025 and runs normally in 2026, from 10:00 to 18:00, every day except Tuesday.

    How much is the Dar Batha Museum entrance fee?

    An adult ticket costs 60 dirhams, about US$6.30 or £4.70. Pay in cash and keep your ticket during the visit.

    What day is the museum closed?

    The museum closes on Tuesdays. On other days it opens from 10:00 to 18:00, although hours may change during Ramadan.

    How long do you need at the Dar Batha Museum?

    Plan 90 minutes to two hours. If you read every panel and relax in the garden, allow up to two and a half hours.

    Are Dar Batha and the Batha Museum the same?

    Yes. Dar Batha is the name of the palace. The museum inside is now called the Al Batha Museum of Islamic Arts.

    Can you take photos inside?

    Recent visitors could use phones without flash. However, staff asked people to put away DSLR cameras. Check at the desk in case the rule changes.

    Where is the Dar Batha Museum?

    It is on Place du Batha, at the western edge of the Fes el Bali medina, about five minutes on foot from Bab Boujloud.

    Is the Dar Batha Museum worth visiting?

    Yes, especially since the reopening. The palace, the Andalusian garden and pieces like the Andalusian Mosque minbar justify the visit.

    Is it good for kids?

    Yes, for short visits. The garden gives children room to rest, and the craft videos usually hold their attention.

    Do you need a guide?

    No, because labels are in Arabic, French and English. Still, a licensed guide adds a lot of historical context.

    What is the most important piece in the museum?

    The minbar of the Andalusian Mosque, with panels from the late 10th century, is one of the great wooden treasures of the Islamic world.

    What other museums are in Fes?

    Highlights include the Nejjarine Museum of Wooden Arts and the new Museum of Moroccan Jewish Culture, opening on 12 October 2026.

    How many days should I spend in Fes?

    Two full days is ideal. That gives you time for the Dar Batha Museum, the medina, the Mellah and the Marinid Tombs without rushing.

    Can I combine the museum with a hammam?

    Yes. Many travellers see the museum in the morning, eat lunch near Bab Boujloud and end the day in a hammam. Read our guide to the Moroccan hammam.

    Key takeaways

    • The Dar Batha Museum reopened on 26 February 2025 as the Museum of Islamic Arts.
    • It opens from 10:00 to 18:00, closes on Tuesdays and costs 60 MAD.
    • About 700 pieces are on show, including Idrisid beams and the Andalusian Mosque minbar.
    • The Andalusian garden covers more than half the palace and hosts concerts.
    • It is five minutes from Bab Boujloud and pairs perfectly with the Bou Inania Madrasa.
    • From 12 October 2026, Fes also has a new Museum of Moroccan Jewish Culture in the Mellah.

    Conclusion: a must-see stop in Fes

    The Dar Batha Museum is no longer a minor museum. After its restoration, it has become the best gateway to Moroccan history and a peaceful retreat inside the medina. Moreover, the ticket is affordable and the location is easy to find. Whether you love history, architecture or simply quiet gardens, it deserves a place on your list.
