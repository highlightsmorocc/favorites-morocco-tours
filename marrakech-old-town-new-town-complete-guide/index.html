    Marrakech Old Town and New Town: The Complete Guide

    Discover Marrakech Old Town (Medina) and New Town Explore souks, riads, Jemaa el-Fna, modern Guéliz & best tips for both sides of the city

    Favorites Morocco Tours · Updated: 3 September 2026

    Ask ten travellers where the Marrakech old town begins and you will hear several answers. Some picture the walled medina, its souks and its riads. Others think of Gueliz, where wide avenues and pavement cafés replace donkey carts. Both groups are right, because Marrakech is really two cities stacked side by side.

    They sit barely three kilometres apart, yet roughly eight centuries separate them. This guide draws the line between the two halves and explains what each one offers. You will learn where the ramparts run, what to see on either side, which district suits your trip, and how to cross town without wasting an afternoon.

    What People Actually Mean by the Marrakech Old Town

    Marrakech is a city, not a town. The 2024 Moroccan census counted more than 1.5 million people across the Marrakech prefecture. Even so, the word “town” sticks, because most visitors only ever experience a compact core that genuinely feels town-sized.

    Three meanings, one word

    When guides, drivers and hotel staff talk about town, they usually mean one of three areas:

    • The Marrakech old town — the walled medina, founded around 1070, home to Jemaa el-Fnaa, the souks and most riads.
    • Gueliz and Hivernage — the new town, laid out under the French protectorate after 1912.
    • Jemaa el-Fnaa and around — what people loosely call the town centre.

    Knowing which one someone means saves real confusion. A taxi driver told simply “town” will almost always drive to Jemaa el-Fnaa.

    A quick mental map

    Picture an oval of red earth walls about four kilometres across. Inside sits the Marrakech old town. To the west and north-west, outside the ramparts, lies the new town. The Kasbah and the royal palace grounds fill the south, while the Palmeraie palm grove stretches north-east. On a clear morning the High Atlas closes the southern horizon.

    Our complete Marrakech travel guide covers the wider city, while this article focuses on the split between the two halves.

    Inside the Marrakech Old Town: The Medina

    The medina is why most people come. UNESCO inscribed the Marrakech old town as a World Heritage Site in 1985, and it remains one of the largest inhabited historic quarters in North Africa. Tens of thousands of people still live inside the walls, so this is a working neighbourhood rather than an open-air museum.

    Where the walls run

    The ramparts define the Marrakech old town. Almoravid builders laid them out in the early twelfth century using rammed earth, and craftsmen have repaired them ever since. They stand roughly six to eight metres high, sit between 1.4 and 2 metres thick, and carry a square tower every 25 to 30 metres. Around twenty gates, called babs, pierce them today. Pass through any one of them and you have entered the Marrakech old town.

    Jemaa el-Fnaa, the centre of the centre

    Every walk through the medina eventually returns to Jemaa el-Fnaa. By day the square fills with juice carts, henna artists and musicians. After sunset, food stalls appear with benches, lanterns and clouds of grill smoke. Use the square as your anchor: whenever you lose your bearings, ask for “Jemaa el-Fnaa” and someone will point.

    The souks

    North of the square, a covered maze carries the historic trades. Souk Semmarine handles textiles and souvenirs. Spices and perfume fill Souk el Attarine, while hammered metal rings through Souk Haddadine. Further on, Souk Chouari smells of cedar and thuya shavings. Wandering without a plan works beautifully here, although a saved map pin helps you find the way back.

    The New Town: Gueliz and Hivernage

    Cross the ramparts heading west and the mood changes completely. Streets widen, pavements appear, and glass shopfronts replace market stalls. Many visitors never see this side at all, which is a shame, because it shows how Marrakech actually lives today.

    Gueliz

    French planners drew up Gueliz from 1913 around a central axis, now Avenue Mohammed V, which runs straight from the new town to the Koutoubia. Today the district holds pavement cafés, art galleries, patisseries, bookshops, cinemas and the city’s best contemporary Moroccan design stores.

    Prices are fixed here, which many travellers find restful after a morning of haggling. Furthermore, Gueliz is where you find pharmacies, banks, phone shops and full-size supermarkets.

    Hivernage

    South of Gueliz, Hivernage began life as a garden district and stays leafy and low-rise. Five-star hotels, spas, embassies and most of the city’s nightlife cluster along its quiet streets. It sits about fifteen minutes on foot from the Koutoubia, so it balances calm with access.

    Other modern districts

    • Semlalia — hotels and apartment blocks north of Gueliz, quieter and cheaper.
    • Targa — a spread-out residential suburb popular with long-stay visitors.
    • Sidi Ghanem — the design and manufacturing quarter, about ten kilometres out.
    • Palmeraie — the palm grove, with resorts, villas and golf courses.

    So Where Is the Town Centre?

    Travellers usually ask this while checking how close a hotel sits to the action. The honest answer depends on what you want from the trip.

    If sightseeing comes first, your centre is Jemaa el-Fnaa and the streets within ten minutes of it. Nearly every landmark sits inside that radius: the Koutoubia, the souks, Ben Youssef Madrasa, the Bahia Palace and Le Jardin Secret.

    If cafés, restaurants and shopping matter more, your centre is the junction of Avenue Mohammed V and Boulevard Mohammed Zerktouni in Gueliz. Both answers are legitimate, and the two points sit roughly three kilometres apart.

    Walking between the two halves

    The walk from Jemaa el-Fnaa to central Gueliz follows Avenue Mohammed V past the Koutoubia and the Cyber Park. It takes about thirty minutes at an easy pace, stays completely flat, and feels safe by day and in the evening. In summer, however, do it before ten in the morning or after six.

    The “Blue Town” Question, Answered

    People frequently search for a blue town in Marrakech. Two different things cause the mix-up, and neither is a neighbourhood.

    Chefchaouen is the real blue town

    Morocco’s famous blue city is Chefchaouen, tucked into the Rif Mountains far to the north. It lies roughly 570 to 600 kilometres away, which means seven to eight hours by road. You therefore cannot see it as a day trip from the Marrakech old town. Plan two nights at minimum, ideally as part of a northern loop from Tangier or Fes.

    Jardin Majorelle is the blue in Marrakech

    The intense cobalt known as Majorelle Blue covers the villa and planters in Jacques Majorelle’s garden, just outside the medina walls. The garden is small, extremely photogenic and very busy, so book a timed ticket. That garden, rather than any district, is what most “blue Marrakech” photographs actually show.

    Which Half Should You Sleep In?

    This is the most consequential choice of the whole trip, because the two halves produce completely different holidays.

    Choose the Marrakech old town if…

    • Atmosphere is the point and you want the call to prayer from your terrace.
    • You plan to spend most days among the souks and monuments.
    • Courtyard architecture and small, personal guesthouses appeal to you.
    • You do not mind wheeling luggage the last few hundred metres.

    Choose the new town if…

    • A lift, a car park and a full-size pool matter to you.
    • You travel with reduced mobility or with very young children.
    • Quiet nights and fixed-price restaurants matter to you.
    • You are combining the trip with business or a longer stay.

    The split stay that works

    Repeat visitors often divide their nights. Two or three go to a medina riad for the atmosphere, then two go to Hivernage or the Palmeraie to recover beside a pool. Because the districts sit so close together, the transfer costs almost nothing in time or money.

    What to See in the Marrakech Old Town

    Nearly every landmark that made the city famous stands inside the Marrakech old town. The sights below justify their entry fees, listed roughly in the order most visitors enjoy them.

    Koutoubia Mosque

    The twelfth-century minaret rises 77 metres and anchors the western edge of the medina. Non-Muslim visitors cannot go inside, yet the rose gardens around it stay open to everyone and look wonderful after dark.

    Bahia Palace

    A nineteenth-century vizier’s residence with painted cedar ceilings, zellige floors and shaded courtyards. Arrive at opening time, because coach groups reach it by ten.

    Ben Youssef Madrasa

    Morocco’s largest historic Quranic school reopened after a long restoration. It now offers one of the most striking interiors in the country, and forty-five minutes is enough.

    Saadian Tombs and El Badi Palace

    Both sit in the Kasbah quarter, five minutes apart. The tombs dazzle with marble and gilding, while El Badi impresses through sheer scale and emptiness. Visit them together in one morning.

    Le Jardin Secret

    A restored riad garden in Mouassine, with a tower that looks out across the whole Marrakech old town. It is the best place inside the walls to sit down for twenty minutes.

    Musée des Confluences — Dar el Bacha

    A restored pasha’s palace with rotating exhibitions and a famous courtyard coffee house. Come for the architecture, then stay for the cardamom coffee.

    The Mellah spice market

    Place des Ferblantiers opens into the old Jewish quarter, where spice, olive and preserved-lemon stalls serve residents rather than visitors. Prices sit noticeably below the main souks.

    What to See in the New Town

    The new town rewards a half-day, especially if you enjoy design, art and coffee. Three stops deserve your time.

    Jardin Majorelle, the YSL Museum and the Berber Museum

    All three share one site on the edge of Gueliz, and together they form the most visited attraction in the city. The garden opens at 8:00 and takes its last entry at 18:00, so book online and go early or late.

    Galleries and concept stores

    Rue de la Liberté, Rue Yves Saint Laurent and Boulevard Mohammed Zerktouni hold contemporary galleries, ceramic studios and labels working with Moroccan textiles. Prices are fixed, and most shops ship internationally.

    Café terraces and the Menara Gardens

    Gueliz built its identity on terraces, so ordering a coffee and watching the avenue counts as a legitimate activity. Later, head south-west to the Menara Gardens, a twelfth-century olive grove around a reservoir with the Atlas behind it. Sunset is the moment to be there.

    Getting Around Both Halves of Town

    Walking

    Inside the medina, walking is the only realistic option, since most lanes are far too narrow for cars. Distances look short on a map and take longer in practice, because crowds and wrong turns slow you down. Budget roughly double what your map suggests.

    Petit taxis

    Beige petit taxis work within the city limits and carry up to three passengers. Agree the fare before you get in: 20 to 40 dirhams covers most journeys across town, rising a little at night. Drivers cannot legally take you beyond the city boundary.

    Grand taxis and airport transfers

    Larger cream Mercedes taxis handle longer trips and airport runs, and fixed tariffs appear at the rank. For a first arrival, a prepaid transfer to your riad gate removes almost all the stress. Our guide to flights to Marrakech explains the airport layout in detail.

    Buses, calèches and bikes

    Alsa city buses link the medina, Gueliz and the airport cheaply. Horse-drawn calèches circle the ramparts on a set tariff and make a pleasant late-afternoon loop. Bicycle and scooter rentals have expanded in the new town, where wide avenues make riding realistic; inside the walls, cycling is possible but demanding.

    Two Self-Guided Walks Across Town

    Walk one: the Marrakech old town core, about three hours

    1. Start in the Koutoubia gardens and walk east to Jemaa el-Fnaa.
    2. Enter the souks at Souk Semmarine and keep heading north.
    3. Turn right for Ben Youssef Madrasa and the Almoravid Koubba.
    4. Loop west into Mouassine for Le Jardin Secret and a mint tea.
    5. Return south to the square through the spice lanes.

    Walk two: Marrakech old town to new town, about two hours

    1. Begin at Place des Ferblantiers and the Mellah spice market.
    2. Walk to the Bahia Palace, then west past the Koutoubia.
    3. Follow Avenue Mohammed V through the Cyber Park.
    4. Cross Bab Nkob and continue into Gueliz.
    5. Finish with coffee on Rue de la Liberté, then take a taxi back.

    Shopping District by District

    Where you shop determines both the price and the experience. The souks expect negotiation, while the new town does not.

    The souks: negotiation territory

    Lanterns, rugs, leather, ceramics, babouche slippers and spices all originate here. Opening quotes usually sit two to four times above the realistic price, so counter at about 40 per cent and settle near the middle. Compare three stalls before committing, and stay good-humoured throughout.

    Ensemble Artisanal: the calibration stop

    This government craft centre near Bab Nkob shows fixed-price versions of almost everything sold in the souks. Spend twenty minutes there before you start bargaining and you will negotiate far more confidently afterwards.

    Gueliz concept stores and Sidi Ghanem

    Contemporary Moroccan design lives in the new town. Studios there produce ceramics, raffia bags, linen and jewellery at fixed prices, usually with international shipping. Serious shoppers go further out to Sidi Ghanem, where designers and manufacturers keep showrooms; go on a weekday morning and allow three hours.

    Bab el Khemis flea market

    The market beside the north-eastern gate runs most days and goes quiet on Fridays. Thursday gave the gate its name and remains the busiest morning. Furniture, doors, lamps and genuine antiques all turn up, alongside plenty of fakes, so treat it as a treasure hunt.

    Eating on Either Side of the Walls

    Food in the Marrakech old town leans traditional: tagine, tanjia, harira and grilled meat, often served on a rooftop. The new town leans international and contemporary, with fixed prices, wine lists and later hours.

    Both work well, and the smartest approach alternates between them. Eat street food and rooftop tagines inside the walls, then book one evening in Gueliz for something modern. Our guide to Moroccan cuisine explains what to order and when.

    Is the Marrakech Old Town Safe at Night?

    Broadly, yes. Main routes stay busy until midnight, and Jemaa el-Fnaa itself keeps going well past eleven. Nevertheless, side lanes empty quickly after ten, and the lighting is uneven.

    Simple night-time habits

    • Stick to lit routes with open shops instead of shortcuts.
    • Ask your riad for its recommended walking line from the square.
    • Carry a small torch, or use your phone light in unlit alleys.
    • Take a petit taxi to the nearest gate if you return very late.
    • Keep bags closed and worn across the body in crowds.

    In the new town, streets are wider, better lit and patrolled, so evening walking feels much like any Mediterranean city. For the wider picture, read our summary of current Morocco travel warnings.

    How One City Became Two Halves

    The split you walk through today was never designed as a single plan. It happened in waves, roughly 850 years apart, and knowing the sequence makes the layout much easier to read.

    Wave one: the walled capital

    The Almoravids founded the settlement around 1070 as a camp on the caravan route between the Sahara and the Atlantic. Walls followed within decades, and everything a medieval capital needed sat inside them: mosques, markets, workshops, water channels and housing. That enclosed world is the Marrakech old town of today.

    Wave two: the French plan

    After 1912, colonial planners made an unusual choice. Rather than clearing the medina, they built a separate district outside the walls, with a grid, tree-lined avenues and European apartment blocks. Gueliz was the result, and the decision preserved the Marrakech old town almost intact. Consequently the city kept its historic heart while gaining a modern centre.

    Wave three: the current expansion

    Since the 1990s, growth has pushed north toward Semlalia and west toward Targa, while Sidi Ghanem turned into a design and manufacturing quarter. Tourism reshaped the medina too, converting hundreds of decaying courtyard houses into riads. As a result, residents, artisans and guesthouses now share the same lane. If the history side interests you, our article on Marrakech as a capital city follows the story further, and our guide to Amazigh culture in Morocco covers the people who built it.

    Day Trips From Town

    One advantage of basing yourself here is how much sits within a short drive. Each option below works comfortably as a single day out.

    • Ourika Valley — an hour south, with waterfalls, riverside cafés and Amazigh villages. It is the easiest escape from summer heat.
    • Imlil and the High Atlas — ninety minutes away, and the trailhead for Mount Toubkal.
    • Agafay stone desert — thirty minutes out, with rocky hills, camel rides and sunset dinners rather than sand dunes.
    • Essaouira — under three hours west, bringing ramparts, seafood and constant wind.
    • Ouzoud Waterfalls — two and a half hours north-east, where the falls drop about 110 metres past cliffs full of Barbary macaques.
    • Ait Ben Haddou — four hours over the Tizi n’Tichka pass to the most photographed kasbah in Morocco.

    The full range sits on our day trips from Marrakech page. For the Sahara itself you need longer, and a 3-day desert tour is the shortest sensible option, while the 4-day version and the 5-day route add Merzouga and the Todra Gorge without rushing.

    What to Wear, and When to Come

    No strict dress code applies, yet what you wear changes how comfortable you feel, particularly inside the walls where daily life stays more traditional.

    In the medina

    Covered shoulders and knees are the practical norm. Loose cotton or linen keeps you cool and attracts less comment than shorts and vest tops. A light scarf earns its place three times over, providing sun protection, dust protection and modesty at religious sites.

    In the new town

    Gueliz and Hivernage feel noticeably more relaxed, and residents dress much as they would in any Mediterranean city. Swimwear, however, belongs at the pool rather than on the street anywhere in town.

    Footwear matters most

    Choose closed, broken-in shoes with grip. Medina lanes run uneven, occasionally wet from workshops, and shared with motorbikes. Sandals look sensible in the heat but leave your toes exposed in a crowd.

    Season by season

    March to May and September to November bring daytime temperatures between roughly 22 and 33 degrees, which suits walking at any hour. June to August regularly passes 40 degrees, so plan sightseeing before eleven and return outdoors after six. December to February gives bright days near 18 degrees and cold nights around five, so confirm that your riad has heating.     Accessibility and Travelling With Children

    The Marrakech old town presents real challenges: uneven paving, steps into riads, narrow lanes and very few ramps. Travellers with limited mobility usually find the new town far more workable, since Gueliz and Hivernage offer modern hotels with lifts, level pavements and taxi access to the door.

    Several medina monuments have improved, though. The Bahia Palace, El Badi and Jardin Majorelle all provide at least partial step-free routes. Hiring a private driver for the day removes most of the difficulty, because you can be dropped at each gate instead of walking between them.

    Children receive a warm welcome everywhere, since Moroccan life is family-centred. Even so, crowds and uneven ground tire small legs quickly. Use a carrier rather than a pushchair inside the walls, break the day with gardens, and consider a new town hotel where a pool provides an afternoon reset.

    Buying tickets

    Jardin Majorelle sells timed slots through its official site and often sells out in spring. Other sites still accept cash at the gate, and several now take cards. Carry 100-dirham notes, because change runs short early in the morning.

    Mistakes to Avoid

    • Assuming the two halves are far apart. Three kilometres separate them, not thirty.
    • Booking a riad without checking gate access. Ask which gate taxis use and how long the final walk is.
    • Skipping the new town entirely. You miss the galleries, the design scene and half the good restaurants.
    • Expecting a blue town here. Chefchaouen sits seven to eight hours north.
    • Planning too many sights per day. Three is plenty, because moving around takes time.
    • Relying on cards. Stalls, taxis and small cafés still prefer cash.

    How Long Do You Need?

    Two days

    Spend day one in the Marrakech old town: Koutoubia, Jemaa el-Fnaa, the souks and Ben Youssef Madrasa, then dinner on the square. Give day two to the Bahia Palace, the Saadian Tombs and El Badi, followed by Jardin Majorelle and dinner in Gueliz.

    Four days

    Add a traditional hammam, Le Jardin Secret, the Mellah spice market and a full afternoon in the new town. Keep the fourth day for the Ourika Valley or the Agafay desert.

    One week

    Use the city as a base and combine the above with Essaouira, the Ouzoud Waterfalls and a night in the High Atlas. If the Sahara tempts you, our 10-day Morocco itinerary and our private Sahara desert tour from Marrakech show how the pieces fit together.

    Marrakech Compared With Other Moroccan Medinas

    Travellers often ask how this medina measures up against Morocco’s other historic centres. Each one has a distinct personality.

    Fes

    Fes holds the larger and older medina, with thousands of lanes and a stronger scholarly and craft tradition. It feels less commercial and harder to navigate. Marrakech suits a first visit better, while Fes rewards a second one. Our tours from Fes cover that side of the country.

    Essaouira and Asilah

    Essaouira genuinely is a town, walkable end to end in an hour, with wind, seafood and a relaxed mood. Further north, Asilah offers whitewashed ramparts and painted murals on the Atlantic.

    Chefchaouen and the south

    The blue town of the Rif belongs to a northern loop from Tangier rather than a Marrakech add-on. South of the Atlas, meanwhile, the architecture shifts toward kasbahs and oases, and Ait Ben Haddou is the highlight.

    Where to Find Quiet, and Where to Photograph

    The Marrakech old town can feel intense, so knowing where to decompress makes a long trip far more sustainable.

    Five quiet corners

    • Le Jardin Secret — shaded benches and birdsong two minutes from the souks.
    • Cyber Park — a walled garden on Avenue Mohammed V, halfway between the two halves.
    • The El Badi ramparts — wide, breezy and usually near-empty.
    • Menara Gardens — olive shade and mountain views at the edge of town.
    • Your own riad terrace — honestly the best option between two and four in the afternoon.

    Six photographs worth planning

    • A rooftop café on Jemaa el-Fnaa as the square fills at dusk.
    • The Ben Youssef Madrasa courtyard at opening time, before groups arrive.
    • Souk Semmarine, where slatted roofs stripe the lane with light.
    • Bab Agnaou, carved and warm in late afternoon sun.
    • Jardin Majorelle, ideally in the first slot of the day.
    • The outer ramparts near Bab el Jdid, red walls against the Atlas.

    Always ask before photographing people. Some will decline, some will agree happily, and some will expect a small payment.

    Six Words That Help Everywhere

    • Salam alaikum — a respectful greeting
    • Shukran — thank you
    • La, shukran — no, thank you, the single most useful phrase in the souks
    • Bshhal? — how much?
    • Fin…? — where is…?
    • Safi — that’s enough, all good

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Where exactly is the old town in Marrakech?

    The Marrakech old town is the walled medina at the centre of the city, ringed by roughly nineteen kilometres of red rammed-earth ramparts. Jemaa el-Fnaa sits at its heart, and the souks spread north from the square.

    What is the Marrakech old town called?

    Locals call it the medina, which simply means “city” in Arabic and refers to the historic walled core of any Moroccan town. UNESCO lists it as the Medina of Marrakesh.

    Where is the new town?

    The new town lies immediately west and north-west of the walls. Gueliz forms its commercial centre, while Hivernage, just to the south, holds the larger hotels and the nightlife venues.

    How long does it take to walk across town?

    Crossing the medina on foot takes about forty minutes. Walking from Jemaa el-Fnaa to central Gueliz takes roughly thirty minutes along Avenue Mohammed V.

    Should I stay in the medina or in Gueliz?

    Stay in the medina for atmosphere, souks and riad architecture. Choose Gueliz or Hivernage for lifts, pools, parking and fixed-price restaurants. Families who want space often prefer the Palmeraie instead.

    Is the Marrakech old town safe at night?

    Main routes stay busy and well used until late, and the square itself remains lively past eleven. Side lanes empty quickly, though, so keep to lit streets and take a taxi to the nearest gate if you are out very late.

    Is there a blue town in Marrakech?

    No. Morocco’s blue town is Chefchaouen, roughly 570 to 600 kilometres north in the Rif Mountains. The blue you see in Marrakech photographs is Jardin Majorelle.

    How many days do I need?

    Two days cover the essentials, three feel comfortable, and four let you add a day trip to the mountains or the coast.

    Can I visit the Marrakech old town with limited mobility?

    Partly. Several major monuments now offer step-free routes, yet the lanes themselves stay uneven. Basing yourself in the new town and hiring a driver makes the medina far more manageable.

    Final Thoughts

    The best way to understand Marrakech is to stop treating its two halves as rivals. The Marrakech old town gives you the history, the noise and the craft. Gueliz and Hivernage give you shade, design and somewhere to sit down.
