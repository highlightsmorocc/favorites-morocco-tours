    Fantasia Show Marrakech: Chez Ali, Tbourida & Where to See It (2026)

    Fantasia show Marrakech guide: what tbourida is (UNESCO 2021), the Chez Ali Marrakech dinner show, 2026 prices, moussems and the Hassan II

    Favorites Morocco Tours · Updated: 23 September 2026

    Quick answer: fantasia (tbourida) is Morocco’s traditional equestrian art: a line of riders gallops together and fires black-powder rifles in a single blast. It has been on UNESCO’s Intangible Cultural Heritage list since 2021. In Marrakech, the most popular way to see a fantasia show is the Chez Ali dinner show in the Palmeraie. The authentic version is found at summer moussems and at the Hassan II Tbourida Trophy in Rabat.

    Summary in 30 seconds

    • “Fantasia” is the name used in English, French and Spanish. In Morocco it is called tbourida, from “baroud”, gunpowder.
    • Each troop of riders is called a sorba and is led by a chief, the allam or moqaddem.
    • UNESCO inscribed tbourida on its Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2021.
    • The 25th Hassan II Tbourida Trophy took place from 15 to 21 June 2026 at Dar Es Salam (Rabat), with 24 sorbas.
    • In Marrakech, Chez Ali hosts the best-known fantasia show: Moroccan dinner, music, dance and horsemen.
    • Agencies sell the Chez Ali evening with transfers for about USD 76-109 per person, depending on the date (September 2026).
    • Summer moussems, such as Moulay Abdellah Amghar near El Jadida, are the best place to see the tradition in its original form.
    • It is a loud show: the rifle blasts are strong, so prepare small children.

    What is a Moroccan fantasia show and why see it in Marrakech?

    Picture a row of ten or fifteen riders dressed in white. They wait, motionless, at the end of a sandy track. Suddenly the leader gives a command and they all burst into a gallop. For a few seconds the horses race in a perfect line. Then, at the exact moment, every rider fires his rifle into the air. If the blast sounds like a single thunderclap, the sorba has triumphed.

    That is fantasia, or tbourida. It is neither a race nor a simple parade. It is a display of coordination, discipline and harmony between rider, horse and group. That is why crowds follow it with such passion.

    Moreover, fantasia is not just a tourist show. It is part of religious festivals, weddings, regional fairs and major national celebrations. In many villages, families keep horses and costumes for generations in order to take part.

    For travellers, it is one of Morocco’s most powerful images. In addition, a fantasia show in Marrakech can be seen any night of the year, above all at the famous Chez Ali dinner show.

    Tbourida: the name and the origins

    From gunpowder to performance

    The word tbourida comes from “baroud”, meaning gunpowder in Moroccan Arabic. That is why it is also called “l’ab el baroud”, the game of gunpowder. The term “fantasia”, on the other hand, was popularised by 19th-century European travellers and painters.

    A warrior tradition

    It originates in the cavalry tactics of Arab and Amazigh (Berber) tribes. Cavalry charges and volley fire were common in battle. Over time, these manoeuvres came to be performed at festivals and ceremonies.

    Delacroix and the European gaze

    In 1832 the French painter Eugène Delacroix travelled to Morocco with a diplomatic mission. There he saw fantasias and turned them into celebrated paintings. Ever since, the image of riders galloping through gunpowder smoke has been linked to Morocco around the world.

    The horse in Moroccan culture

    The horse holds a special place in the country’s culture. The breeds most used in tbourida are the Barb and the Arab-Barb, which are hardy and agile. Many families also treat their horse as a member of the household. If Amazigh culture interests you, read our guide to Berber Morocco.

    Tbourida, UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage

    In December 2021 UNESCO inscribed tbourida on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The listing recognises its value as a social, festive and artistic practice passed down through generations.

    The inscription has given the tradition fresh momentum. On the one hand, young people are more interested. On the other, schools, competitions and support for sorbas have grown. There are also more and more women’s sorbas, something unthinkable a few decades ago.

    Morocco has other elements on this list, such as Gnawa (2019) and the cultural space of Jemaa el-Fna square. Learn more in our pillar guide to Morocco.

    How a sorba works: riders, leader and gunpowder

    The sorba

    A sorba is a troop of riders who perform together. It usually has between ten and twenty members. All wear the same costume and ride horses with matching tack.

    The allam or moqaddem

    The troop leader rides in the centre of the line. He sets the pace, calls the commands and signals the moment to fire. His experience is therefore decisive.

    The phases of a charge

    • The start: riders line up and salute the crowd or the judges.
    • The gallop: they set off together and keep a straight line.
    • Weapon handling: they raise and spin their rifles at full gallop.
    • The volley: on command, everyone fires at once.
    • The halt: they rein in together at the end of the track.

    What the judges look for

    In competitions, judges score alignment, synchronised firing, the riders’ elegance, the horses’ preparation and the beauty of the costumes. A ragged volley costs a lot of points.

    Costumes, saddles and rifles: the look of fantasia

    A big part of the show is visual. Riders and horses wear traditional pieces, many of them handmade.

    The rider’s costume

    Riders wear white tunics, a djellaba or selham (cape), a turban and leather slippers or boots. In some regions they add embroidered leather bags and decorative daggers. Learn to recognise these garments in our guide to traditional Moroccan clothing.

    Saddle and tack

    Saddles are high and embroidered with silk or metal thread. The tack includes breastplates and bridles decorated in bright colours. Each sorba has its own style.

    The powder rifle (moukahla)

    The rifles are long muzzle-loaders, often inlaid with silver or bone. They are loaded with powder only, no projectile. The white smoke of the volley is part of the spectacle.

    Chez Ali Marrakech: the most famous fantasia show in Marrakech

    Chez Ali is without doubt the most searched-for name when people talk about fantasia in Marrakech. It is a large venue in the Palmeraie, north of the city, combining a Moroccan dinner, music, dance and horsemen. It has been running for decades and is one of the city’s best-known evenings out.

    What the evening looks like

    • Arrival: guests walk through a monumental gate lined with musicians and torch-bearers.
    • Dinner: served in large decorated tents, usually harira, tagine or couscous, pastries and mint tea.
    • Folklore: music and dance troupes from different regions move between the tents during dinner.
    • The fantasia: at the end, everyone heads to the big arena to watch the riders charge and fire.
    • The finale: depending on the night, there may be acrobats, belly dancing and fireworks.

    Price and booking

    Agencies sell the Chez Ali evening with round-trip hotel transfers. In September 2026, prices on platforms such as GetYourGuide ran at about USD 76-109 per person, depending on the date and what is included. Departure is usually in the evening and the experience lasts around 4 hours. Always check what the price covers: transfers, drinks and menu type.

    Is it worth it?

    Chez Ali is designed for large groups, so some travellers find it too touristy. However, it is the easiest way to see a fantasia show in Marrakech in a single evening. If you want something more authentic, the next sections explain where to see real tbourida.

    Chez Ali Marrakech step by step: a practical guide

    How to book

    You can book through your riad, a local agency or online platforms. Most bookings include hotel pick-up. If you travel with us, we build it into your programme with a private driver.

    Pick-up

    Minibuses collect guests from hotels and medina access points. Riads inside the medina usually have a nearby meeting point, because vehicles cannot enter the lanes. Be on time: the route stops at several hotels.

    What to wear

    There is no dress code. However, the fantasia arena is outdoors. So between November and March bring a jacket, because Palmeraie nights are cool.

    Where to sit for the fantasia

    After dinner, everyone moves to the arena stands. For the best view of the charge, sit near the centre and in the front rows. From there you will see the line of riders coming straight at you.

    Drinks

    Some packages include water and soft drinks; others do not. Ask in advance which drinks are included and which cost extra.

    Getting back

    The return is usually around midnight. If you have an early flight the next day, bear that in mind when choosing a date.

    A day at a moussem with tbourida

    A moussem is much more than a show. It is a great social gathering lasting several days. You arrive in the morning to find a huge camp of white tents beside an earth track. Whole families have travelled from their villages, and there are sweet sellers, grilled-meat stalls and music everywhere.

    By mid-morning the first charges begin. Sorbas take turns for hours without a break. Every time a perfect volley rings out, the crowd cheers. When the powder goes off unevenly, however, a murmur of disappointment spreads.

    In the afternoon heat, the pace slows. People rest in the shade, drink tea and discuss the best charges. At sunset the sorbas return, and the golden light turns the gunpowder smoke into a dream for photographers.

    At night the tents fill with music, conversation and food. That is when you realise tbourida is not just a sport but a way of life and a shared identity.

    Fantasia in Marrakech and the Haouz region

    The Haouz plain around Marrakech is horse country. Many sorbas from the region compete at moussems and national events. So it is not unusual to see riders training on rural tracks at dusk.

    Marrakech was also for centuries a city of caravans and cavalry. Sultans and the great Atlas caids kept mounted troops, and official receptions often featured cavalry charges. This heritage explains why the fantasia show in Marrakech became, in the 20th century, one of visitors’ favourite spectacles.

    Today, fantasia in Marrakech is mostly experienced at dinner shows such as Chez Ali and at some private events in Palmeraie hotels and villas. If you are planning a wedding or corporate event, a sorba can also be hired for a private performance.

    Women in tbourida

    For centuries tbourida was an almost exclusively male world. In recent decades, however, all-female sorbas have emerged. Today they perform at moussems and exhibitions in several regions.

    The horsewomen wear adapted traditional costumes, with kaftans or tunics and colourful veils, and charge with the same precision as the men. For many of them, riding in a sorba continues the tradition of their fathers and grandfathers.

    Their presence has also become a symbol of social change in Morocco. So if you get the chance to see a women’s sorba, do not miss it: it is often one of the most applauded moments of the day.

    Where to see authentic fantasia in Morocco

    Moussems

    A moussem is a large regional festival, often linked to a local saint. For several days there is a market, music, food and, above all, tbourida. It is the best place to see the tradition in its original form, with sorbas from nearby villages and an enthusiastic local crowd.

    • Moussem of Moulay Abdellah Amghar (near El Jadida): usually in summer, one of the country’s largest tbourida gatherings, with hundreds of riders.
    • Tan-Tan Moussem (southern Morocco): brings together nomadic Saharan tribes and is recognised by UNESCO as a masterpiece of oral and intangible heritage.
    • Local moussems: many villages in the Haouz, Chaouia or Gharb hold their own moussem after the harvest.

    Dates change every year with the lunar calendar and local authorities. So confirm them shortly before you travel.

    The Hassan II Tbourida Trophy

    This is the major national competition. It is organised by the Royal Moroccan Equestrian Sports Federation under the patronage of King Mohammed VI. The 25th edition took place from 15 to 21 June 2026 at the Dar Es Salam complex in Rabat, with 24 sorbas: 18 senior and 6 junior. It is a unique chance to see the country’s best sorbas in one place.

    If you are travelling to Rabat in June, look out for the 2027 edition. Find route ideas in our tours from Casablanca.

    National holidays and weddings

    Tbourida may also feature at holidays such as Throne Day or at large rural weddings. However, these are not always open to the public. A local guide can tell you whether a celebration is happening nearby.

    Fantasia and other equestrian traditions of the world

    Horse cultures exist all over the world, and comparing them helps you understand what makes tbourida special.

    • Andalusian horse shows (Jerez, Spain): focus on dressage and elegance, with horses “dancing” to music.
    • Charrería (Mexico): a ranching tradition built on roping and riding skills, also recognised by UNESCO.
    • Cossack trick riding (dzhigitovka): acrobatics performed at full gallop.
    • Tbourida (Morocco): a collective charge ending in a perfectly synchronised gunpowder volley.

    What sets fantasia apart is the group. In tbourida, no rider shines alone: the whole sorba succeeds or fails together. That is why the single blast of the rifles is such an emotional moment for the crowd.

    Fantasia in the Moroccan calendar

    Tbourida is often tied to key moments of the year:

    • Harvest festivals: many moussems take place once work in the fields is done.
    • Religious and saints’ festivals: a moussem blends devotion, trade and celebration. Read our guide to religion in Morocco.
    • Throne Day: on 30 July, with celebrations across the country.
    • Weddings: in rural areas, a fantasia may welcome the bride or the guests.

    The monarchy also supports tbourida through the Hassan II Trophy. Learn about its role in the country’s culture in our guide to the Royal Family in Morocco.

    Fantasia as a cultural showcase ahead of the 2030 World Cup

    Morocco will co-host the 2030 FIFA World Cup with Spain and Portugal. In this context, the country is stepping up the promotion of its cultural heritage, and equestrian arts feature prominently in many official ceremonies and events.

    So you are likely to see more tbourida displays at festivals and public events in the coming years. Find out what the tournament means for travellers in our guide to the Morocco World Cup 2030.

    Staying in the Marrakech Palmeraie

    Chez Ali lies in the Palmeraie, a vast palm grove north of the city. The area is also home to luxury resorts, villas, golf courses and riding stables. If you want peace and space after days in the medina, it is a great base.

    In addition, many Palmeraie stables offer horse and camel rides among the palms, which pair naturally with a fantasia evening. The drive to the medina takes about 20 to 30 minutes, depending on traffic. Our private drivers can link both worlds easily during your stay.

    At night the square fills with musicians, storytellers and food stalls. It is a free, spontaneous show. Read more in our complete Marrakech travel guide.

    What is served at a fantasia dinner

    • Harira: a soup of pulses, tomato and spices.
    • Moroccan salads: aubergine, carrot, pepper and tomato.
    • Tagine or mechoui: slow-cooked lamb or chicken.
    • Couscous: with vegetables and meat.
    • Dessert: seasonal fruit, almond pastries or sweet pastilla.
    • Mint tea: the essential finale.

    If you have allergies or special diets, mention them when booking. Get to know these dishes in our guide to traditional Moroccan dishes.

    Tips for watching a fantasia

    • Protect your ears: the blasts are very loud. Bring earplugs if you travel with children.
    • Choose your spot: at moussems, stay behind the barrier, never on the track.
    • Shoot in burst mode: the volley lasts under a second.
    • Bring water and a hat: summer moussems are very hot.
    • Wrap up in winter: Marrakech nights are cold from December to February.
    • Respect the riders: ask before taking close-up portraits.
    • Carry cash: moussems have food and craft stalls. See our guide to the Moroccan dirham.

    Photographing a fantasia

    • Fast shutter: at least 1/1000 s to freeze the gallop in daylight.
    • Stand at an angle: you capture the line of riders and the smoke together.
    • Golden hour: at moussems, sunset light is the best.
    • At night, raise the ISO: Chez Ali’s arena is lit, but the light is artificial.
    • Protect your gear: dust and gunpowder smoke dirty the lens.

    Fantasia in Marrakech with children

    • Explain beforehand that there will be very loud gunpowder blasts.
    • Bring earplugs or ear defenders for little ones.
    • At Chez Ali the evening ends late; pick a night without an early start the next day.
    • At moussems, keep children behind the barriers at all times.

    Animal welfare at fantasia shows

    Many travellers ask about this, and rightly so. In traditional sorbas, the horse is a precious asset and riders care for it closely. The Royal Federation and regional associations promote veterinary checks at official competitions.

    However, as with any show involving animals, conditions vary. If this matters to you, watch the horses’ condition, their rest between charges and how grooms treat them. Official events and reputable providers are the better choice.

    When to see fantasia through the year

    • Spring: good weather and the first moussems in some regions.
    • Summer: peak moussem season and the Hassan II Trophy in June. It is very hot.
    • Autumn: post-harvest moussems and pleasant temperatures. Since 20 September 2026 Morocco has used permanent GMT, so it gets dark earlier on the clock and dinner shows start under a dark sky.
    • Winter: no moussems, but Chez Ali runs year-round. Ideal for Christmas and New Year in Morocco.

    Safety during the show

    Watching a fantasia is generally safe if you follow basic rules. Horses run at high speed and the rifles produce smoke and sparks, so the barriers exist for good reason. Never cross the track, keep an eye on children, mind your belongings in moussem crowds, and stand away from the smoke if you have breathing problems.     Common mistakes with fantasia

    • Thinking it is a race: synchrony matters, not speed.
    • Getting close to the track: horses arrive at high speed.
    • Expecting full authenticity at a dinner show: Chez Ali is a tourist show, not a moussem.
    • Not checking what the price includes: transfers, drinks and menu vary by agency.
    • Forgetting warm clothes in winter: the arena is outdoors.
    • Going to a moussem without confirming dates: they change every year.
