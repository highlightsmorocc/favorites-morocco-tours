    Morocco World Cup 2030: The Complete Travel Guide to Stadiums, Host Cities and Trips

    The Morocco World Cup 2030 will be the biggest event this country has ever staged. FIFA confirmed Morocco, Spain and Portugal as the main hosts, and the tournament also honours the centenary of the very first World Cup in Uruguay. For football fans, this is a rare chance to watch elite football and travel through one of the most beautiful countries on earth in the same trip.

    At Favorites Morocco Tours we build private itineraries across Morocco every week. So we wrote this guide to answer the questions travellers keep asking us about the Morocco World Cup 2030: which cities host matches, how to move between them, where to sleep, and what to see when the stadium lights go off.

    Morocco World Cup 2030: Why This Tournament Matters So Much

    Morocco has chased a World Cup for decades. The country bid five times and lost five times. Then, in December 2024, FIFA approved the joint Morocco-Spain-Portugal project and the wait finally ended. The bid even carries a name that mixes both shores of the Mediterranean: Yalla Vamos 2030.

    A centenary tournament spread across six countries

    The 2030 edition breaks the usual pattern. Three nations host the tournament itself: Morocco, Spain and Portugal. Meanwhile, three South American countries stage special centenary matches at the start. Uruguay, Argentina and Paraguay each welcome one game, because Uruguay hosted and won the first World Cup back in 1930.

    Montevideo therefore gets a symbolic moment. The historic Estadio Centenario, home of the 1930 final, opens the celebrations. After those matches, the competition moves to Europe and Africa for the rest of the schedule.

    What Morocco brings to the tournament

    Morocco is not a newcomer to big football. The national team reached the semi-finals in Qatar in 2022, the first African side ever to do so. The country also hosted the Africa Cup of Nations across the 2025-2026 winter, which worked as a full-scale rehearsal for stadiums, transport and fan zones.

    Beyond football, Morocco offers something the other hosts cannot match. You can watch a match in a 115,000-seat stadium and sleep under the stars in the Sahara three days later. That contrast is exactly why so many fans already plan a longer stay.

    Africa’s second World Cup

    South Africa 2010 remains the only World Cup held on African soil. Morocco therefore becomes the second African host, and the first Arab-African nation to co-host. For the region, the symbolism runs deep. For visitors, it means a warm welcome and a country that genuinely wants to impress.

    When Is the Morocco World Cup 2030? Dates and Schedule

    Football fans love a countdown, so here are the Morocco World Cup 2030 dates that matter today.

    The tournament window

    The 2030 FIFA World Cup runs from 8 June to 21 July 2030. The final takes place on Sunday 21 July 2030. That window sits in the Moroccan summer, which shapes almost every practical decision in this guide.

    The format

    The tournament follows the expanded 48-team format introduced in 2026. That means 104 matches in total, spread across the host nations. As a result, Morocco will stage far more games than a traditional single-host World Cup would allow for one country.

    What FIFA has not announced yet

    Several details remain open, and honest planning means saying so clearly. FIFA has not published the match schedule, the group allocation, or the ticket sales calendar. The final venue also remains undecided.

    Therefore, treat any website promising confirmed fixtures or guaranteed tickets today with real suspicion. We cover that risk in detail later in this guide.

    The realistic planning timeline

    Based on how FIFA has handled previous tournaments, expect the qualifying draw and calendar first, then venue allocation, then the ticket phases roughly eighteen to twenty-four months before kick-off. Hotels and riads, however, work on a different clock. Serious travellers start locking accommodation and private transport well before FIFA opens ticket sales.

    The Six Morocco World Cup 2030 Host Cities and Stadiums

    The Morocco World Cup 2030 venue list covers six cities. Five already exist and are being rebuilt to FIFA standards. One rises from scratch near Casablanca, and it will be the largest football stadium on the planet.

    Casablanca: Grand Stade Hassan II

    The headline venue sits in El Mansouria, in Benslimane province, roughly forty kilometres north-east of Casablanca. The Grand Stade Hassan II carries a planned capacity of 115,000 seats. No football stadium in the world will hold more.

    The design comes from a team pairing Moroccan architect Tarik Oualalou with the global stadium studio Populous. Their concept draws on the moussem, the traditional Moroccan tented gathering, so the roof reads like a vast canopy rising from the landscape. Construction started in 2025, and reports through 2026 put the project ahead of schedule.

    Casablanca itself is Morocco’s economic engine. The city gives you the Hassan II Mosque, Art Deco boulevards, a long Atlantic corniche and the country’s main international airport. Our tours from Casablanca already start here every week, so the logistics are familiar territory.

    Rabat: Prince Moulay Abdellah Stadium

    Rabat is the political capital, and its rebuilt Prince Moulay Abdellah Stadium holds about 69,500 spectators. The venue reopened after a deep reconstruction and now ranks among the most modern in Africa.

    The city suits travellers who prefer calm to chaos. Rabat offers the Kasbah of the Udayas, the Chellah necropolis, the Hassan Tower and a clean, walkable medina. Moreover, it sits directly on the high-speed rail line, which makes it an underrated base.

    Tangier: Grand Stade de Tanger (Ibn Batouta)

    Tangier looks out at Spain across the Strait of Gibraltar. Its Ibn Batouta Stadium expands to roughly 75,500 seats for the tournament, making it Morocco’s second-largest venue.

    Few cities carry this much literary and cultural baggage. Tangier attracted writers, painters and musicians for a century, and the old town still holds that restless energy. Fans arriving by ferry from Tarifa or Algeciras will land here first, so expect the city to feel like the bridge between the European and African legs of the tournament. Our tours from Tangier take advantage of exactly that position.

    Fez: Complexe Sportif de Fès

    Fez brings around 55,800 seats after renovation. The stadium sits outside the historic core, which is probably a blessing, because Fez el-Bali is a UNESCO World Heritage medina with thousands of alleys and no room for a stadium crowd.

    This is the spiritual and intellectual capital of Morocco. Al Quaraouiyine, founded in 859, is often described as the oldest continuously operating university in the world. Tanneries, madrasas, artisan workshops and hidden riads fill the rest of the map. Combining a match here with the tours from Fes makes obvious sense.

    Marrakech: Grand Stade de Marrakech

    Marrakech offers about 45,860 seats after its upgrade, with the Atlas Mountains standing behind the stands on a clear day. The stadium lies north of the city, close to the Casablanca road.

    For most international visitors, Marrakech is Morocco. Jemaa el-Fna fills with storytellers and food stalls at dusk, the souks swallow entire afternoons, and the riads hide behind doors you would otherwise walk past. Marrakech also works as the gateway to the desert, so our tours from Marrakech remain the most requested itineraries we run.

    Agadir: Adrar Stadium

    Agadir completes the list with roughly 46,000 seats. The city rebuilt itself after the 1960 earthquake, so it feels modern rather than historic, with wide avenues and a ten-kilometre beach.

    Agadir suits families and anyone who wants sun between matches. Surfing at Taghazout, the Souss-Massa national park and the Anti-Atlas valleys all sit within easy reach. In addition, the summer heat stays gentler here than inland, thanks to the Atlantic.

    Will Morocco host the World Cup final?

    This question comes up constantly, so let us answer it carefully. FIFA has not confirmed the final venue. Three stadiums appeared in the approved bid as candidates: the Santiago Bernabéu in Madrid, the renovated Camp Nou in Barcelona, and the Grand Stade Hassan II near Casablanca.

    Casablanca has the largest capacity by a wide margin. Still, capacity alone does not decide the allocation, and Spanish federation figures have lobbied hard for Madrid or Barcelona. Until FIFA publishes the match allocation, everything else is speculation dressed as news.

    Getting Around Morocco During the World Cup 2030

    Distance worries a lot of first-time visitors to the Morocco World Cup 2030. Morocco is roughly the size of France, yet the host cities connect better than most people expect, and the network keeps improving ahead of 2030.

    Al Boraq: the high-speed train that reshapes the Morocco World Cup 2030

    Al Boraq already links Tangier, Rabat and Casablanca along a 323-kilometre corridor. The journey from Tangier to Casablanca takes about one hour and thirty minutes, down from nearly five hours before the line opened.

    More importantly, Morocco is extending the line from Kenitra to Marrakech. That 430-kilometre section is scheduled to open around 2029, in time for the tournament. Once it runs, Tangier to Marrakech drops to roughly two hours and forty minutes, and Casablanca to Marrakech falls to well under two hours. The plan also includes a direct rail link to Mohammed V International Airport.

    Conventional trains

    ONCF, the national rail operator, also runs standard intercity trains. These serve Fez, Meknes, Oujda and other cities beyond the high-speed corridor. Second-class tickets stay cheap, first class costs little more, and the trains are comfortable enough for a three-hour hop.

    Motorways and private transfers

    Morocco built a strong motorway network long before the World Cup arrived. Toll roads connect Tangier, Rabat, Casablanca, Marrakech, Fez and Agadir. Driving Casablanca to Marrakech takes about two and a half hours, and Marrakech to Agadir around three.

    On match days, however, traffic around stadiums becomes the real obstacle. Private transfers with a local driver solve that problem, because a driver who knows the approach roads saves you an hour of frustration. That is precisely why we recommend booking transport early rather than improvising.

    Airports and domestic flights

    Morocco operates international airports in Casablanca, Marrakech, Tangier, Fez, Agadir and Rabat. Casablanca’s Mohammed V handles the most long-haul traffic, and a major expansion is already underway to raise capacity before 2030.

    Domestic flights exist, yet they rarely beat the train once you count check-in time. Save them for long jumps such as Tangier to Agadir.

    Getting around inside the cities

    Casablanca and Rabat both run tram networks, and both cities keep extending them. Petit taxis handle short urban trips everywhere, so agree the fare or insist on the meter before you set off. Ride-hailing apps work in the big cities, although coverage varies.

    Choosing Your Base for the Morocco World Cup 2030

    You do not need to change hotel after every Morocco World Cup 2030 match. In fact, a smart base plus rail or private transfers usually beats constant repacking. Here is how the six cities compare.

    Marrakech: the all-rounder

    Marrakech gives you the widest choice of accommodation, the best restaurant scene and direct access to the desert and the Atlas. It also sits at the southern end of the future high-speed line. Choose Marrakech if you want atmosphere and excursions in the same package.

    Casablanca: the practical choice

    Casablanca offers the main airport, the biggest business hotels and the largest stadium. The city feels less romantic than Marrakech or Fez, yet it works brilliantly as a logistics hub. Choose Casablanca if you value connections above charm.

    Rabat: the calm capital

    Rabat combines a manageable size with real history and excellent rail links. Prices tend to sit below Marrakech levels, and the crowds thin out. Choose Rabat if you want quiet evenings after loud afternoons.

    Tangier: the European gateway

    Tangier suits fans arriving from Spain, whether by ferry or by the short flight. The city also lets you reach Chefchaouen, Asilah and Tetouan on day trips. Choose Tangier if your tournament plan straddles both continents.

    Fez: the cultural heart

    Fez delivers the most intense medina experience in Morocco. The city offers fewer beds than Marrakech, so book earlier here than anywhere else. Choose Fez if history matters more to you than nightlife.

    Agadir: the beach base

    Agadir gives you resorts, a long beach and the mildest summer temperatures of the six. Families travelling with children often prefer it. Choose Agadir if you want the tournament to feel like a holiday rather than a campaign.

    One base or several during the Morocco World Cup 2030?

    Our honest advice depends on your tickets. Two matches in the north, for example, work well from a single base in Rabat or Tangier. Matches split between Marrakech and Fez, on the other hand, justify moving. As a rule, avoid changing city more than every three nights, because packing days eat your holiday.

    Where to Stay During the Morocco World Cup 2030

    Accommodation will become the single hardest part of any Morocco World Cup 2030 trip. Demand will spike, prices will rise, and the best properties will sell out first.

    Riads: the Moroccan classic

    A riad is a traditional house built around an interior courtyard, usually with a fountain, a plunge pool and a roof terrace. Most riads hold between five and fifteen rooms, so they fill fast. They also sit inside the medina, which means charm, quiet and a short walk to the action.

    One warning: cars cannot always reach the door. Expect a porter with a cart for your luggage in Fez and parts of Marrakech.

    Hotels and international brands

    Casablanca, Marrakech, Rabat and Agadir all offer four and five-star international hotels. These properties suit travellers who want a gym, reliable air conditioning and a bar that serves alcohol. Business hotels in Casablanca also handle early airport departures better than a medina riad.

    Kasbahs and desert camps

    Outside the cities, the accommodation changes character completely. Kasbah hotels in the Dades and Draa valleys occupy restored mud-brick fortresses. Luxury desert camps near Merzouga and Erg Chigaga put you between the dunes, with private tents, proper beds and dinner under an enormous sky.

    Apartments and short-term rentals

    Rentals work well for groups of four or more, particularly in Casablanca and Agadir. Verify the exact address before paying, though, and prefer platforms with a refund policy. During a World Cup, rental scams multiply.

    When should you book?

    Book accommodation as soon as your match plan takes shape, and ideally before FIFA finalises the schedule. Flexible cancellation policies exist for a reason, so use them. Riad owners in particular will hold rooms months in advance for confirmed bookings, and they rarely release good rooms late.

    Tickets, Hospitality and How to Avoid Scams

    Tickets generate more confusion than any other Morocco World Cup 2030 topic, so let us keep this simple and factual.

    How FIFA usually sells tickets

    FIFA sells World Cup tickets through its own official platform, normally in several phases. Early phases often use a random selection draw rather than first-come-first-served. Later phases move to direct sales as remaining inventory appears. Hospitality packages, which bundle premium seats with catering, sell through officially appointed partners.

    For the 2030 tournament, FIFA has not yet opened any phase. Registration usually opens on FIFA’s own website, so create an account there and wait for the official announcement.

    Morocco World Cup 2030 ticket scams: red flags to watch for

    Be wary of any site that claims to sell confirmed 2030 tickets today. Nobody holds them yet. Equally, ignore offers that guarantee specific teams in specific stadiums, because the draw has not happened.

    Other warning signs include payment by bank transfer only, prices far below market, no company address, and pressure to decide within hours. Legitimate operators never rush you like that.

    What a realistic budget looks like

    Costs will depend on your ticket category and your travel style. Even so, Morocco remains cheaper than most European destinations for food, transport and mid-range hotels. A meal in a good local restaurant costs a fraction of the Madrid equivalent, and taxis stay inexpensive.

    Expect accommodation to be the exception. Prices in host cities will climb sharply during match weeks, which is another argument for booking early.

    Where travel agencies genuinely help

    A licensed local operator cannot sell you FIFA tickets. What we can do is handle everything around the match: airport pickups, private transfers between host cities, vetted riads, licensed guides and desert extensions. In other words, we take care of the parts that go wrong when 300,000 visitors arrive at once. You can contact our team to start planning that framework early.

    What to Do Between Matches: Morocco Beyond the Stadium

    A Morocco World Cup 2030 match lasts ninety minutes. Your trip should last a great deal longer, and Morocco rewards every extra day.

    The imperial cities

    Morocco has four imperial cities: Marrakech, Fez, Meknes and Rabat. Each served as a capital at some point, and each left behind palaces, walls and medinas.

    Marrakech dazzles with the Koutoubia minaret, the Bahia Palace, the Saadian Tombs and the Majorelle Garden. Fez overwhelms with its labyrinth, its tanneries and its craftsmen. Meknes impresses with monumental gates and the enormous granaries of Moulay Ismail. Rabat charms quietly, with blue-and-white lanes above the ocean.

    The Sahara: dunes, camels and silence

    The desert remains the trip most guests remember for life. Merzouga sits below Erg Chebbi, where the dunes rise up to 150 metres. Erg Chigaga, reached from Zagora or M’Hamid, feels wilder and emptier.

    A typical route from Marrakech crosses the Tizi n’Tichka pass, stops at the fortified village of Aït Benhaddou, follows the Dades or Todra gorges, and reaches the dunes on the second afternoon. You ride camels at sunset, eat tagine at camp, and watch a sky most city dwellers never see. Our Morocco tours cover these routes in three, four or five-day formats.

    The Atlas Mountains

    The High Atlas rises within an hour of Marrakech. The Ourika Valley suits a half-day escape, with waterfalls and Berber villages along the river. Imlil serves as the base camp for Mount Toubkal, North Africa’s highest peak at 4,167 metres.

    Summer heat makes the mountains especially appealing during the tournament. Temperatures drop noticeably with altitude, so a mountain day works as natural air conditioning. Several of our day trips from Marrakech follow exactly this logic.

    The Atlantic coast

    Essaouira sits three hours from Marrakech and feels completely different: fortified ramparts, blue fishing boats, seagulls and constant wind. Surfers head to Taghazout near Agadir. Oualidia offers lagoons and oysters. Asilah, north of Rabat, covers its white walls with murals every summer.

    Moroccan food

    Do not treat food as an afterthought. Tagine varies by region, couscous belongs to Friday, and pastilla layers pigeon or chicken with cinnamon and almond in flaky pastry. Street food deserves attention too, from harira soup to grilled sardines on the coast.

    Mint tea arrives constantly, poured from height into small glasses. Accept it. Refusing tea in Morocco is a bit like refusing a handshake.

    Chefchaouen and the north

    The blue city sits in the Rif Mountains, roughly two hours from Tangier. Every wall glows in a different shade of blue, and the photographs almost take themselves. Fans based in Tangier can visit in a single day, which makes it an easy addition between fixtures.

    Practical Travel Tips for the Morocco World Cup 2030

    These details rarely appear in football coverage, yet they decide whether your Morocco World Cup 2030 trip feels smooth or stressful.

    Visas and entry

    Morocco allows visa-free entry for tourists from many countries, including the European Union, the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, Australia and Brazil, usually for ninety days. Other nationalities need a visa or an electronic travel authorisation.

    Rules change, however, and major tournaments often bring temporary measures. Morocco introduced a special electronic authorisation for several African nations during the last Africa Cup of Nations, for example. Therefore, check the official requirements for your passport closer to 2030, and make sure your passport stays valid for at least six months.

    Money and payments

    The currency is the Moroccan dirham, a closed currency you cannot easily buy abroad. Withdraw cash from ATMs on arrival or exchange at official bureaux. Cards work in hotels, larger restaurants and shops, yet the medina runs on cash.

    Keep small notes for taxis, tips and market purchases. Vendors rarely have change for large bills.

    Staying connected

    Buy a local SIM card at the airport from Maroc Telecom, Orange or Inwi. Data packages cost very little and coverage is good across the host cities. eSIM options also work well if your phone supports them. Reliable data matters more than usual during a tournament, because you will rely on maps, ride-hailing and ticket apps.

    Weather in June and July

    Summer heat is real, and you should plan around it. Marrakech and Fez regularly pass 38°C in July, while the desert climbs higher. The coast behaves differently: Agadir, Essaouira and Casablanca stay far cooler thanks to the Atlantic breeze.

    Consequently, structure your days around the temperature. Visit medinas and monuments in the morning, rest or swim in the afternoon, and go out again after six. Drink far more water than you think you need, and carry a hat.

    Language

    Moroccan Arabic and Amazigh are the everyday languages, and French serves as the main second language. Spanish appears widely in the north, especially in Tangier and Tetouan. English spreads fast in tourist areas, and by 2030 you will find it comfortably in every host city.

    Learning a few words still pays off. Salam for hello, shukran for thank you, and bslama for goodbye will earn genuine smiles.

    Culture and etiquette

    Dress moderately outside beach resorts, particularly in medinas and religious areas. Ask before photographing people, and expect to pay performers in Jemaa el-Fna if you photograph them. Alcohol is available in hotels, licensed restaurants and some shops, but not everywhere.

    Bargaining belongs to the souk experience, so approach it with humour rather than aggression. Start low, smile, and walk away politely if the price never lands.

    Safety

    Morocco ranks among the safer destinations in the region, and tourist areas receive heavy policing. Standard precautions still apply: watch your pockets in crowds, use registered taxis at night, and keep valuables in the hotel safe.

    During matches, expect strict stadium security and long entry queues. Arrive early, carry minimal bags, and check the venue rules before you leave the hotel.

    Travelling with children

    Moroccan culture adores children, and families receive a warm welcome everywhere. Riads sometimes have steep stairs and open courtyards, though, so check suitability before booking. Agadir and Essaouira usually work best for younger kids.

    Sample Itineraries for the Morocco World Cup 2030

    These outlines show how a Morocco World Cup 2030 match schedule can fit around real travel. We adapt them once FIFA confirms fixtures.

    Seven days: the northern circuit

    Start in Tangier for two nights, with a day trip to Chefchaouen. Take Al Boraq to Rabat for two nights and a match. Continue to Casablanca for three nights, including the Grand Stade Hassan II and the Hassan II Mosque. This route uses the train almost exclusively, so you avoid long drives entirely.

    Ten days: Morocco World Cup 2030 football and desert

    Begin in Marrakech for three nights, including a match at the Grand Stade de Marrakech. Head into the desert for three days via Aït Benhaddou, the Dades Valley and Merzouga. Return to Marrakech, then move north to Casablanca for the final stretch. This itinerary balances stadium days with the landscapes people fly to Morocco for.

    Fourteen days: the full tournament trip

    Combine the imperial cities with the coast and the Sahara. A workable order runs Casablanca, Rabat, Fez, Merzouga, Dades, Marrakech, Essaouira and back. Two weeks lets you see three or four matches without rushing between them.

    Short trips for a single match

    Fans flying in for one fixture still deserve more than an airport and a stadium. A four-night visit to Marrakech, for example, fits a match, a medina day, an Atlas excursion and a proper dinner. Our travel blog covers many of these short formats in detail.

    The Morocco World Cup 2030 Fan Experience Outside the Stands

    Tournaments live outside the stadium as much as inside it. Morocco already knows this, because the country filled squares and cafés during the last Africa Cup of Nations.

    Fan zones and public screenings

    Expect official fan zones in every host city, plus hundreds of unofficial ones. Moroccan cafés set up screens on the pavement for every big match, and the atmosphere spills into the street the moment a goal goes in. Jemaa el-Fna in Marrakech and the Casablanca corniche will almost certainly become the loudest gathering points.

    Watching football like a local

    Order a mint tea, take a chair facing the screen, and settle in. Moroccan café culture turns strangers into commentators within minutes. Furthermore, you will hear three or four languages around one table, which sums up the country neatly.

    Football culture in Morocco

    Domestic clubs carry serious weight here. Raja and Wydad divide Casablanca, and their derby ranks among the fiercest in Africa. AS FAR represents Rabat, MAS Fez holds the north-east, and Kawkab Marrakech keeps the red city loud. Learning a little of this backstory makes every conversation richer.

    Souvenirs worth carrying home

    Skip the mass-produced scarves and buy something made locally instead. Leather from Fez, argan oil from the Souss, ceramics from Safi and rugs woven in the Middle Atlas all travel well. Cooperatives usually offer fairer prices than roadside stalls, and the quality shows.

    Morocco World Cup 2030: Frequently Asked Questions

    Which cities in Morocco will host the 2030 World Cup?

    Six cities host matches: Casablanca, Rabat, Tangier, Fez, Marrakech and Agadir. Casablanca’s Grand Stade Hassan II is a new build, while the other five venues undergo major renovation.

    When does the Morocco World Cup 2030 start?

    The tournament runs from 8 June to 21 July 2030. Three centenary matches take place in Uruguay, Argentina and Paraguay before the main competition continues in Morocco, Spain and Portugal.

    Will the World Cup final be played in Morocco?

    FIFA has not decided. The Grand Stade Hassan II, the Santiago Bernabéu and the Camp Nou all appear as candidates. Only FIFA’s official match allocation will settle the question.

    How many people will the Casablanca stadium hold?

    The Grand Stade Hassan II is designed for 115,000 spectators, which would make it the largest football stadium in the world.

    Do I need a visa to attend the Morocco World Cup 2030?

    Many nationalities enter Morocco visa-free for up to ninety days, including most European, North American and Latin American passports. Other travellers need a visa or an electronic authorisation, so check the official rules for your nationality before booking.

    When do 2030 World Cup tickets go on sale?

    FIFA has not announced the ticket calendar. Based on previous tournaments, expect the first phase roughly eighteen to twenty-four months before kick-off, sold exclusively through FIFA’s official platform.

    How do I travel between Moroccan host cities?

    Use Al Boraq high-speed trains between Tangier, Rabat and Casablanca, with the Marrakech extension due around 2029. Conventional trains reach Fez, and private transfers or flights cover Agadir. Private drivers save the most time on match days.

    Is Morocco expensive during the World Cup?

    Food, transport and guiding stay affordable by European standards. Accommodation in host cities, however, will rise sharply during match weeks, so early booking makes the biggest financial difference.

    Can I combine matches with a Sahara desert tour?

    Yes, and most of our guests do exactly that. A three-day desert loop from Marrakech fits neatly between fixtures, and a four-day version reaches Erg Chigaga or the Todra Gorge as well.

    What is the weather like during the tournament?

    June and July bring hot, dry conditions inland, often above 38°C in Marrakech and Fez. Coastal cities such as Agadir, Essaouira and Casablanca remain much cooler thanks to Atlantic breezes.

    Is Morocco safe for football fans?

    Morocco is generally safe, with strong security in tourist areas and stadiums. Normal urban precautions apply, particularly in crowded medinas and around transport hubs.

    Should I book accommodation before I have tickets?

    Book flexible accommodation early and adjust later. Riads and boutique hotels have very few rooms, so waiting for confirmed fixtures usually means paying more for something worse.

    Which Moroccan city is the best base for the Morocco World Cup 2030?

    Marrakech suits travellers who want atmosphere and excursions, while Casablanca wins on flights and rail connections. Rabat offers a calmer, cheaper middle ground with excellent transport links.

    How far in advance should I plan a Morocco World Cup 2030 trip?

    Start shaping the trip two to three years ahead. Lock flexible accommodation and private transport first, then adjust the details once FIFA publishes fixtures and opens ticket sales.
