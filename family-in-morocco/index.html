    Royal Family in Morocco: Origins, History & Legacy

    Explore the history of Morocco's Royal Family: its origins, the Alaouite dynasty, key milestones, and the legacy that endures to this day.

    Favorites Morocco Tours · Updated: 23 September 2026

    Updated: 23 September 2026 · Written by the Favorites Morocco Tours team in Marrakech.

    The royal family in Morocco fascinates travellers from the UK, the US and far beyond. The king’s face is on every banknote, in every café and at every national celebration. Yet few visitors know who is who, how much power the king really holds or which palaces they can actually see. This guide answers all of that in plain English, with facts updated for 2026.

    We also show you how to turn that curiosity into a trip. You can follow the dynasty through the imperial cities, the royal mausoleums and the desert oasis where it all began. If you prefer a private driver, you will find our Morocco tours linked throughout the article.

    Quick answer: is there a royal family in Morocco?

    Yes. Morocco is a constitutional monarchy ruled by the Alaouite dynasty since the 17th century. The king is Mohammed VI, born in Rabat in 1963 and on the throne since 23 July 1999. His heir is Crown Prince Moulay Hassan, born in 2003. The core of the Moroccan royal family also includes Princess Lalla Khadija, the king’s brother Prince Moulay Rachid and his three sisters.

    Who is who in the royal family in Morocco (2026)

    Before the history, let’s put a name and a role to each member of the royal family in Morocco. That way the news, the official portraits and your guide’s stories in Fes or Rabat will make much more sense.

    King Mohammed VI

    Mohammed VI was born in Rabat on 21 August 1963. He is the eldest son of King Hassan II and Lalla Latifa. He came to the throne on 23 July 1999, just hours after his father died. According to Wikipedia, he is a member of the Alaouite dynasty. He also holds the religious title Amir al-Mu’minin, or Commander of the Faithful.

    Early in his reign, Moroccans called him “the king of the poor”. He promised to fight poverty and to break with the repressive “years of lead” of the previous era. His best-known reform is the 2004 Family Code, the Moudawana, which expanded women’s rights. He also launched projects that visitors use every day: the Tanger Med port, the Al Boraq high-speed train and the huge solar plants near Ouarzazate.

    In daily life, the king is everywhere. His portrait hangs in shops, hotels and offices. So understanding who he is helps you read the country you are visiting.

    Crown Prince Moulay Hassan

    Crown Prince Moulay Hassan was born on 8 May 2003 at the Royal Palace in Rabat. He is named after his grandfather, Hassan II. He studied at Mohammed VI Polytechnic University and has joined his father at official events since childhood. In 2025 he was promoted to the rank of colonel-major in the Royal Armed Forces.

    2026 has been a turning point for him. On 2 May, the king appointed him Coordinator of the Offices and Services of the General Staff of the Royal Armed Forces. The official portal maroc.ma notes that Mohammed VI held the very same post as crown prince in 1985. Many analysts read this parallel as a calm, deliberate preparation for succession.

    In addition, the heir opened the Mohammed VI Tower between Rabat and Salé on 13 April 2026. He also presided over the opening of the Africa Cup of Nations in December 2025. Today he is the most visible young member of the royal family in Morocco.

    Princess Lalla Khadija

    Princess Lalla Khadija was born in 2007 and is the king’s younger child. For years she stayed away from the cameras. Since her teens, however, she has appeared beside her father and brother at some ceremonies. She speaks Arabic, French and English and is still studying. The Constitution reserves the throne for men, so she is not in the direct line of succession.

    Princess Lalla Salma, the mother of the royal children

    Salma Bennani is a computer engineer from Fes. She married Mohammed VI in 2002 and changed the dynasty’s traditions. For the first time, the king’s wife received an official title, “Princess Lalla Salma”, and a public role. She chaired a foundation dedicated to fighting cancer.

    She stopped appearing at official events at the end of 2017. International media reported a divorce in 2018, but the palace never issued a detailed statement about her status. Even so, she remains the mother of the future king. That is why she still drives so many searches about the royal family in Morocco.

    Prince Moulay Rachid and his family

    Prince Moulay Rachid was born on 20 June 1970. He is the king’s younger brother and often represents him, for example when welcoming heads of state at Rabat airport. He chairs the foundation behind the Marrakech International Film Festival, a highlight for autumn visitors.

    In 2014 he married Lalla Oum Keltoum. The couple have two sons: Prince Moulay Ahmed, born in 2016, and Prince Moulay Abdeslam. At Throne Day 2026, Moulay Ahmed stood beside the king together with Moulay Hassan and Moulay Rachid.

    Princesses Lalla Meryem, Lalla Asma and Lalla Hasna

    The king’s three sisters play a quiet but steady role. Each one heads social or environmental foundations:

    • Lalla Meryem (1962), the eldest sibling, works on women’s and children’s causes.
    • Lalla Asma (1965) gives her name to a foundation for deaf children.
    • Lalla Hasna (1967) chairs the Mohammed VI Foundation for Environmental Protection, known for its clean-beach programmes.

    In fact, if you see a Moroccan beach flying the Blue Flag, that foundation is usually behind it.

    Lalla Latifa, the king’s mother (died 2024)

    Lalla Latifa was the second wife of Hassan II and the mother of his five children. She came from an Amazigh (Berber) family in the Khenifra region. Following the protocol of her time, she never held the title of queen and never appeared in official photos. She died in Rabat on 29 June 2024.

    Origins of the royal family in Morocco: from oasis to throne

    To understand the royal family in Morocco, you have to travel to the south-east. The story does not start in Rabat. It starts in a desert oasis, very close to the dunes of Merzouga.

    Sijilmassa and Rissani, the cradle of the dynasty

    The Alaouites are sharifs, meaning they claim descent from the Prophet Muhammad through his grandson Hassan. According to tradition, their ancestors came from Arabia to the Sijilmassa oasis around the 13th century. At that time Sijilmassa was a great caravan city and a gateway for the gold and salt trade with sub-Saharan Africa.

    Its ruins now lie beside the town of Rissani, in the Tafilalt. There you will find the mausoleum of Moulay Ali Cherif, regarded as the founder of the dynasty. If you plan a trip to the Sahara Desert in Morocco, Rissani is right on the way. Few travellers stop there, yet it gives the whole journey real meaning.

    Moulay Rachid and Moulay Ismail: unifying the country

    By the mid-17th century, Morocco was divided. Sultan Moulay Rachid took Fes in 1666 and brought the country under Alaouite rule. His brother, Moulay Ismail, reigned from 1672 to 1727, one of the longest reigns in Moroccan history.

    Moulay Ismail turned Meknes into a monumental capital, with walls, gates and vast granaries. He also built the royal stables of Heri es-Souani, which still impress with their scale. UNESCO listed the historic city of Meknes as a World Heritage Site in 1996.

    The first country to recognise the United States

    Here is a fact about the royal family in Morocco that American visitors love. In 1777, Sultan Mohammed ben Abdallah, an Alaouite ruler, opened Moroccan ports to American ships. Historians often describe this as the first recognition of the young United States by a foreign state. The two countries then signed a Treaty of Peace and Friendship in 1786.

    Later, in 1821, Sultan Moulay Slimane gave the US a building in the Tangier medina. Today it is the Tangier American Legation, the first American-owned property abroad. You can visit it on our tours from Tangier.

    Mohammed V: exile and independence

    In the 20th century, the dynasty lived through its most dramatic moment. Sultan Mohammed V, on the throne since 1927, backed the nationalist movement against the French protectorate. In 1953 the colonial authorities sent him into exile, first to Corsica and then to Madagascar.

    His return in November 1955 set off nationwide celebrations. In 1956 Morocco regained its independence, and the sultan soon took the title of king. That is why Mohammed V is so beloved and why his mausoleum in Rabat feels almost like a pilgrimage site.

    Hassan II: the king of the Green March

    Hassan II reigned from 1961 to 1999. He was a strong monarch with a very personal style of power. His reign included the Green March of 1975 and also the “years of lead”, marked by political repression. He commissioned the great Hassan II Mosque in Casablanca, opened in 1993. It is one of the few mosques in Morocco that non-Muslims can visit on a guided tour.

    Mohammed VI: modernisation and continuity

    Mohammed VI inherited a young and unequal country. He bet on infrastructure, renewable energy and greater social openness. In 2011, after the protests of the February 20 Movement, he proposed a new Constitution. That charter still defines the role of the monarchy today.

    How much power does the King of Morocco have?

    Many readers ask whether Morocco is an absolute monarchy. The short answer is no, but the king holds far more power than a European monarch. These articles of the 2011 Constitution are worth knowing:

    • Article 41: the king is Amir al-Mu’minin, the highest religious authority in Moroccan Islam.
    • Article 42: he is head of state, guarantor of the state’s continuity and arbiter between institutions.
    • Article 43: it sets out succession to the throne through the male line.
    • Article 46: the person of the king is inviolable and owed respect.
    • Article 47: the king appoints the head of government from the party that wins the legislative elections.

    In practice, the government runs day-to-day affairs. The king, however, keeps control over religion, defence and the broad lines of foreign policy. He also chairs the Council of Ministers. In short, Morocco is a constitutional monarchy, but its king governs rather than simply reigns.

    Religion is a central piece of that balance. To go deeper, read our guide to religion in Morocco, where we explain the role of the Commander of the Faithful.

    How succession works

    Succession in the royal family in Morocco is hereditary. The crown passes to the king’s eldest son, unless the king designates another son. If there were no male descendants, the throne would go to the closest male relative in the collateral line.

    Today the order is clear. First comes Moulay Hassan. Since the heir has no children yet, Prince Moulay Rachid would follow, and then his sons. The princesses do not inherit the throne, although they keep an institutional and social role.

    If a new king were a minor, the Constitution provides for a Regency Council. Moulay Hassan turned 23 in May 2026, so that scenario no longer applies.

    The royal family in Morocco in 2026: the year’s highlights

    This year brought several stories that show where the royal family in Morocco is heading. Here are the most relevant ones:

    • 13 April 2026: Moulay Hassan opens the Mohammed VI Tower, a 55-storey skyscraper that is already a new landmark between Rabat and Salé.
    • 30 April 2026: the heir opens the 31st International Book Fair in Rabat.
    • 2 May 2026: the king appoints Moulay Hassan as coordinator of the General Staff of the Royal Armed Forces.
    • 8 May 2026: the crown prince turns 23.
    • 30 July 2026: the 27th anniversary of the king’s accession is marked in M’diq-Fnideq, in the north, with FIFA President Gianni Infantino in attendance.

    Morocco World News reports that the king received officials, diplomats and national team players there. FIFA’s presence is no accident: the country is preparing the Morocco World Cup 2030, a project closely tied to the king’s image.

    A new holiday: Eid Al Wahda on 31 October

    In November 2025, the Royal Cabinet declared a new national holiday on 31 October, named Eid Al Wahda, or Unity Day. It marks the UN Security Council resolution on the Sahara. According to the official statement, royal speeches are now reserved for two occasions: Throne Day and the opening of Parliament.

    For travellers, this has a practical effect. 31 October 2026 will be the first full year of this holiday. Expect closed public offices and busier domestic travel that weekend.

    Meghan Markle in Morocco with the Moroccan royal family

    This is one of the most popular searches, and it often circulates with mistakes. Here are the facts. In February 2019, Prince Harry and Meghan Markle made a three-day official visit to Morocco. They stopped in Casablanca, at a girls’ boarding school in Asni in the Atlas Mountains, and in Rabat.

    On 25 February 2019, the couple had an audience with King Mohammed VI at his residence in Rabat. On arrival, they were greeted by Crown Prince Moulay Hassan, who was 15 at the time. Princesses Lalla Meryem and Lalla Hasna also joined the meeting. The couple handed the king letters from Queen Elizabeth II. So, unlike what some blogs claim, they did meet the monarch in person.

    During the visit, the couple stayed as guests of the king at a private royal residence. The trip showed the warmer, more modern face of the royal family in Morocco to the international press.

    The Moroccan royal family, Britain and the United States

    Ties between the Alaouite throne and the English-speaking world run deep. With the United States, the story begins in 1777, as explained above. The Tangier American Legation remains a powerful symbol of that friendship.

    With Britain, the links are both royal and diplomatic. Prince Moulay Rachid welcomed the then Prince Charles and Camilla to Rabat in April 2011. Harry and Meghan’s 2019 visit followed. British and American media regularly cover the royal family in Morocco, especially the heir’s rising profile.

    For English-speaking visitors, the most visible trace lies in the north. Tangier has long hosted writers, diplomats and artists. The king also spends long summer stays in his northern palaces.

    A royal route: palaces, mausoleums and imperial cities to visit

    Now for the practical part. You cannot enter the palaces where the royal family in Morocco lives, but you can follow its story across the country. Here are the must-see places, city by city.

    Rabat: the royal capital

    Rabat is the king’s official seat and the best place to start. These are the key stops:

    • Mausoleum of Mohammed V: Mohammed V and his sons Hassan II and Moulay Abdellah rest here. Entry is free. The mounted guards at the gate make one of the most iconic photos in Morocco.
    • Hassan Tower: the unfinished 12th-century minaret facing the mausoleum. Together they form a single site.
    • Royal Palace (Dar al-Makhzen): seen from the outside. You can often walk across the large Mechouar esplanade, always respecting the checkpoints.
    • Kasbah of the Udayas: not a royal residence, but it rounds off the visit with ocean views.

    UNESCO listed Rabat as a World Heritage Site in 2012, as both a modern capital and a historic city. Our tours from Casablanca almost always include Rabat on day one.

    Fes: the golden gates of the Royal Palace

    Fes holds one of the most photographed sights in Morocco. The seven gilded brass doors of Dar al-Makhzen face the Place des Alaouites. You cannot tour the palace, but the facade is worth the stop. Go early: morning light makes the metal glow and there are fewer groups.

    Next door, the Jewish quarter of the Mellah tells another side of royal history. The sultans protected the Jewish community right beside their residence. You will find all the details in our Fes Morocco guide and in what to do in Fez.

    Meknes: the city of Moulay Ismail

    Meknes is the forgotten imperial city, and that is exactly why people love it. The essentials:

    • Bab Mansour: one of the most beautiful monumental gates in North Africa.
    • Mausoleum of Moulay Ismail: one of the few shrines that usually admits non-Muslims to part of its courtyards. It has gone through long restorations, so check it is open before you go.
    • Heri es-Souani: the granaries and stables that, according to tradition, housed thousands of the sultan’s horses.

    Meknes pairs easily with the Roman ruins of Volubilis on the same day.

    Marrakech: a royal palace and palaces that are not royal

    Marrakech has a working Royal Palace near the Kasbah, which you can only see from outside. Here we must clear up a common mistake. The Bahia Palace was never an Alaouite royal residence; it belonged to a 19th-century grand vizier. Likewise, the El Badi Palace and the Saadian Tombs belong to the Saadian dynasty, which ruled before the Alaouites.

    Still, they are beautiful visits for understanding Moroccan court art. You will find the full walk in our Marrakech old town guide. To head south afterwards, see our tours from Marrakech.

    Casablanca: King Hassan II’s mosque

    The Hassan II Mosque rises over the Atlantic. It was a personal project of Hassan II and is one of the largest mosques in the world. Non-Muslims can enter on guided tours at set times. Dress modestly, with shoulders and knees covered. If in doubt, read what to wear in Morocco.

    Rissani: the founder’s mausoleum

    Rissani is the most authentic stop on this route. Here you find the mausoleum of Moulay Ali Cherif and the ruins of Sijilmassa. On market days, the Rissani souk remains one of the most traditional in the south. It also sits less than an hour from the Erg Chebbi dunes.

    So you can combine royal history and the desert in a single trip. We do exactly that on our 3-day tour from Fes to Merzouga and our 3-day desert tour from Errachidia.

    The north: Tangier, Tetouan and Ifrane

    The royal family in Morocco has residences in the north, where the king often spends the summer. Tetouan, with its white medina, is a good base to grasp that connection. In the Middle Atlas, Ifrane hosts another royal palace among cedar forests, in a town with an Alpine feel unlike anywhere else in Morocco. To round off the north, see our guide to Chefchaouen.

    Etiquette: what every visitor should know

    The royal family in Morocco is a serious subject for Moroccans. Moroccans discuss politics freely in many areas, but respect for the king is protected by the Constitution and the Penal Code. These tips will spare you awkward moments:

    • Do not joke about the king in public or on social media while you are in the country.
    • Treat portraits with care, as well as banknotes, which carry the royal effigy. Never tear them or use them in mocking photos.
    • Do not fly drones near palaces, barracks or residences. Drones require prior authorisation anywhere in Morocco.
    • Ask before photographing guards. Wide shots of the gates are usually fine; close-ups, not always.
    • Respect road closures. When the king travels, streets close without warning. It is normal and passes quickly.

    None of this affects your safety. Read more in our guide on whether Morocco is safe.

    The king in everyday life

    You will see the king everywhere. His portrait hangs in shops, banks and hotels. His effigy appears on dirham banknotes; to understand the new series, see our guide to the Moroccan dirham. In addition, Friday prayers in every mosque include a prayer for the monarch. That explains why the royal family in Morocco is not just a political institution but a daily symbol.

    Myths and common mistakes about the royal family in Morocco

    “The king’s wife is the queen”

    False. The title of queen does not exist in Morocco. Lalla Salma was the first royal wife with an official title, but as a princess.

    “The Bahia Palace is the king’s home”

    False. It was the residence of a 19th-century grand vizier. The Royal Palace of Marrakech is a different building and is closed to visitors.

    “The king is only a figurehead”

    False. Unlike European monarchs, the king keeps real power over religion, the army and foreign policy.

    “Meghan Markle never met the king”

    False. The audience with Mohammed VI took place in Rabat on 25 February 2019, with the heir and two princesses present.

    “The Alaouites are of Berber origin”

    Not exactly. The dynasty claims an Arab sharifian lineage from Arabia. However, the family is deeply intertwined with the Amazigh world: the king’s own mother was of Berber origin. We explain more in our guide to Berber Morocco.

    Frequently asked questions about the Moroccan royal family

    Is there a royal family in Morocco?

    Yes. Morocco is a constitutional monarchy ruled by the Alaouite dynasty. The current king is Mohammed VI and his heir is Crown Prince Moulay Hassan.

    What is the name of the King of Morocco?

    The King of Morocco is Mohammed VI. He has reigned since 23 July 1999 and was born in Rabat in 1963.

    How many children does the King of Morocco have?

    The king has two children: Crown Prince Moulay Hassan, born in 2003, and Princess Lalla Khadija, born in 2007.

    Who will be the next King of Morocco?

    The next king will be Crown Prince Moulay Hassan. He is Mohammed VI’s eldest son and the heir named under the Constitution.

    Is the King of Morocco married?

    The king married Salma Bennani in 2002. She has not appeared at official events since late 2017 and media reported a divorce, but the palace gave no public details.

    Where does the royal family in Morocco live?

    The royal family in Morocco has its official seat at the Royal Palace in Rabat. However, it uses palaces in several cities, such as Fes, Marrakech, Tetouan, Tangier and Ifrane, depending on the season.

    What religion is the Moroccan royal family?

    The Moroccan royal family is Sunni Muslim of the Maliki school. The king is also Commander of the Faithful, the country’s highest religious authority.

    Can you visit the Royal Palace in Morocco?

    You cannot tour the palaces in use. You can, however, see the gates in Fes and the Rabat palace grounds from outside, and enter the Mausoleum of Mohammed V.

    Is Morocco an absolute monarchy?

    No. It is a constitutional monarchy with an elected parliament and government, but the king keeps wide powers under the 2011 Constitution.

    When is Throne Day in Morocco?

    Throne Day is celebrated every 30 July. In 2026 it marked the 27th anniversary of Mohammed VI’s reign.
