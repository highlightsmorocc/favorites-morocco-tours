    Morocco: The Complete 2026 Guide to the Kingdom, Its Casbahs and Souks

    Morocco guide 2026: key facts, history, is Morocco rich, the safest place in Morocco, top sightseeing places, casbahs and Marrakech souk tips

    Favorites Morocco Tours · Updated: 17 September 2026

    Quick answer: Morocco is a kingdom in north-west Africa, only 14 km from Spain across the Strait of Gibraltar. Its capital is Rabat and its largest city is Casablanca. About 36.8 million people live there (2024 census). Travellers know Morocco for Marrakech, the Sahara, the Atlas Mountains, ancient kasbahs and lively souks.

    Morocco stays with you long after you fly home. In the morning, you smell cumin and mint in a Marrakech souk. A few days later, you watch the Sahara dunes turn orange at sunset, after crossing the snowy High Atlas.

    This guide puts everything in one place. First, you get the key facts about Morocco. Next, we answer the questions travellers ask Google every day. Is Morocco rich? Which is the safest place in Morocco? What is a casbah in Morocco? And what does “yalla habibi” mean? Finally, we share the best Morocco sightseeing places and tips to experience Morocco with confidence.

    Our team at Favorites Morocco Tours is based in Morocco. In fact, we drive these roads, walk these medinas and camp in these dunes every season. So every tip below comes from the ground, and every figure carries a date and a source.

    Morocco in 30 Seconds

    • Location: north-west Africa, with Atlantic and Mediterranean coasts.
    • Capital: Rabat. Largest city: Casablanca, with about 3.2 million people.
    • Population: 36,828,330 people (HCP census, September 2024).
    • Official languages: Arabic and Amazigh (Berber). Moroccan Arabic, called Darija, is the everyday language.
    • Currency: the Moroccan dirham (MAD).
    • Tourism: a record 19.8 million arrivals in 2025, then 14.1 million from January to August 2026 (Ministry of Tourism).
    • Time zone: Morocco returns to GMT (UTC+0) on 20 September 2026.
    • Heritage: nine UNESCO World Heritage Sites, from the medina of Fes to the ksar of Ait Ben Haddou.

    Where Is Morocco on the Map?

    Morocco sits in the north-west corner of Africa. The Atlantic Ocean washes its long western coast, while the Mediterranean Sea borders the north. At the narrowest point, only 14 km of water separates Morocco from Spain. In fact, on a clear day you can see Europe from the hills around Tangier.

    Algeria lies to the east, but that land border has been closed since 1994. Spain still holds two small cities on the northern coast, Ceuta and Melilla. To the south lies Western Sahara, which Morocco administers and considers part of the kingdom. However, its final status remains part of a UN-led process. In October 2025, UN Security Council Resolution 2797 pointed to Morocco’s 2007 autonomy plan as a basis for talks.

    For a deeper look at maps, borders and distances, read our full guide on where Morocco is located.

    Is Morocco in Africa or the Middle East?

    Morocco is an African country. In fact, it helped found the Organisation of African Unity in 1963, left in 1984 and rejoined the African Union in January 2017. At the same time, it belongs to the Arab League, so many people group it with the Middle East and North Africa (MENA).

    So the honest answer has two layers. On the map, it sits in Africa. In culture, however, it mixes Amazigh, Arab, African, Andalusian and Jewish roots. Our article on Moroccan African identity explains this mix in detail.

    How Big Is Morocco?

    Without Western Sahara, Morocco covers about 446,550 km², slightly more than California. With Western Sahara, official Moroccan figures reach about 710,850 km². Moreover, mountains fill much of the interior, from the Rif in the north to the Middle Atlas, High Atlas and Anti-Atlas.

    Jbel Toubkal, in the High Atlas, reaches 4,167 metres, the highest peak in North Africa. Then, beyond the mountains, lie the pre-Saharan valleys and the dunes of Erg Chebbi and Erg Chigaga. Our Atlas Mountains guide covers the high country in detail.

    A Short History of Morocco

    Every medina, kasbah and palace fits into a long timeline. Here is the short version.

    Amazigh Roots and Roman Volubilis

    The Amazigh people, often called Berbers, have lived in North Africa for thousands of years. Moreover, Jebel Irhoud in Morocco holds Homo sapiens fossils dated to about 315,000 years ago. Later, Phoenicians traded along the coast, and Rome ruled the north from Volubilis, near Meknes. Learn more in our guide to Berber culture in Morocco.

    The Great Islamic Dynasties

    Islam reached Morocco in the late 7th century. In 789, Idris I founded the first Moroccan dynasty, and Fes grew into a great centre of learning. After that, several dynasties shaped the country:

    • Almoravids (11th–12th centuries): they founded Marrakech around 1070.
    • Almohads (12th–13th centuries): they built the Koutoubia Mosque and the Hassan Tower in Rabat.
    • Merinids (13th–15th centuries): they filled Fes with carved madrasas.
    • Saadians (16th–17th centuries): they left the Saadian Tombs and El Badi Palace in Marrakech.
    • Alaouites (17th century to today): Sultan Moulay Ismail made Meknes his imperial capital, and the same family still reigns.

    Here is a little-known fact: in 1777, Morocco became one of the first countries to recognise the young United States. The 1786 Treaty of Friendship between the two nations is still in force.

    From Protectorate to Independence

    Later, in 1912, the Treaty of Fes placed most of Morocco under a French protectorate. Spain took zones in the north and south, and Tangier later became an international zone. After years of resistance, the country regained independence from France on 2 March 1956. Sultan Mohammed V later took the title of king, and his son Hassan II ruled from 1961 to 1999.

    Modern Morocco Under Mohammed VI

    King Mohammed VI came to the throne on 23 July 1999. Since then, the country has built motorways, ports and Africa’s first high-speed railway, Al Boraq. The 2011 constitution also made Amazigh an official language alongside Arabic. Today, the kingdom also co-hosts the 2030 FIFA World Cup with Spain and Portugal. Our Morocco World Cup 2030 guide explains what that means for visitors.

    People, Languages and Faith in Morocco

    How Many People Live in Morocco?

    The last national census counted 36,828,330 people on 1 September 2024. That is 8.8% more than in 2014. Most Moroccans now live in cities, led by Casablanca with about 3.2 million people. Next come Tangier, Fes and Marrakech, each with more than one million. Our guide to cities in Morocco ranks them all.

    What Language Do Moroccans Speak?

    Arabic and Amazigh are the two official languages. In daily life, however, most people speak Darija, a Moroccan form of Arabic. Moreover, the 2024 census found that 24.8% of Moroccans speak an Amazigh language, mainly Tachelhit, Tamazight or Tarifit. French remains common in business and hotels, Spanish is heard in the north, and English is spreading fast among young people. For the full picture, read our guide to the languages spoken in Morocco.

    Religion and Daily Rhythm

    Islam is the state religion, and the King holds the title Commander of the Faithful. The call to prayer sounds five times a day, while Friday is the main prayer day. The country also keeps a long Jewish heritage, with restored synagogues and mellahs (Jewish quarters) in Fes, Marrakech and Essaouira. Our article on religion in Morocco covers etiquette for visitors, including Ramadan.

    “Yalla Habibi” and Other Darija Words to Know

    You will hear “yalla habibi” in songs, on social media and sometimes in the street. So what does it mean? “Yalla” means “come on” or “let’s go”. “Habibi” means “my dear” or “my darling” when you speak to a man. When they speak to a woman, people say “habibti”. Put together, “yalla habibi” is a warm, playful way to say “come on, my friend, let’s go”.

    Moroccans know the phrase well from Egyptian and Middle Eastern pop songs and films. Still, local Darija has its own favourites. For example, a shopkeeper may call you “a sahbi” (my friend) or “a khoya” (my brother). A few words of Darija open doors fast, and people smile when you try.

    Is Morocco Rich? The Economy in Plain Numbers

    Many travellers search “Morocco rich or poor” before they book. The short answer: the World Bank classes Morocco as a lower-middle-income country, with a growing economy and real inequality. Yet you will also see luxury hotels, new stadiums and a high-speed train.

    Where the Money Comes From

    Phosphates are the backbone of the Moroccan economy. Specifically, the state group OCP turns them into fertilisers for world markets. In addition, the country has become Africa’s leading car producer, with Renault near Tangier and Stellantis in Kenitra. Besides, aerospace parts, textiles, citrus fruit, fish and renewable energy add more income.

    Tourism matters too. In the first eleven months of 2025, tourism receipts reached MAD 124 billion, up 19% on a year earlier. Money sent home by Moroccans abroad also supports millions of families. You can explore the detailed World Bank data on Morocco.

    The Gap Between Rich and Poor

    Morocco is not rich for everyone. Unemployment reached 9.5% in the second quarter of 2026, and youth jobless rates stay much higher. Indeed, in autumn 2025, young people marched in several cities to demand better schools and hospitals. Rural areas in the Atlas and the south still lag behind the big cities.

    On the other hand, progress is visible. New roads reach mountain villages, and after the 2023 Al Haouz earthquake, tens of thousands of homes were rebuilt. Therefore, the fair answer to “is Morocco rich?” is “richer every year, but unevenly”.

    What This Means for Your Trip

    For visitors, the country offers good value, although luxury riads can match top prices anywhere. Your spending also matters. When you hire local guides, buy from cooperatives and eat in family restaurants, your money stays in local hands. Our guide to the Moroccan dirham explains cash, cards and exchange rates.

    Top Morocco Sightseeing Places You Should Not Miss

    Morocco sightseeing places range from imperial cities to empty dunes. Below are the stops our travellers love most.

    1. Marrakech, the Red City

    Marrakech is the heart of most first trips. Start at Jemaa el-Fna, the great square that comes alive at dusk with storytellers and food stalls. Then visit the Koutoubia Mosque, the Bahia Palace, the Ben Youssef Madrasa and the Majorelle Garden. Our complete Marrakech travel guide and old town and new town guide cover every corner.

    2. Fes, the Spiritual Capital

    Fes el-Bali is one of the largest car-free urban areas in the world. Inside, you will find the Al-Qarawiyyin, founded in 859 by Fatima al-Fihri and often called the oldest working university. Next, climb to a terrace above the Chouara tannery to see the dye pits. Read our Fes Morocco guide and our list of things to do in Fez.

    3. Chefchaouen, the Blue Pearl

    Founded in 1471, Chefchaouen sits in the Rif Mountains and glows in shades of blue. It is a calm medina for a slow walk, and the Spanish Mosque offers the classic sunset view. See our Chefchaouen guide.

    4. Merzouga and the Sahara Desert

    The dunes of Erg Chebbi rise up to about 150 metres near Merzouga. Here, you ride a camel at sunset, sleep in a desert camp and wake up to silence. For many travellers, this is the highlight of the whole trip. Compare the two main dune seas in Erg Chebbi vs Erg Chigaga, and read our Sahara Desert Morocco guide.

    5. Ait Ben Haddou

    This fortified village of earth and straw has been a UNESCO site since 1987. Film crews also love it, from Lawrence of Arabia to Gladiator and Game of Thrones. You can see it on the way to the desert or on an Ait Ben Haddou day trip from Marrakech.

    6. The Atlas Mountains

    Only an hour from Marrakech, the High Atlas offers Berber villages, walnut groves and cool air. Imlil is the base for Toubkal treks, while the Ourika Valley is ideal for an easy day out. Try our Imlil day trip or our Ourika Valley day trip.

    7. Essaouira, the Windy City

    Essaouira is a white-and-blue port on the Atlantic. Its 18th-century medina is on the UNESCO list, and grilled fish, argan cooperatives and kitesurfing make it a perfect break. Visit it on our full-day trip from Marrakech.

    8. Casablanca and the Hassan II Mosque

    Casablanca is the economic capital of the country. Its star is the Hassan II Mosque, completed in 1993. Part of it stands over the Atlantic, and its minaret rises about 210 metres. Unlike most mosques in Morocco, it welcomes non-Muslim visitors on guided tours. Browse our tours from Casablanca.

    9. Rabat, the Calm Capital

    Walk from the Hassan Tower to the Mausoleum of Mohammed V. Then wander through the Kasbah of the Udayas, described below. Since 2012, Rabat has been on the UNESCO list. For context, read is Marrakech the capital of Morocco?

    10. Meknes and Volubilis

    Meknes was the imperial city of Moulay Ismail, and its gate, Bab Mansour, is one of the finest in North Africa. Just 30 km away, Volubilis shows Roman mosaics in open fields.

    11. Tangier and Asilah

    Tangier is the gateway from Europe. Its kasbah, its cafés and the Caves of Hercules draw artists and writers. Asilah, 45 minutes south, is a small white town famous for murals. Read our Asilah guide and browse tours from Tangier.

    12. Todra Gorge, Dades Valley and Ouzoud Falls

    The Todra Gorge narrows between rock walls around 300 metres high. Nearby, the Dades Valley winds past kasbahs and rose fields. In the Middle Atlas, the Ouzoud waterfalls drop about 110 metres. You can reach them on our Ouzoud waterfalls day trip.

    The Casbah in Morocco: Kasbahs and Ksars Explained

    Travellers often search for the “casbah in Morocco”. The word comes from the Arabic qasba, meaning a fortress or citadel. English and French writers spelled it “casbah”, while Moroccan signs usually say “kasbah”. Both words mean a fortified house or quarter built to protect a family, a tribe or a ruler.

    Kasbah, Casbah or Ksar: What Is the Difference?

    Generally, a kasbah is a single fortified building with high walls and corner towers. A ksar, by contrast, is a whole fortified village where many families lived together. In cities, “kasbah” can also mean the walled royal quarter, as in Rabat or Tangier. Note that the famous “Casbah of Algiers” is in Algeria, not in Morocco.

    Ait Ben Haddou: The Most Famous Ksar

    Ait Ben Haddou is technically a ksar, yet most people call it a kasbah. Its earthen houses climb a hill above the Ounila river. Caravans once passed here between the Sahara and Marrakech. Climb to the old granary at the top for a wide view of the valley.

    Taourirt Kasbah in Ouarzazate

    Taourirt stands in the centre of Ouarzazate. During the 19th century, the powerful Glaoui family expanded it. Inside, you can visit painted rooms and small courtyards.

    Telouet Kasbah

    Telouet hides on the old caravan road near the Tizi n’Tichka pass. Much of it is crumbling or under restoration. Inside, however, a few rooms still keep carved cedar ceilings and fine zellige tiles, which tell the story of the Glaoui pashas.

    Kasbah Amridil in Skoura

    Amridil sits in the palm grove of Skoura, near Ouarzazate. The same family has cared for it for generations, and it once appeared on a Moroccan 50-dirham banknote. Its small museum also shows olive presses, tools and old doors.

    Kasbah of the Udayas in Rabat

    The Almohads built this citadel in the 12th century, at the mouth of the Bou Regreg river. Its white and blue lanes lead to a terrace over the ocean, and the Andalusian Gardens below offer shade.

    Tips for Visiting a Casbah in Morocco

    • Go early in the morning or late in the afternoon for soft light and fewer groups.
    • Wear sturdy shoes, because earthen stairs can be uneven.
    • Ask before you photograph residents inside a ksar.
    • Hire a local guide at Ait Ben Haddou or Telouet to understand the history.

    Inside the Marrakech Souk: A Practical Guide

    The Marrakech souk is not one market but a maze of many, starting just north of Jemaa el-Fna. Each lane once held a single trade, and many still do. As a result, you can shop by craft. Most lane names begin with “Souk”, followed by the trade.

    How the Souks Are Organised

    • Semmarine: the main artery, with textiles, lamps and souvenirs.
    • Rahba Kedima (Spice Square): spices, herbal remedies, baskets and hats.
    • El Attarine: brass, metalwork and perfume stalls.
    • Haddadine: blacksmiths who hammer lanterns and iron furniture.
    • Teinturiers (dyers): skeins of coloured wool hang above your head.
    • Cherratine: leather bags, belts and poufs.
    • Smata: babouches, the soft Moroccan slippers.
    • Criée Berbère: carpet sellers and rug auctions.

    Opening Hours and Best Time to Go

    Most stalls open around 9 or 10 a.m. and close around 8 or 9 p.m. Mornings are calmer for serious shopping, while late afternoons feel more atmospheric. However, on Friday many shops close for a few hours around midday prayers. During Ramadan, hours shift later into the evening.

    How to Bargain in a Marrakech Souk

    Bargaining is normal and, above all, friendly. First, decide what the item is worth to you before you ask the price. Then offer a lower figure with a smile and meet in the middle. If the seller will not move, simply say “la, shukran” and walk away. Often, the price drops as you leave.

    Remember one rule, though. If you name a price and the seller accepts, you should buy. To learn fair prices first, visit the government-run Ensemble Artisanal on Avenue Mohammed V. Since its prices are fixed, it gives you a useful benchmark.

    What to Buy

    • Argan oil from women’s cooperatives (check the label and the smell).
    • Hand-woven Berber rugs and kilims.
    • Leather babouches and bags.
    • Ceramic tagines from Safi or Fes.
    • Spices such as ras el hanout, cumin and saffron from Taliouine.

    Before you shop, read our guide to SIM cards and artisan factories in Morocco. It explains how cooperatives work and how to avoid pressure sales.

    Common Mistakes in the Marrakech Souk

    • Following a stranger who says the square “is closed” or offers to show you a “free” tannery.
    • Paying for photos with snake charmers or chained monkeys. Please skip them, because the animals suffer.
    • Letting a henna artist start before you agree on a price.
    • Carrying your phone in a back pocket in crowded lanes.

    What Is the Safest Place in Morocco?

    Morocco is one of the safest countries in Africa for visitors. The HelloSafe 2026 index ranked it first in Africa. The US State Department rates the country at Level 2, “Exercise Increased Caution” (reissued on 4 September 2026). That is the same level it gives France and Spain. You can read the current US travel advisory for Morocco. Violent crime against tourists is rare; the main risks are petty theft, scams and road traffic.

    The Safest Places in Morocco for Travellers

    • Essaouira: relaxed, compact and friendly. Many travellers call it the safest place in Morocco.
    • Chefchaouen: small, quiet and easy to walk, even for solo travellers.
    • Rabat: the capital has a visible police presence and far less hassle than Marrakech.
    • Agadir and Taghazout: modern beach towns built for tourism.
    • Asilah: a peaceful seaside town with a gentle pace.
    • Ifrane: a clean mountain town in the Middle Atlas, often called “the Switzerland of Morocco”.
    • Merzouga desert camps: very safe when you book with a licensed operator and stay with your guide.

    Marrakech and Fes are safe too. However, they are busier, so you need more street smarts. For a full breakdown, read is Morocco safe? and our summary of Morocco travel warnings.

    Safety Tips That Work

    • Use licensed guides; they wear an official badge.
    • Ask taxi drivers in cities to use the meter, or agree on a price first.
    • Drink alcohol only in licensed hotels, bars and restaurants.
    • Dress modestly, especially in rural areas. Our what to wear in Morocco guide helps.
    • Avoid political rallies and large gatherings, especially around the legislative elections of 23 September 2026.
    • Save the emergency numbers: 19 (police in cities), 177 (Royal Gendarmerie outside cities) and 15 (ambulance and fire).

    How to Experience Morocco Like a Local

    Here are simple ways to experience Morocco beyond the postcards.

    Sleep in a Riad

    A riad is a traditional house built around an inner courtyard. Most have only a few rooms, a fountain and a roof terrace, so they feel intimate. Breakfast on the terrace, with msemen pancakes and fresh orange juice, is a daily joy.

    Take a Hammam

    The hammam is Morocco’s steam bath. You sweat, scrub with black soap and a kessa glove, then rinse. Local hammams are cheap and social, while spa hammams are private and calm. Read our Moroccan hammam guide first.

    Share a Meal

    First, try a slow-cooked tagine, harira soup and Friday couscous. Then finish with sweet mint tea, poured from high above the glass. Our guides to traditional Moroccan dishes and cuisine in Morocco list what to order. For eating out, see our Marrakech restaurant guide.

    Spend a Night in the Desert

    Nothing compares with a night under Saharan stars. Ride a camel at sunset, then gather around a fire for Berber drums. Read about Merzouga camel trekking to choose the right trip.

    Time Your Trip With a Festival

    • Fes Festival of World Sacred Music: usually in May.
    • Rose Festival in Kelaat M’Gouna: May, when the Dades roses bloom.
    • Gnaoua World Music Festival in Essaouira: usually in June.

    Practical Morocco Travel Tips for 2026

    When Is the Best Time to Visit Morocco?

    Spring (March to May) and autumn (September to November) are the sweet spots, with warm days and mild nights. In contrast, summer is very hot in Marrakech and the desert, although the coast stays pleasant. Finally, winter brings sunny days, cold nights and snow on the Atlas. Read our full guide to the best time to visit Morocco.

    Do You Need a Visa?

    Citizens of the UK, the US, Canada, Australia and EU countries can usually visit Morocco without a visa for up to 90 days. Also, your passport should be valid for your whole stay. Rules change, so check with a Moroccan consulate first.

    Morocco Time Zone: The 2026 Change

    On the night of 19 to 20 September 2026, Morocco turns its clocks back one hour and returns to GMT (UTC+0). This ends eight years of GMT+1, as Morocco World News confirmed. As a result, sunset comes one hour earlier on the clock. So plan your medina walks and desert drives with that in mind. Check the live clock in our guide to what time it is in Morocco now.

    Getting There and Getting Around

    Direct flights link the country with most of Europe and several cities in North America and the Middle East. Compare routes in our guides to flights to Morocco and flights to Marrakech.

    Inside the country, trains connect Tangier, Rabat, Casablanca, Fes and Marrakech. Moreover, the Al Boraq high-speed train links Tangier and Casablanca in about 2 hours 10 minutes. Timetables are on the ONCF website. For the desert and the mountains, however, a private car with a driver is far easier.

    Money, SIM Cards and Connectivity

    You can only get dirhams inside the country. ATMs are common in cities but rare in desert villages, so carry cash before you head south. Local SIM cards from Maroc Telecom, Orange or inwi are also cheap.

    Frequently Asked Questions About Morocco

    Where is Morocco?

    Morocco is in north-west Africa. It faces the Atlantic Ocean to the west and the Mediterranean Sea to the north. Spain lies only 14 km away across the Strait of Gibraltar, and Algeria borders it to the east.

    Is Morocco a country in Africa?

    Yes. Morocco is an African country and a member of the African Union since January 2017. It is also part of the Arab world, so people often include it in the MENA region.

    Is Morocco a rich country?

    Morocco is a lower-middle-income country, according to the World Bank. In addition, the IMF projects a GDP of about US$194 billion in 2026. Wealth is unevenly shared, and rural areas remain poorer than big cities.

    What is Morocco famous for?

    Morocco is famous for Marrakech, the Sahara dunes, the blue town of Chefchaouen, the medina of Fes, kasbahs, mint tea, tagines, argan oil and colourful souks.

    What is the safest place in Morocco?

    Essaouira, Chefchaouen, Rabat, Asilah and Agadir are among the safest places in Morocco for visitors. Big cities are also safe, but you need more awareness of scams and pickpockets.

    What does “yalla habibi” mean?

    “Yalla habibi” means “come on, my dear” or “let’s go, my friend”. “Yalla” means “let’s go”, and “habibi” means “my dear” when you address a man.

    What is a casbah in Morocco?

    A casbah, or kasbah, is a fortified house or citadel with thick earthen walls and towers. Famous examples include Taourirt, Telouet, Amridil and the Kasbah of the Udayas.

    What time does the Marrakech souk open?

    Most stalls in the Marrakech souk open around 9 or 10 a.m. and close around 8 or 9 p.m. Many shops pause on Friday around midday prayers.

    How many people live in Morocco?

    The 2024 census counted 36,828,330 people in Morocco. Casablanca is the largest city, with about 3.2 million residents.

    How many days do you need in Morocco?

    Seven to ten days is ideal for a first trip. That gives you time for Marrakech, the Sahara, Fes and one extra stop, such as Chefchaouen or Essaouira.

    Final Thoughts: Your Morocco Story Starts Here

    Morocco rewards curious travellers. In one week, you can bargain in a Marrakech souk, explore a casbah on the old caravan road and fall asleep in the Sahara. The country is welcoming, safe when you travel smart and more affordable than most people expect.
