    Traditional Moroccan Clothing: Djellaba, Kaftan, Takchita and Babouches

    Traditional Moroccan clothing explained: djellaba, caftan takchita selham and babouches plus fabrics, prices and where to buy them in Morocco.

    Favorites Morocco Tours · Updated: 5 September 2026

    Traditional Moroccan clothing is one of the first things travellers notice, and one of the last things they understand. You land in Marrakech, you walk into the medina, and the street fills with hooded robes, embroidered gowns and pointed yellow slippers. Some of it is everyday wear. Some is ceremonial. Almost none is a costume.

    This guide explains every major garment, piece by piece: what each item is called, who wears it, when it appears and how it is made. You will also learn to tell a hand-finished piece from a machine-made souvenir before you pay.

    The timing matters, too. In December 2025, UNESCO added the Moroccan caftan to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. That decision turned a wardrobe into a protected craft, and it changed how Moroccans themselves talk about what they wear.

    If you are planning a trip, pair this article with our practical packing guide, what to wear in Morocco. That one covers your suitcase. This one covers the culture.

    What traditional Moroccan clothing actually means

    Traditional Moroccan clothing is not a single style. Instead, it is a layered system built from Amazigh, Arab, Andalusian, Saharan and Ottoman influences, refined over roughly twelve centuries. Each wave left something behind, and Moroccan tailors kept the parts that worked.

    A wardrobe built by geography

    Morocco stretches from the Mediterranean to the Sahara. Consequently, the clothing had to solve very different problems. Wool answered the cold of the Middle Atlas. Loose cotton answered the heat of the Draa. White linen answered the Atlantic humidity.

    A wardrobe built by faith and custom

    Modesty shaped the cut of nearly every garment. Sleeves stayed long and hems reached the ankle. However, the result was never plain. Moroccan artisans poured their creativity into colour, braid and embroidery instead.

    Everyday dress versus ceremonial dress

    Most Moroccans separate their wardrobe into two categories. Daily wear means the djellaba, the gandoura and the babouche. Ceremonial wear means the caftan, the takchita and the jabador. Understanding that split is the fastest way to read a Moroccan street.

    What is not traditional

    Harem pants, “genie” costumes and cheap synthetic robes sold near the main squares are not traditional Moroccan clothing. Instead, they are tourist products. Real pieces come from named workshops, and locals will tell you exactly which ones.

    UNESCO and the caftan: why 2025 was a turning point

    On 10 December 2025, in New Delhi, the 20th session of UNESCO’s Intergovernmental Committee for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage inscribed “Moroccan Caftan: art, traditions and skills” on the Representative List.

    What UNESCO actually recognised

    The inscription does not protect one dress. Instead, it protects a chain of skills. UNESCO’s file describes weavers who produce brocade, velvet and silk, tailors who build the structure, and specialists who make the buttons, braids and embroidery.

    Why it mattered so much in Morocco

    Ownership of the caftan had been publicly disputed in the region for years. As a result, the file carried real emotional weight. Moroccan officials described the recognition as confirmation of centuries-old know-how, and social media reacted for weeks.

    What it changes for visitors

    Practically speaking, it raises the profile of workshops. More cooperatives now advertise the craft chain openly. If you want to see traditional Moroccan clothing being made rather than sold, ask your driver-guide for an atelier rather than a shop. Our article on artisan factories and cooperatives explains how those visits work.

    The reference file

    You can read the official entry on the UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage list, file number 02077. It is short, and it is worth five minutes before you shop.

    The djellaba: Morocco’s everyday robe

    If you remember only one item of traditional Moroccan clothing, remember the djellaba. It is the long, loose, hooded robe you will see on men and women in every city, village and market in the country.

    The hood has a name and a purpose

    The pointed hood is called the qob. However, it is not decorative. Farmers pull it up against sun, wind and sand, and city dwellers pull it up against winter rain. In the Sahara, it doubles as protection during a sandstorm.

    Men’s djellabas

    Men’s versions stay sober. Expect beige, grey, brown, cream, olive or black. The decoration is limited to fine braid running down the front opening and around the neckline. Friday prayer is when you will see the best ones.

    Women’s djellabas

    Women’s djellabas are a different world. They arrive in every colour, and they carry embroidery on the cuffs, the hem and the placket. Moreover, tailoring has become slimmer over the last twenty years, with belted and buttoned versions now common in Casablanca and Rabat.

    Winter wool versus summer cotton

    Winter djellabas use heavy wool, and mountain versions can weigh several kilos. Summer djellabas use light cotton, linen or a cotton-viscose blend. If you visit between June and September, buy the light one. Our guide to the best time to visit Morocco explains the seasonal swings in detail.

    How to buy one that fits

    Djellabas run long by design, and the hem should sit just above the ankle. Try it on over your normal clothes, because that is how Moroccans wear it. Then raise both arms, since a badly cut shoulder shows immediately.

    The caftan: the ceremonial dress of Moroccan women

    The caftan is the crown of traditional Moroccan clothing. In other words, it is a long, one-piece tunic with a central opening, and it is worn for weddings, religious feasts, engagements and family celebrations.

    Where the caftan came from

    The garment reached Morocco through Andalusian and Ottoman routes, and Moroccan tailors reshaped it completely. By the sixteenth century, Fez had become the capital of caftan making, and it still holds that reputation today.

    What makes a caftan expensive

    Fabric sets the floor, and handwork sets the ceiling. Brocade, velvet, silk and hand-woven mlifa all cost more than polyester. Above that, every hand-stitched button and every metre of hand-rolled braid adds hours of labour.

    The colour code

    Colour carries meaning at Moroccan celebrations. White suggests purity and appears at religious ceremonies. Green carries a blessing. Deep red and gold dominate weddings, while pastels now suit engagement parties.

    Who wears a caftan today

    Nearly every Moroccan woman owns at least one, and brides traditionally change into a series of them across a single wedding night. Foreign guests are welcome to wear one, and hosts usually consider it a compliment.

    The takchita: two layers, one silhouette

    Travellers new to traditional Moroccan clothing often confuse the caftan with the takchita. The difference is simple, and once you see it you cannot unsee it. A caftan is one piece. A takchita is two.

    The tahtiya

    The under-layer is called the tahtiya. Usually, it is cut from fine but plain fabric, and it carries almost no decoration. Its job is to give the silhouette its shape.

    The dfina

    The outer layer is the dfina. This is where the workshop shows off, with embroidery, beadwork, sequins and sometimes semi-precious stones. Often the dfina is semi-transparent, so the colour of the tahtiya glows through it.

    The mdamma belt

    A wide belt called the mdamma closes the look. It matches the dfina, and it is frequently the single most expensive component. Master belt makers in Fez still hand-embroider them with gold thread.

    Sfifa and aqad

    The closures deserve their own mention. In particular, sfifa is the flat braid that frames the opening, and aqad are the small handmade loop-and-knot buttons. Counting them is the fastest quality test in any shop.

    When you will see one

    Takchitas belong to weddings, henna nights and major family events. Furthermore, they appear at diplomatic dinners. Hillary Clinton famously wore one at a state dinner for the Moroccan king in 2000.

    The gandoura and the fouqia: comfort first

    Not all traditional Moroccan clothing is formal. For example, the gandoura is the country’s answer to a hot afternoon, and Moroccan men wear it at home constantly.

    What a gandoura is

    A gandoura is a short-sleeved, hoodless tunic that falls to mid-calf. It is usually cotton or linen, and it is cut wide so that air moves freely underneath.

    Where you will see it

    You will see gandouras in the Draa and Ziz valleys, in Saharan towns like Rissani and Merzouga, and inside almost every riad courtyard in summer. Guides working in the dunes often wear a light blue or white one. Read our comparison of Erg Chebbi and Erg Chigaga if you plan to spend a night out there.

    The fouqia

    The fouqia is a lighter indoor tunic, and Moroccans treat it roughly the way Europeans treat a housecoat. It is comfortable, it is cheap, and it is rarely sold to tourists because nobody thinks to offer it.

    Why travellers love it

    A gandoura packs flat and weighs nothing. Therefore it is the most practical souvenir on this list. Buy a natural fibre, and it will still be in your wardrobe in ten years.

    The jabador: the two-piece Moroccan men wear now

    The jabador has quietly become the most popular ceremonial menswear in Morocco. It is a two-piece set, and younger men often prefer it to a full djellaba.

    The structure

    A jabador pairs a hip-length or knee-length tunic with matching straight trousers. In addition, the tunic carries a mandarin collar, a short placket and a row of aqad buttons.

    Fabrics and colours

    Linen and cotton dominate summer versions. Meanwhile, wool blends and jacquards handle winter weddings. Navy, ivory, sand, sage and burgundy sell best.

    Women’s jabador

    A women’s version exists, and it has grown fast since about 2015. It offers the embroidery of a caftan with the freedom of trousers, which suits younger guests at long celebrations.

    Buying advice

    Check the trouser rise and the tunic length together. A good tailor in the medina will adjust both in a day, and the alteration usually costs a fraction of the garment.

    The selham: the hooded cape of kings

    The selham, also called a burnous, is the most dramatic piece of traditional Moroccan clothing. It is a sleeveless hooded cape, cut in a wide circle, and it is worn over everything else.

    Royal associations

    Moroccan kings appear in a white selham at official ceremonies, which fixed the garment in the national imagination. Consequently, a fine white selham signals authority in a way no other item does.

    Everyday versions

    Away from the palace, the selham is pure function. Shepherds in the Middle Atlas wear thick brown or grey wool versions against snow, and they sleep in them during transhumance.

    How it is made

    The best examples come from a single piece of hand-woven wool, and the seam runs down the back only. Finally, a hand-twisted cord finishes the hood point.

    Where to find a real one

    Look in the wool souks of Fez, Sefrou and Khenifra rather than in Marrakech’s main streets. Our tours from Fes pass through exactly that kind of workshop territory.

    The haik: the white veil that almost disappeared

    The haik is the ghost of traditional Moroccan clothing. Until the 1960s, women across the north wrapped themselves in this long white or cream cloth before leaving the house.

    What it looked like

    A haik was a single uncut rectangle of wool, cotton or silk, roughly five metres long. Women draped it over the head and body, then held it closed at the chin.

    Regional names

    People in Tangier and Tetouan used the word haik. Further south, similar wraps were called izar or lizar. Each city had its own weave, and locals could identify a woman’s origin from the cloth alone.

    Why it faded

    Urban life and the arrival of the tailored djellaba replaced it. Nevertheless, the haik survives in the far north, in Chefchaouen’s older generation, and at cultural festivals. See our guide to Chefchaouen for that corner of the country.

    Collecting one

    Antique haiks appear in Tetouan and Rabat antique dealers. Check for moth damage along the folds, and expect natural creamy tones rather than bright white.

    Babouches: the yellow slippers of Fez

    No survey of traditional Moroccan clothing is complete without footwear. In particular, the babouche, known in Moroccan Arabic as belgha, is a backless leather slipper with a flattened heel.

    The classic yellow pair

    Plain yellow babouches are the men’s standard, and they carry real social weight. Older men still buy a new pair before Eid, and the colour has stayed constant for centuries.

    Women’s cherbil

    The women’s ceremonial version is called cherbil. These are embroidered in silk and gold thread, often with a small heel, and they are made to match a caftan exactly.

    How they are made

    Real babouches start in the tanneries. Fez’s Chouara tannery still uses stone vats, natural dyes and traditional lime treatment, and the leather then passes to specialist slipper makers in the surrounding streets.

    Goat, sheep or camel

    Goat leather is soft and takes dye brilliantly. Sheepskin feels warmer. Camel leather is thicker and lasts longest, although it needs a week of wear before it softens.

    The quality test

    Turn the slipper over. Hand-stitched soles show slightly irregular stitching and a leather insole. Glued synthetic versions smell of solvent and separate within a season, so check both before you agree a price.

    Sizing

    Babouches stretch about half a size. Buy them snug rather than roomy, because a loose pair will slide off within weeks.

    Headwear: tarbouch, rezza and Rif straw hats

    Head coverings complete the picture, and each one signals something specific about region, age and occasion.

    The tarbouch

    The red felt cylinder known internationally as a fez is called tarbouch in Morocco. In fact, it takes its foreign name from the city of Fez, which supplied the crimson dye. Men wear it at religious ceremonies and national holidays.

    The rezza

    The rezza is a long turban cloth, wound around the head in a style that changes from valley to valley. Moreover, Saharan men favour indigo blue, and the dye famously rubs off on the skin.

    The taguelmoust

    Further south, the taguelmoust covers both head and face. It is the classic desert wrap, and your guides in Merzouga will happily teach you the technique. Our Merzouga camel trekking guide covers that experience.

    Rif straw hats

    In the northern Rif, women wear wide-brimmed straw hats trimmed with wool pompoms in red, white and blue. They are functional field hats, and they are among the most photographed items in the country.

    Women’s scarves

    Many Moroccan women cover their hair with a light scarf, while many do not. Both are normal, and neither carries judgement in the street. Our article on religion in Morocco gives the broader context.

    Amazigh dress and silver jewellery

    Amazigh communities shaped traditional Moroccan clothing long before the imperial cities did, and their pieces look nothing like the urban caftan.

    The handira

    The handira is a wedding cape woven by women of the Middle Atlas. Sequins cover the surface, and their flash is meant to deflect the evil eye. Brides wear it once, then the family keeps it as an heirloom.

    The fibula

    Amazigh women fastened their cloth with triangular silver brooches called tizerzai, joined by a chain. In practice, they served as clasps, as savings and as identity markers at once.

    Silver over gold

    Rural Amazigh jewellery favours silver, amber, coral and enamel rather than gold. Old pieces from the Anti-Atlas and the Nador region now sell to collectors across Europe.

    Tattoo and textile motifs

    Diamonds, chevrons and stylised eyes repeat across weaving, jewellery and older facial tattoos. They form a visual language, and elders can still read most of it. Our guide to Amazigh culture in Morocco explores those symbols further.

    Where to see it living

    Weekly souks in the High Atlas and the Anti-Atlas still show real Amazigh dress. A day trip to the Ourika Valley or the Imlil Valley puts you in that world within an hour of Marrakech.

    The fabrics behind the garments

    Understanding cloth is understanding price. Fortunately, traditional Moroccan clothing uses a small, consistent set of materials, and each one behaves differently.

    Wool

    Mountain wool from the Middle Atlas produces the warmest djellabas and selhams. It smells faintly of lanolin when new, which is a good sign rather than a fault.

    Mlifa

    Mlifa is a dense, felted wool cloth, and it is the aristocrat of Moroccan winter fabrics. As a result, a mlifa djellaba holds a sharp line and lasts decades.

    Sabra

    Sabra is often sold as “cactus silk”, although most of it is viscose. It shines beautifully and dyes well, so use it for cushions and scarves rather than assuming it is a plant fibre.

    Brocade and velvet

    Ceremonial caftans rely on brocade woven with metallic thread and on heavy velvet. Moreover, both come mainly from specialist looms and importers, and both drive the final price.

    Cotton and linen

    Summer garments use plain cotton, linen and blends. In addition, they wash easily, they breathe, and they suit travellers far better than synthetics in July. See our notes on climate in the complete trip to Morocco planner.

    Sfifa, aqad and randa: the handwork that sets the price

    Two caftans can look identical on a hanger and differ tenfold in price. In fact, the reason is almost always the trim.

    Sfifa

    Sfifa is the narrow flat braid that outlines every opening. Moreover, hand-made sfifa uses twisted silk thread, and a single metre can take an artisan an hour.

    Aqad

    Aqad are the knotted loop buttons. Furthermore, a ceremonial caftan can carry sixty of them down the front, and each one is tied by hand.

    Randa

    Randa is a decorative hand-worked edging, often in contrasting silk. It appears at cuffs and hems, and it is the detail Moroccan grandmothers check first.

    Terz embroidery

    Terz means embroidery, and each city guards its own style. Fez favours dense monochrome geometry. Rabat uses flowing floral curves. Tetouan works in silk with Andalusian motifs, while Azemmour produces figurative designs unique in North Africa.

    How to price handwork

    Turn the garment inside out. Then, hand embroidery shows a slightly imperfect reverse with visible thread paths. Machine work shows a flat, identical, glued-looking back.

    Region by region: how the map changes the wardrobe

    Traditional Moroccan clothing shifts every few hundred kilometres. Once you learn the pattern, you can guess where someone comes from before they speak.

    Fez and the imperial north

    Fez is the capital of formal dress. Its caftans are structured, its embroidery is dense and monochrome, and its tailors still train through long apprenticeships. Our Fes tours reach those workshops directly.

    Rabat and Salé

    Rabat produces softer, more floral embroidery, and the city gave its name to the rbati style. In addition, court dressmakers historically worked here, so the finish tends to be extremely clean.

    Tetouan, Tangier and the Rif

    The north keeps its Andalusian inheritance. For example, silk embroidery, striped fouta skirts and straw hats appear across the Rif countryside. Start from our tours from Tangier if the north is your priority.

    Marrakech and the Haouz plain

    Marrakech dresses louder. Colours run brighter, cuts run looser, and the souks mix Amazigh silver with urban tailoring. Read the complete Marrakech travel guide before you shop there.

    The Souss and the Anti-Atlas

    Around Taroudant, Tiznit and Tafraoute, indigo, saffron yellow and heavy silver dominate. Moreover, Tiznit remains the country’s silver capital, and its jewellery souk is worth a detour.

    The Sahara and the south-east

    Desert dress prioritises coverage. For that reason, long white or blue gandouras, wide turbans and loose trousers handle sun and sand. You will meet that style on any private Sahara desert tour.

    When Moroccans actually wear traditional dress

    Visitors sometimes assume traditional Moroccan clothing is daily uniform. In reality, its use follows a clear calendar and a clear social logic.

    Fridays

    Friday is the strongest weekly signal. As a result, men wear a clean djellaba and yellow babouches to the mosque, and the medinas fill with white and beige around midday.

    Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha

    Both feasts trigger a nationwide wardrobe refresh. In practice, families buy new djellabas for the children, and tailors work through the final nights of Ramadan without sleeping much.

    Weddings

    A Moroccan wedding is a fashion marathon. In fact, the bride may change outfit five to seven times, moving between regional caftans, a green Fassi look, an Amazigh set and a white gown.

    Religious and family milestones

    Circumcision ceremonies, name-giving parties on the seventh day, and Quran memorisation celebrations all call for formal dress. As a result, guests dress up, and photographs matter enormously.

    Ordinary days

    On a normal Tuesday, most urban Moroccans wear jeans, shirts and trainers. Traditional pieces stay for home, for cold evenings and for anything that counts.

    Traditional Moroccan clothing on the modern runway

    The craft is not frozen. On the contrary, Moroccan designers have pushed these garments into contemporary fashion, and the results now sell internationally.

    Caftan shows

    Annual caftan shows in Marrakech and Casablanca function as the industry’s shop window. Designers reinterpret sleeves, necklines and belts every season while keeping the sfifa and aqad language intact.

    The djellaba as streetwear

    Young Moroccan labels now cut the djellaba shorter, add pockets and use denim or technical fabric. Consequently, the robe has stopped reading as old-fashioned to people under thirty.

    International influence

    Yves Saint Laurent, who kept a home in Marrakech for decades, drew openly on Moroccan colour and cut. His Jardin Majorelle remains one of the city’s most visited sites.

    Export and e-commerce

    Moroccan ateliers now ship worldwide, and the UNESCO listing has strengthened the “made in Morocco” argument. Copies still circulate, so provenance has become a selling point.

    Where to buy: souks, cooperatives and tailors

    Buying traditional Moroccan clothing well is mostly about choosing the right place before you start negotiating.

    The medina souks

    Marrakech and Fez offer the widest choice. Prices open high, and bargaining is expected. Aim to settle somewhere between forty and sixty percent of the first number.

    Cooperatives

    Women’s cooperatives sell at fixed prices, and a larger share reaches the makers. Quality is usually consistent, and you can watch the work happen. Our day trips from Marrakech often include one.

    Commissioning a tailor

    A medina tailor will make a djellaba or jabador to measure in two to four days. Beforehand, bring a photo, agree the fabric separately, and confirm the delivery date in writing.

    Typical prices

    Expect roughly 250-600 MAD for a simple cotton djellaba, 700-2,000 MAD for wool, 150-400 MAD for honest babouches, and anywhere from 1,500 to 30,000 MAD for a caftan. In each case, handwork explains the range.

    Five checks before you pay

    First, read the fibre. Second, count the aqad buttons. Third, inspect the reverse of the embroidery. Fourth, test the seams by pulling gently. Fifth, ask where it was made, and watch how quickly the answer comes.

    Getting it home

    Fold garments flat, and pack babouches with paper inside to keep their shape. Most shops will vacuum-pack a caftan on request. Check our flights to Morocco guide for baggage planning.

    What travellers can and should wear

    You are not required to wear traditional Moroccan clothing anywhere in the country. Nevertheless, understanding the codes makes daily life smoother.

    Is it appropriate for visitors

    Yes. Moroccans generally read a well-worn djellaba as respect rather than imitation. The exception is wearing a ceremonial caftan casually in the street, which simply looks odd.

    Practical modesty

    Covered shoulders and knees work everywhere, and they attract far less attention in medinas and villages. By contrast, beach resorts and Marrakech’s new town are considerably more relaxed.

    Mosques

    Non-Muslims cannot enter most working mosques in Morocco. Hassan II in Casablanca is the famous exception, and it enforces covered shoulders, long trousers or skirts, and removable shoes.

    Desert nights

    Sahara evenings drop sharply, even in summer. Therefore, a wool djellaba or a selham outperforms a fleece, and it doubles as a blanket in camp.

    Hammam etiquette

    Traditional bathhouses have their own dress rules, and swimwear bottoms are standard for visitors. In any case, our Moroccan hammam guide covers the whole ritual.

    Caring for what you bring home

    Traditional Moroccan clothing lasts for decades when it is handled properly, and it fails fast when it is not.

    Washing

    Hand-wash cotton and linen in cool water. By contrast, send wool, brocade and anything embroidered to a dry cleaner instead, and mention the metallic thread.

    Storage

    Store caftans on wide padded hangers, and cover them in cotton rather than plastic. Cedar blocks handle moths without staining silk.

    Leather

    Babouches need occasional neutral leather cream. Avoid soaking them, and let them dry away from radiators so the sole keeps its shape.

    Silver

    Clean Amazigh silver gently with a soft cloth. Otherwise, aggressive polish removes the dark patina that gives old pieces their character and their value.

    Reading about textiles is one thing. By contrast, watching a tailor knot sixty aqad buttons is another entirely.

    Frequently asked questions about traditional Moroccan clothing

    What is traditional Moroccan clothing called?

    There is no single name, because Morocco uses a family of garments. The hooded robe is a djellaba. The ceremonial one-piece gown is a caftan. The two-piece ceremonial gown is a takchita. The men’s two-piece set is a jabador, the hooded cape is a selham, and the leather slippers are babouches or belgha.

    What is the difference between a caftan and a takchita?

    A caftan is one layer. A takchita is two: a plain under-dress called the tahtiya and a decorated over-dress called the dfina, usually closed with a wide mdamma belt. Takchitas are heavier, dressier and more expensive, so brides and mothers of the bride tend to choose them.

    Do Moroccan men still wear the djellaba every day?

    Many do, particularly in smaller cities, in rural areas and among older generations. In Casablanca or Rabat, however, most men wear Western clothes during the working week and switch to a djellaba on Friday, at Eid and at family events.

    Can tourists wear traditional Moroccan clothing?

    Yes, and Moroccans usually take it as a compliment. Buy your own rather than borrowing a costume, wear it the way locals do, and keep ceremonial caftans for actual celebrations. A djellaba, a gandoura or babouches are appropriate anywhere.

    Why is the Moroccan caftan on the UNESCO list?

    UNESCO inscribed “Moroccan Caftan: art, traditions and skills” on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity on 10 December 2025, during the committee’s twentieth session in New Delhi. Moreover, the listing recognises the whole chain of skills: weaving, tailoring, braid making, button knotting and embroidery.

    How much does a real Moroccan caftan cost?

    Simple machine-finished caftans start around 1,500 MAD. Mid-range pieces with partial handwork sit between 3,000 and 8,000 MAD. Bridal takchitas made entirely by hand in Fez routinely pass 20,000 MAD, and top designer pieces go far beyond that.

    What are Moroccan slippers called?

    They are babouches in French and English, and belgha in Moroccan Arabic. The embroidered women’s ceremonial version is a cherbil. Plain yellow leather remains the classic men’s choice, especially for Friday prayer.

    What fabric is used for traditional Moroccan clothing?

    Wool and felted mlifa dominate winter garments. Cotton and linen handle summer. Ceremonial pieces use brocade, velvet, silk and sabra, which is usually viscose rather than genuine cactus fibre despite the marketing.

    What do Moroccan women wear in daily life?

    It varies enormously by city, age and personal choice. Many women wear jeans, dresses and trainers. Many others wear a fitted djellaba, and some cover their hair. All of these are normal, and none of them signals anything about a stranger’s views.

    What should I wear as a visitor in Morocco?

    Cover your shoulders and knees in medinas, villages and religious sites, and dress as you like in beach resorts and new towns. In addition, light layers plus one warm item work almost everywhere. Our packing guide for Morocco goes month by month.

    Where is the best place to buy Moroccan clothes?

    Fez is the best city for caftans, embroidery and leather. Meanwhile, Marrakech offers the widest choice and the liveliest bargaining. Tiznit leads for silver, and Middle Atlas cooperatives lead for wool. Buying at a cooperative costs a little more and pays the maker far better.

    Is traditional Moroccan clothing religious?

    It is cultural first, and it reflects Islamic modesty norms alongside Amazigh, Andalusian and Saharan traditions. In fact, Moroccan Jews wore closely related garments for centuries, with their own embroidery styles, which shows how regional the wardrobe really is.

    Final thoughts

    Traditional Moroccan clothing works because it solves real problems elegantly. It handles heat, cold, sand and formality with the same small set of shapes, and it has absorbed influences for over a thousand years without losing its own character.

    Learn the six core words before you arrive: djellaba, caftan, takchita, jabador, selham and babouche. With those, you can talk to any shopkeeper in the country as a curious visitor rather than a target.

    Then go and watch it being made. A tailor’s needle in a Fez workshop explains more in ten minutes than any article can, and the memory outlasts the souvenir. Explore our tours from Marrakech, our tours from Casablanca or the Morocco travel blog to keep planning.
